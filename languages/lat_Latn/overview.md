# Latin (lat_Latn) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Latin (Latina, Lingua Latina)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** lat
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** la
- **Script:** Latin (Latn)
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Italic > Latino-Faliscan
- **Typological Classification:** Fusional, synthetic
- **Native Speakers:** None (classical language); estimated several thousand fluent second-language users worldwide
- **Status:** Extinct as a native language; actively used in specialized domains

## Historical Background

Latin originated as the language of Latium, the region surrounding Rome in central Italy. As the Roman Republic and later the Roman Empire expanded, Latin spread throughout the Mediterranean world, Western Europe, and parts of North Africa and the Near East. Classical Latin, the literary standard refined by authors such as Cicero, Virgil, Caesar, and Ovid during the late Republic and early Empire (roughly 1st century BCE to 1st century CE), remains the primary form studied and used today.

Following the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476 CE, spoken Latin gradually diverged into the various Romance languages — Italian, French, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian, and others. However, written Latin persisted as the lingua franca of educated Europe throughout the medieval period, the Renaissance, and well into the early modern era. Scientific, philosophical, legal, and theological works were routinely composed in Latin until the 17th and 18th centuries, and in some fields even later.

## Current Usage

Latin retains official status in Vatican City, where it serves as the formal language of the Roman Catholic Church. Papal encyclicals, canon law, and liturgical texts are published in Latin. The Pontifical Academy for Latin (Pontificia Academia Latinitatis) actively promotes the language's continued use.

In academia, Latin remains a requirement or strong recommendation in many programs of classical studies, medieval history, theology, philosophy, and comparative linguistics. Universities worldwide offer Latin instruction, and organizations such as the Circulus Latinus and the Septentrionale Americanum Latinitatis Vivae Institutum (SALVI) promote conversational Latin.

Latin's influence on modern languages is immense. English derives approximately 60% of its vocabulary from Latin, either directly or through French. Scientific nomenclature in biology (Linnaean taxonomy), medicine, chemistry, and law continues to rely heavily on Latin terminology. Legal phrases such as *habeas corpus*, *pro bono*, and *amicus curiae* remain standard in judicial systems worldwide.

## Geographic and Demographic Information

While Latin has no geographic community of native speakers, its use is concentrated in several institutional contexts:

- **Vatican City:** Official administrative and liturgical language
- **Roman Catholic Church worldwide:** Liturgical and canonical use
- **Universities and schools:** Academic instruction globally, particularly in Europe and the Americas
- **International organizations:** Latin mottos and terminology appear in institutions worldwide

## Writing System

Latin is written in the Latin alphabet, which it originally developed and which subsequently became the most widely used writing system in the world. The classical Latin alphabet contained 23 letters (lacking J, U, and W, which were later additions). Modern editions of Latin texts typically use the full 26-letter alphabet, with V/U and I/J distinguished.

Classical Latin did not employ diacritical marks. However, modern pedagogical texts frequently use macrons (long marks) over vowels to indicate vowel length, which is phonemically distinctive in Latin. The apex, a mark resembling an acute accent, was occasionally used in inscriptions to mark long vowels.

## Significance for Language Identification

Latin presents particular challenges for automated language identification due to its close genetic relationship with the Romance languages. Many Latin words and phrases have been borrowed directly into Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, French, and English, creating potential for misidentification. However, Latin's distinctive morphological patterns — particularly its case endings, verb-final tendencies, and absence of definite and indefinite articles — provide reliable diagnostic features for accurate identification.
