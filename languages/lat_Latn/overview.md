# Latin (lat)

## Summary

Latin (endonym: **lingua Latīna**) is a classical Indo-European language of the Italic branch, ancestor of the Romance languages (French, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, Romanian, Catalan, and others). It has no native speakers today but remains in active use as the official language of Vatican City, in Roman Catholic liturgy, in scientific nomenclature (biology, medicine, law), in academic mottos, and in ongoing scholarly publication. ISO codes: **639-1 `la`**, **639-2/639-3 `lat`**. Script: Latin (ISO 15924 `Latn`).

## Writing system

Latin is written in the Roman alphabet — the script to which it lent its name. Classical Latin orthography uses 23 letters (A–Z minus J, U, W; I and V served both vowel and consonant roles). Modern editorial conventions often distinguish **I/J** and **U/V**, yielding 25 letters, and sometimes silent K, Y, Z for Greek loans. **No modern diacritics are standard**: there are no accents, cedillas, umlauts, tildes, or circumflexes in everyday written Latin. Pedagogical and scholarly editions may use macrons (ā ē ī ō ū) to mark vowel length and the breve (ă ĕ) to mark short vowels, but these are metadata, not part of running text.

## History and status

Written Latin is typically distinguished as: **Classical Latin** (the literary standard of the late Republic and early Empire, c. 100 BCE–200 CE, e.g. Cicero, Virgil, Caesar), **Ecclesiastical / Late Latin** (the Christian Church from c. 300 CE onward), **Medieval Latin** (c. 700–1400, the lingua franca of learning and administration in Western Christendom), **Renaissance / Neo-Latin** (c. 1400–1800, the language of humanists and science). For LID purposes all these varieties share the same orthography and the same characteristic grammatical endings.
