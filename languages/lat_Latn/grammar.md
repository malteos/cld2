# Latin (lat_Latn) - Grammar

## Word Order

Latin is traditionally classified as an SOV (Subject-Object-Verb) language, with the finite verb typically appearing at the end of a clause. However, Latin word order is highly flexible due to its rich inflectional morphology. Case endings on nouns and agreement markers on verbs allow speakers and writers to rearrange constituents for emphasis, stylistic effect, or information structure without ambiguity. In practice, Classical Latin prose exhibits considerable variation: Cicero frequently places verbs at clause boundaries, while Caesar's military prose tends toward more regular SOV patterns. Poetry exploits word order freedom extensively for metrical and aesthetic purposes through a device known as hyperbaton, the separation of syntactically related words.

Despite this flexibility, certain tendencies are consistent: adjectives often follow their nouns (though attributive adjectives of size, quantity, and demonstratives typically precede), genitives tend to follow the nouns they modify, and subordinate clauses generally precede main clauses. Prepositions always precede their objects.

## Morphological Type

Latin is a highly inflectional (fusional) language. A single morpheme on a noun or verb simultaneously encodes multiple grammatical categories. For example, the noun ending *-orum* simultaneously marks genitive case, plural number, and second declension membership. Similarly, a verb form like *amavissent* encodes third person, plural number, pluperfect tense, subjunctive mood, and active voice within a single word. This fusional character is a hallmark of the language and distinguishes it sharply from analytic languages like English or Chinese.

## Nominal Morphology

### Declensions

Latin nouns are organized into five declension classes, each characterized by its stem vowel and distinctive set of case endings:

- **First Declension (a-stems):** Predominantly feminine nouns (e.g., *puella* "girl," *terra* "earth"). Some masculine nouns also belong here (e.g., *nauta* "sailor," *poeta* "poet").
- **Second Declension (o-stems):** Predominantly masculine nouns ending in *-us* (e.g., *dominus* "master," *amicus* "friend") and neuter nouns ending in *-um* (e.g., *bellum* "war," *templum* "temple").
- **Third Declension (consonant and i-stems):** The largest and most varied declension, encompassing all three genders (e.g., *rex* "king," *lex* "law," *corpus* "body," *mare* "sea"). This declension includes both consonant-stem and i-stem subtypes with partially overlapping paradigms.
- **Fourth Declension (u-stems):** Mostly masculine nouns ending in *-us* (e.g., *exercitus* "army," *senatus* "senate") and a few neuter nouns ending in *-u* (e.g., *cornu* "horn," *genu* "knee").
- **Fifth Declension (e-stems):** A small class of predominantly feminine nouns (e.g., *res* "thing," *dies* "day," *spes* "hope").

### Cases

Latin employs six grammatical cases, each serving specific syntactic and semantic functions:

1. **Nominative:** Marks the subject of a finite verb and predicate nouns. (*Puella cantat.* "The girl sings.")
2. **Genitive:** Expresses possession, partitive relationships, and various other adnominal functions. (*Liber puellae.* "The book of the girl.")
3. **Dative:** Marks the indirect object and the beneficiary or experiencer. (*Puellae librum do.* "I give a book to the girl.")
4. **Accusative:** Marks the direct object of transitive verbs, the object of many prepositions, and extent of time or space. (*Puellam video.* "I see the girl.")
5. **Ablative:** The most versatile case, expressing instrument, means, manner, accompaniment, time when, place from which, cause, and agent (with passive verbs). (*Cum puella ambulo.* "I walk with the girl.")
6. **Vocative:** Used for direct address. Identical to the nominative in most declensions except second declension masculine *-us* nouns, where it ends in *-e* (e.g., *Marce!* "Marcus!").

An archaic seventh case, the **locative**, survives in a few fixed forms indicating place where, primarily with names of cities and small islands (e.g., *Romae* "at Rome," *domi* "at home").

### Gender

Latin has three grammatical genders: masculine, feminine, and neuter. Gender assignment is partly semantic (male beings are typically masculine, female beings feminine) but largely arbitrary for inanimate nouns. The gender of a noun determines the agreement forms of associated adjectives, pronouns, and participles.

### Adjectives

Latin adjectives agree with their nouns in gender, number, and case. They fall into two main classes: first/second declension adjectives (e.g., *bonus, bona, bonum* "good") and third declension adjectives (e.g., *fortis, forte* "brave"). Adjectives have three degrees of comparison: positive, comparative (formed with *-ior/-ius*), and superlative (formed with *-issimus/-a/-um*).

## Verbal Morphology

### Conjugations

Latin verbs are organized into four conjugation classes based on their stem vowel:

- **First Conjugation (a-stems):** e.g., *amare* "to love"
- **Second Conjugation (e-stems):** e.g., *monere* "to warn"
- **Third Conjugation (consonant and i-stems):** e.g., *ducere* "to lead," *capere* "to seize"
- **Fourth Conjugation (i-stems):** e.g., *audire* "to hear"

A number of important verbs are irregular, including *esse* "to be," *posse* "to be able," *ire* "to go," *ferre* "to carry," *velle* "to wish," and *nolle* "to be unwilling."

### Tenses

Latin has six tenses organized into two systems:

**Infectum (incomplete action):**
- **Present:** Action occurring now (*amo* "I love")
- **Imperfect:** Ongoing or repeated past action (*amabam* "I was loving / I used to love")
- **Future:** Action yet to occur (*amabo* "I will love")

**Perfectum (completed action):**
- **Perfect:** Completed past action (*amavi* "I loved / I have loved")
- **Pluperfect:** Action completed before another past action (*amaveram* "I had loved")
- **Future Perfect:** Action to be completed before a future point (*amavero* "I will have loved")

### Moods

Latin verbs are conjugated in three moods:

1. **Indicative:** Expresses statements of fact or reality.
2. **Subjunctive:** Expresses wishes, possibilities, purposes, conditions contrary to fact, and various subordinate clause relationships. The subjunctive is far more common and functionally varied in Latin than in modern Romance languages.
3. **Imperative:** Expresses commands and requests.

### Voices

Latin distinguishes two voices:
- **Active:** The subject performs the action (*Marcus puellam videt.* "Marcus sees the girl.")
- **Passive:** The subject receives the action (*Puella a Marco videtur.* "The girl is seen by Marcus.")

Passive forms in the infectum tenses use synthetic endings (*-r, -ris, -tur, -mur, -mini, -ntur*), while perfectum passive forms are analytic, combining the perfect passive participle with forms of *esse*.

### Non-Finite Forms

Latin has a rich system of non-finite verbal forms:

- **Infinitives:** Present, perfect, and future, in both active and passive voices (six total).
- **Participles:** Present active (*amans* "loving"), perfect passive (*amatus* "having been loved"), future active (*amaturus* "about to love"), and the gerundive or future passive participle (*amandus* "needing to be loved").
- **Gerund:** A verbal noun declined in the oblique cases (*amandi, amando, amandum, amando* "of loving, for loving," etc.).
- **Supine:** A verbal noun in two case forms (accusative *amatum* "to love" expressing purpose, ablative *amatu* "in loving").

## Syntax

Latin syntax relies heavily on subordination through subjunctive constructions, participial phrases, and the ablative absolute. Indirect statement uses the accusative-infinitive construction rather than subordinate clauses with a conjunction (unlike most modern European languages). Relative clauses, purpose clauses (*ut* + subjunctive), result clauses (*ut* + subjunctive), temporal clauses (*cum* + subjunctive), and conditional sentences follow well-defined structural patterns. The sequence of tenses governs the relationship between main clause and subordinate clause verb tenses in complex sentences.
