# Swahili (Kiswahili) — Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Swahili (autonym: Kiswahili)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** swh
- **ISO 639-1 code:** sw
- **Script:** Latin (Latn)
- **Language family:** Niger-Congo > Atlantic-Congo > Volta-Congo > Benue-Congo > Bantoid > Southern Bantoid > Bantu > Northeast Coast Bantu (Sabaki subgroup, zone G.42)
- **Speakers:** Approximately 16-20 million L1 speakers; over 100 million L2 speakers, with some estimates placing the total number of speakers (L1+L2) at 150-200 million
- **Primary regions:** Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Rwanda, Burundi, Mozambique, Somalia, Comoros, and parts of Malawi, Zambia, and Oman

## Geographic Distribution

Swahili is the most widely spoken language of the Bantu family and serves as the primary lingua franca of East Africa. It holds official or national language status in Tanzania (where it is the de facto national language and the medium of primary education), Kenya (co-official with English), Uganda (national language since 2005), the Democratic Republic of the Congo (one of four national languages), and Rwanda. It is also recognized by the African Union as one of its official working languages, a status it gained in 2004, and the East African Community uses Swahili as a lingua franca.

## Historical Development

The origins of Swahili trace back to the Bantu-speaking communities along the East African coast, roughly around the first millennium CE. The language developed in the context of extensive Indian Ocean trade networks linking East Africa with the Arabian Peninsula, Persia, India, and Southeast Asia. This sustained contact is reflected in the substantial Arabic lexical influence on Swahili — estimates suggest that Arabic-origin words constitute roughly 20-30% of the traditional vocabulary, though the proportion in everyday speech is lower.

The earliest known Swahili texts, written in a modified Arabic script known as Ajami, date to the 17th and 18th centuries and include poetry and religious manuscripts. The Latin script was introduced during the European colonial period in the 19th century and gradually replaced Arabic script for most written purposes. By the mid-20th century, Latin orthography had become standard.

## Standardization

Modern Standard Swahili is based primarily on the Kiunguja dialect spoken in Zanzibar (Unguja island). Standardization efforts were formalized through the Inter-Territorial Language Committee (established in 1930 under British colonial administration), which produced dictionaries, grammars, and guidelines for the standard written language. After independence, Tanzania's National Swahili Council (Baraza la Kiswahili la Taifa, BAKITA, founded 1967) and later the Institute of Kiswahili Research (TUKI) at the University of Dar es Salaam became the principal bodies responsible for corpus planning, terminology development, and standardization. Kenya established the Chama cha Kiswahili cha Taifa (CHAKITA) for similar purposes.

## Sociolinguistic Status

Swahili occupies a unique position among African languages as one of the few that functions as a major cross-ethnic and cross-national lingua franca with a large and growing speaker base. In Tanzania, it is the medium of instruction in primary schools and increasingly used in government and media. The language's prestige and utility continue to grow, with Swahili courses offered at universities worldwide and the language gaining recognition in international diplomacy and digital communication.

## Dialectal Variation

Major dialect groups include Kiunguja (Zanzibar, the basis of the standard), Kimvita (Mombasa), Kiamu (Lamu), Kipemba (Pemba island), Kimrima (coastal Tanzania mainland), and Kingwana (spoken in the eastern DRC, sometimes considered a separate but closely related language). Urban varieties in cities like Nairobi and Dar es Salaam show significant innovation, slang development, and code-mixing with English.
