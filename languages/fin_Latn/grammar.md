# Finnish Grammar (suomen kielioppi)

## Word Order

Finnish has a basic SVO (Subject-Verb-Object) word order, but word order is remarkably free due to the extensive case marking system. Cases unambiguously mark grammatical roles, so word order can be rearranged for emphasis, topic-comment structure, and information flow without ambiguity. For example:

- **Koira puri miestä.** (The dog bit the man.) - neutral SVO
- **Miestä puri koira.** (The man was bitten by the dog / It was the dog that bit the man.) - OVS, emphasizing the agent
- **Puri koira miestä.** (The dog did bite the man.) - VSO, emphasis on the verb

The first position in the sentence typically carries the topic (what the sentence is about), while new or emphasized information tends to appear later. In questions, the interrogative word typically comes first. In existential sentences, the word order is often inverted (e.g., *Pöydällä on kirja* "On the table is a book").

## Agglutinative Morphology

Finnish is a strongly agglutinative language, meaning that grammatical relationships are expressed by adding suffixes (and occasionally prefixes) to word stems. A single Finnish word can carry information that would require an entire phrase in English. For example:

- **talossanikin** = talo + ssa + ni + kin = "in my house too" (house + inessive + my + also)
- **juoksennellessammekin** = juoksennella + ssa + mme + kin = "even while we were running around"

Suffixes are added in a specific order: stem + plural marker + case ending + possessive suffix + clitic particles.

## Phonology

### Vowels

Finnish has eight vowel phonemes: **a, e, i, o, u, y, ä, ö**. The vowels y, ä, and ö are front vowels written with distinct letters (y represents /y/, ä represents /æ/, ö represents /ø/). Unlike Estonian, Finnish lacks the vowel **õ** (/ɤ/), and unlike German, Finnish does not use **ü** (using **y** instead for the same sound /y/).

### Vowel Length

Vowel length is phonemically distinctive in Finnish. Every vowel can occur short or long, and the difference changes meaning:

- **tuli** (fire) vs. **tuuli** (wind) vs. **tulli** (customs)
- **tapa** (way/manner) vs. **tapaa** (meets)

Long vowels are written as double letters: aa, ee, ii, oo, uu, yy, ää, öö.

### Vowel Harmony

Finnish exhibits strict vowel harmony. Vowels are divided into three groups:

- **Back vowels:** a, o, u
- **Front vowels:** ä, ö, y
- **Neutral vowels:** e, i (can occur with either group)

Within a native Finnish word, back and front vowels cannot co-occur. Suffixes have front and back variants: *talossa* (in the house) but *metsässä* (in the forest). Compound words and recent loanwords may violate vowel harmony, with each component maintaining its own harmony domain.

### Consonant Gradation

Consonant gradation is a systematic morphophonological alternation affecting the stops p, t, and k. When a syllable that was originally open becomes closed (by adding a suffix), the consonant "weakens":

- **Strong → Weak:** pp → p, tt → t, kk → k, p → v, t → d, k → (zero/v/j)
- Examples: *kirkko* (church, nom.) → *kirkossa* (in church, but stem becomes *kirko-*); *kauppa* (shop) → *kaupan* (shop's, gen.)

### Gemination

Consonant and vowel gemination (doubling) is extremely common in Finnish and is phonemically distinctive:

- **kuka** (who) vs. **kukka** (flower)
- **mato** (worm) vs. **matto** (carpet)

## Noun System

### Cases

Finnish has 15 grammatical cases, one of the most extensive case systems among European languages:

**Grammatical cases:**
1. **Nominative** (-): basic form, subject (*talo* - house)
2. **Genitive** (-n): possession, object of some verbs (*talon* - of the house)
3. **Partitive** (-a/-ä, -ta/-tä): partial object, indefinite quantity (*taloa*)
4. **Accusative** (-n or -t): total object, mainly with pronouns (*minut* - me)

**Internal local cases (inside):**
5. **Inessive** (-ssa/-ssä): in, inside (*talossa* - in the house)
6. **Elative** (-sta/-stä): out of, from inside (*talosta* - from the house)
7. **Illative** (-Vn, -hVn, -seen): into (*taloon* - into the house)

**External local cases (on/at surface):**
8. **Adessive** (-lla/-llä): on, at, by means of (*talolla* - at the house)
9. **Ablative** (-lta/-ltä): from (a surface), from (*talolta* - from the house)
10. **Allative** (-lle): onto, to (*talolle* - to the house)

**Other cases:**
11. **Essive** (-na/-nä): as, in the capacity of (*talona* - as a house)
12. **Translative** (-ksi): becoming, into (a state) (*taloksi* - into a house / to become a house)
13. **Abessive** (-tta/-ttä): without (*talotta* - without a house)
14. **Comitative** (-ne-): together with (plural only, *taloineen* - with its houses)
15. **Instructive** (-n, plural -in): by means of, manner (*jaloin* - on foot)

### No Grammatical Gender

Finnish has no grammatical gender whatsoever. The third-person pronoun *hän* refers to both "he" and "she." This is a feature shared with other Uralic languages and has attracted attention in discussions of gender-neutral language.

### No Articles

Finnish has no definite or indefinite articles. Definiteness is expressed through word order, context, and case selection (the partitive often implies indefiniteness, while the accusative/nominative can imply definiteness):

- *Koira on pihalla.* (The dog is in the yard.) - definite by context
- *Pihalla on koira.* (There is a dog in the yard.) - indefinite by word order

### Number

Finnish distinguishes singular and plural. The plural marker is -t in the nominative and -i- (with various stem changes) in oblique cases:

- *talo* (house) → *talot* (houses)
- *talossa* (in a house) → *taloissa* (in houses)

## Verb System

### Tenses

Finnish has four tenses, two simple and two periphrastic (compound):

1. **Present** (*puhun* - I speak)
2. **Past/Imperfect** (*puhuin* - I spoke)
3. **Perfect** (*olen puhunut* - I have spoken) - formed with auxiliary *olla* + past participle
4. **Pluperfect** (*olin puhunut* - I had spoken) - formed with past tense of *olla* + past participle

There is no morphological future tense; future meaning is expressed by present tense with context or temporal adverbs (*Lähden huomenna* - I leave/will leave tomorrow).

### Moods

Finnish has four moods:

1. **Indicative** - statements of fact (*puhun* - I speak)
2. **Conditional** (-isi-): hypothetical situations (*puhuisin* - I would speak)
3. **Imperative** - commands (*puhu!* - speak! *puhukaa!* - speak! [plural/formal])
4. **Potential** (-ne-): probability, possibility (*puhunen* - I probably speak / I may speak). Rare in modern spoken language but used in written Finnish.

### Voice

Finnish distinguishes active and passive voices. The Finnish passive is more accurately described as an impersonal form - it indicates an unspecified human agent:

- **Active:** *Syön omenan.* (I eat an apple.)
- **Passive:** *Omenaa syödään.* (An apple is eaten / One eats an apple / People eat apples.)

In colloquial Finnish, the passive form is commonly used as a first-person plural imperative: *Mennään!* (Let's go!).

### Negative Verb

Finnish has a unique negative verb *ei*, which conjugates for person while the main verb appears in an uninflected connegative form:

- *en puhu* (I don't speak) - *en* is 1st person singular negative
- *et puhu* (you don't speak)
- *ei puhu* (he/she doesn't speak)
- *emme puhu* (we don't speak)
- *ette puhu* (you [pl.] don't speak)
- *eivät puhu* (they don't speak)

### Infinitives and Participles

Finnish has five infinitive forms (I-V), each with different syntactic functions:

- **I infinitive** (-a/-ä): basic dictionary form (*puhua* - to speak)
- **II infinitive** (-e-): temporal and manner constructions (*puhuessa* - while speaking)
- **III infinitive** (-ma-): various adverbial uses (*puhumassa* - speaking [inessive], *puhumaan* - to speak [illative])
- **IV infinitive** (-minen): verbal noun (*puhuminen* - speaking [as noun])
- **V infinitive** (-maisilla-): "about to" (*puhumaisillaan* - about to speak)

Finnish also has present and past active and passive participles, which are used extensively in written Finnish to create complex sentence-equivalent constructions that replace relative clauses and subordinate clauses.

## Syntax

### Postpositions

Finnish predominantly uses postpositions rather than prepositions. The noun typically appears in the genitive before the postposition:

- *talon edessä* (in front of the house) - genitive + postposition
- *kadun yli* (across the street)
- *sen jälkeen* (after that)

Some words function as prepositions (governing the partitive), and a few can serve as both:

- *ennen sotaa* (before the war) - preposition + partitive
- *ilman rahaa* (without money) - preposition + partitive

### Possessive Suffixes

Finnish can express possession through possessive suffixes attached to the noun, in addition to or instead of personal pronouns:

- *taloni* (my house), *talosi* (your house), *talonsa* (his/her/their house)
- *talomme* (our house), *talonne* (your [pl.] house)

In spoken Finnish, possessive suffixes are increasingly replaced by genitive pronouns (*mun talo* instead of *taloni*).

### Sentence-Equivalent Participle Constructions

Written Finnish makes extensive use of participial constructions that condense entire subordinate clauses into compact noun phrases. These are characteristic of formal and literary Finnish:

- *Hänen lukemansa kirja* (The book that he/she read) - using agent participle
- *Kaupungissa asuessaan hän oppi suomea.* (While living in the city, he/she learned Finnish.) - using temporal construction

These constructions are one of the most challenging aspects of Finnish for learners and are much less common in spoken language.
