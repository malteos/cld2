# Finnish (suomi) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Finnish (suomi)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** fin
- **ISO 15924 script code:** Latn (Latin)
- **Language family:** Uralic > Finnic
- **Native speakers:** approximately 5.8 million
- **Writing system:** Latin alphabet (extended with ä, ö)

## Geographic Distribution

Finnish is spoken primarily in the Republic of Finland (Suomen tasavalta), where it serves as the mother tongue of approximately 87% of the population. It is also spoken in parts of Swedish Finland (the bilingual coastal regions), and by significant diaspora communities in Sweden (around 450,000 speakers), the United States (particularly in Michigan, Minnesota, and Wisconsin, areas settled during the 19th and early 20th century emigration waves), and Canada (primarily in Ontario and British Columbia). Smaller Finnish-speaking communities exist in Norway, Russia (particularly in the Republic of Karelia and the Leningrad Oblast), Estonia, and Australia.

## Official Status

Finnish holds official language status in Finland alongside Swedish, as enshrined in the Finnish Constitution. It is also one of the 24 official languages of the European Union. Finnish has minority language recognition in Sweden, where it is classified as one of the five official minority languages. In the municipality of Gällivare in northern Sweden, Finnish has co-official status. Finnish is also used in certain administrative contexts in parts of the Republic of Karelia in Russia, though its official status there has varied over time.

## Historical Development

### Proto-Finnic Origins

Finnish descends from Proto-Finnic, the common ancestor of the Finnic branch of the Uralic language family. Proto-Finnic speakers are believed to have migrated to the Baltic region around 1500-1000 BCE. The separation of Finnish from Estonian and other Finnic languages occurred gradually over centuries, with geographic separation and contact with different neighboring languages (Germanic for Finnish, Baltic and Slavic for Estonian) driving divergence.

### Early Written History

The history of written Finnish begins with Mikael Agricola (c. 1510-1557), the Bishop of Turku, who is considered the father of literary Finnish. In 1543, he published the first book in Finnish, the *Abckiria* (ABC book), a primer for reading. He went on to translate the New Testament into Finnish (*Se Wsi Testamentti*, 1548) and parts of the Old Testament. Agricola's work established many conventions of Finnish orthography, though his spelling was inconsistent by modern standards and heavily influenced by Swedish, German, and Latin conventions.

### Standardization

The standardization of Finnish was a long process spanning several centuries. During the Swedish rule of Finland (until 1809), Swedish dominated administration, education, and literature, and Finnish was largely a spoken language of the common people. After Finland became a Grand Duchy of the Russian Empire in 1809, a movement for Finnish national identity and language rights emerged. Key figures include Elias Lönnrot, who compiled the *Kalevala* (1835, expanded 1849), the Finnish national epic, from oral folk poetry, which demonstrated the expressive power of Finnish and inspired national pride.

The language reform movement of the 19th century, driven by figures such as the Fennoman movement's leaders, worked to develop Finnish into a language suitable for all domains of society. In 1863, a language decree by Tsar Alexander II gave Finnish equal legal status with Swedish, to be achieved within 20 years. This spurred rapid development of Finnish-language education, administration, and literature.

### Modern Finnish

Modern standard Finnish (yleiskieli) was largely established by the early 20th century. The Institute for the Languages of Finland (Kotimaisten kielten keskus) serves as the authority on Finnish language norms and usage. Contemporary Finnish continues to evolve, with significant influence from English in technology, business, and popular culture. The spoken colloquial language (puhekieli) differs markedly from the written standard, with extensive phonological reductions, different pronoun forms, and simplified morphology.

### Linguistic Influences

Finnish has borrowed extensively from its contact languages throughout its history. The oldest loanword layer comes from Proto-Germanic and Proto-Baltic languages (e.g., *kuningas* "king" from Proto-Germanic, *hammas* "tooth" from Proto-Baltic). Swedish has been the most significant source of loanwords due to centuries of political union, contributing words in administration, culture, and everyday life. Russian influence entered primarily during the Grand Duchy period (1809-1917). In the modern era, English has become the dominant source of new loanwords, particularly in technology and popular culture.
