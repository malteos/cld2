# Malay (msa_Latn) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Malay (Bahasa Melayu)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** msa (macrolanguage); zlm (individual: Malay, Standard Malay)
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** ms
- **Script:** Latin (Latn), historically also Jawi (Arabic script)
- **Language Family:** Austronesian > Malayo-Polynesian > Malayo-Sumbawan > Malayic > Malayan > Local Malay
- **Speakers:** Approximately 290 million total speakers (including L2), approximately 77 million native speakers
- **Morphological Type:** Agglutinative

## Regions and Official Status

Malay is the national and official language of **Malaysia** (as Bahasa Malaysia), **Brunei** (as Bahasa Melayu), and **Singapore** (as one of four official languages). It also holds a recognized status in parts of **Indonesia** (where the closely related standardized variety is known as Bahasa Indonesia), southern **Thailand** (Pattani region), and the southern **Philippines** (among Malay-speaking communities in Mindanao and Sulu). Malay is a working language of **ASEAN** and serves as a lingua franca across much of maritime Southeast Asia.

In Malaysia, Malay is the sole official language as stipulated in Article 152 of the Federal Constitution. It is the medium of instruction in national schools (Sekolah Kebangsaan) and is required for all government communications. In Brunei, Standard Malay is the official language, used in government administration, education, and media alongside English. In Singapore, Malay holds a symbolic role as the national language (Bahasa Kebangsaan) and is used in the national anthem ("Majulah Singapura"), military commands, and within the Malay-Muslim community.

## Brief History

The Malay language has a long and well-documented history stretching back over a millennium. The earliest known inscriptions in Old Malay date to the 7th century CE, found in Sumatra (the Srivijaya kingdom), including the Kedukan Bukit inscription (683 CE) written in a Pallava-derived script. These inscriptions attest to the early role of Malay as a language of trade, diplomacy, and administration across the Malay Archipelago.

During the era of the **Malacca Sultanate** (15th century), Classical Malay became the dominant lingua franca of maritime Southeast Asia, facilitated by Malacca's position as a major trading port. This period saw extensive borrowing from **Sanskrit**, **Arabic**, **Persian**, **Tamil**, and **Chinese** languages. The adoption of Islam in the region led to the widespread use of the **Jawi** script (Arabic-based) for writing Malay, which remained the primary script until the 20th century.

European colonial powers, particularly the **Portuguese** (from 1511), **Dutch**, and **British**, brought further linguistic influence. The British colonial period (18th-20th centuries) was decisive in shaping modern Malaysian Malay, introducing the **Rumi** (Latin) script through missionary and educational activities. The Rumi script was progressively standardized and eventually became the official script, though Jawi retains legal co-official status in Malaysia and Brunei.

Following Malaysian independence (Merdeka) in 1957, the Malay language was elevated as a key instrument of national unity. The **Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka** (Institute of Language and Literature), established in 1956, became the authoritative body for standardizing Malay in Malaysia. In 1972, Malaysia and Indonesia agreed on a shared spelling reform (Ejaan Rumi Baharu / Ejaan Yang Disempurnakan), which harmonized certain orthographic conventions between Malaysian Malay and Indonesian, though significant lexical and stylistic differences persist to this day.

Modern Standard Malay (as used in Malaysia) continues to evolve through contact with **English**, which exerts considerable influence in education, business, and technology. The language maintains its agglutinative character, rich affixation system, and relatively flexible syntax while incorporating loanwords and calques from global sources.

## Classification Notes for Language Identification

Malay and Indonesian are often considered varieties of the same language and share a very high degree of mutual intelligibility. For language identification purposes, the key challenge is distinguishing Malaysian Malay (msa_Latn) from Indonesian (ind_Latn). Distinguishing features include spelling differences, vocabulary choices (particularly for modern and technical terms), function word preferences, and patterns of loanword adoption.
