# Bengali (ben_Beng)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name**: Bengali (বাংলা, Bangla)
- **ISO 639-3**: ben
- **ISO 639-1**: bn
- **Script**: Bengali script (Eastern Nagari, বাংলা লিপি)
- **Language Family**: Indo-European > Indo-Iranian > Indo-Aryan > Eastern Indo-Aryan
- **Native Speakers**: ~230 million+ (approximately 7th most spoken language worldwide)
- **Total Speakers**: ~300 million+ (including L2 speakers)

## Geographic Distribution

Bengali is primarily spoken in two major regions:

- **Bangladesh**: Bengali (Bangla) is the sole national and official language of Bangladesh, where it is the mother tongue of the vast majority of the population (~170 million speakers). The Language Movement of 1952, in which students died protesting for the recognition of Bengali, is commemorated internationally as International Mother Language Day (February 21).

- **India**: Bengali is the official language of the state of **West Bengal** (~90 million speakers) and co-official in **Tripura**. It is also widely spoken in **Assam** (particularly in the Barak Valley), **Jharkhand**, and the **Andaman and Nicobar Islands**. Bengali is one of the 22 scheduled languages of India recognized by the Indian Constitution.

Significant Bengali-speaking diaspora communities exist in the United Kingdom, the United States, the Middle East (particularly the Gulf states), Malaysia, Singapore, Italy, and Canada.

## Official Status

- National language of the People's Republic of Bangladesh
- Official language of the Indian states of West Bengal and Tripura
- Additional official language in the Barak Valley region of Assam
- One of the 22 scheduled languages of India
- One of the six official languages of the United Nations (proposed but not yet adopted)

## Historical Development

Bengali evolved from **Magadhi Prakrit** and **Apabhramsha** during the period between the 10th and 12th centuries CE. The earliest known Bengali text is the **Charyapada** (চর্যাপদ), a collection of Buddhist mystic songs dating to approximately the 10th-12th centuries.

The language's development can be broadly categorized into three periods:

1. **Old Bengali (900-1400 CE)**: Characterized by the Charyapada literature, early Vaishnava texts, and the initial differentiation from other Eastern Indo-Aryan languages like Odia and Assamese.

2. **Middle Bengali (1400-1800 CE)**: This period saw the flourishing of Bengali literature, including the works of Chandidas, the Mangalkavya tradition, translations of the Ramayana and Mahabharata, and the literary contributions of the Vaishnava movement centered around Chaitanya Mahaprabhu.

3. **Modern Bengali (1800 CE-present)**: The Bengali Renaissance of the 19th century transformed the language and its literature. Key figures include **Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar**, who reformed Bengali prose, and **Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay**, who pioneered the Bengali novel. The crowning achievement was the work of **Rabindranath Tagore** (রবীন্দ্রনাথ ঠাকুর), who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913 for his collection *Gitanjali*, making him the first non-European Nobel laureate in literature.

## Literary and Cultural Significance

Bengali has one of the richest literary traditions in South Asia. The language has produced two national anthems: Bangladesh's *Amar Shonar Bangla* and India's *Jana Gana Mana*, both written by Rabindranath Tagore. The tradition of Bengali cinema, theater, music (particularly Rabindra Sangeet and Nazrul Geeti), and intellectual discourse has had a profound influence on South Asian culture.

The Bengali script is also used to write several other languages, including Assamese (with minor modifications), Meitei (historically), and Sylheti (sometimes). Bengali has a substantial body of digital content and is well-represented in computational linguistics resources, making it a high-resource language for NLP tasks.

## Dialectal Variation

Major dialect groups include Rarhi (the basis for Standard Bengali), Bangali, Varendri, Manbhumi, and various eastern dialects spoken in Bangladesh including Sylheti, Chittagonian, and Rangpuri, some of which are sometimes classified as separate languages.
