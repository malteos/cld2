# Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Indonesian (Bahasa Indonesia)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: ind
- **ISO 639-1 code**: id
- **Script**: Latin (Latn)
- **Language family**: Austronesian > Malayo-Polynesian > Malayo-Sumbawan > Malayic > Malay
- **Native speakers**: ~43 million
- **L2 speakers**: 150-200 million
- **Total speakers**: ~200+ million
- **Country**: Indonesia (official national language)
- **Status**: Official language, used in government, education, media, and commerce

## Overview

Indonesian, known natively as Bahasa Indonesia, is the official and national language of Indonesia, the world's fourth most populous country. While only approximately 43 million people speak it as a first language, it serves as the lingua franca for over 270 million inhabitants of the Indonesian archipelago, making it one of the most widely spoken languages in the world when L2 speakers are included.

Indonesian belongs to the Austronesian language family, specifically the Malayic branch. It is based on Riau Malay (Malay as spoken in the Riau province of Sumatra), which had long served as a trade language (lingua franca) across the archipelago. The language was formally adopted as the national language following the Youth Pledge (Sumpah Pemuda) of 1928, when Indonesian nationalists declared "one nation, one people, one language" as a unifying principle against Dutch colonial rule. After independence in 1945, Indonesian was enshrined in the constitution as the state language.

## Relationship to Malay

Indonesian and Malay (Bahasa Melayu, ISO 639-3: zsm) are closely related and mutually intelligible to a high degree. Both descend from Classical Malay and share the same grammatical core. However, they have diverged in vocabulary, spelling conventions, and register over the past century. Indonesian has absorbed significantly more loanwords from Dutch (due to 350 years of Dutch colonization), Javanese, and Arabic, while Malaysian Malay has incorporated more English loanwords. The two countries attempted to harmonize spelling through the 1972 Ejaan Yang Disempurnakan (Perfected Spelling System) in Indonesia and the corresponding reform in Malaysia, but vocabulary differences persist.

## Standardization and Registers

The language is regulated by the Badan Pengembangan dan Pembinaan Bahasa (Agency for Language Development and Cultivation), which publishes the authoritative dictionary (Kamus Besar Bahasa Indonesia, KBBI). Standard Indonesian exhibits significant register variation: formal written Indonesian uses elaborate affixed verb forms and complex syntax, while colloquial spoken Indonesian (often called bahasa sehari-hari or bahasa gaul) drops many affixes, uses informal pronouns, and incorporates Jakartan slang and regional vocabulary. This diglossia is an important consideration for language identification, as informal Indonesian text may look quite different from formal prose.

## Sociolinguistic Context

Most Indonesians are bilingual or multilingual, speaking a regional language (such as Javanese, Sundanese, Minangkabau, or Balinese) alongside Indonesian. There are over 700 living languages in Indonesia, and Indonesian serves as the essential bridge language for inter-ethnic communication. The language continues to evolve rapidly, absorbing English technical terminology and internet slang while maintaining its Malay grammatical core.
