# Tosk Albanian (als_Latn) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Tosk Albanian (Toskërisht)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** als
- **Language Family:** Indo-European, Albanian branch
- **Script:** Latin
- **Approximate Speakers:** 5 million
- **Typological Classification:** Fusional, SVO word order

## Geographic Distribution

Tosk Albanian is spoken primarily in the southern half of Albania, below the Shkumbin River, which traditionally marks the dialectal boundary between Tosk (south) and Gheg (north). Beyond southern Albania, Tosk Albanian speakers are found in significant communities across several countries:

- **Albania:** Southern and central regions, including the capital Tirana (which has historically been a Tosk-influenced area in terms of standard language policy)
- **Greece:** The Cham Albanian (Chameria) communities in northwestern Greece and the Arvanite communities scattered across central and southern Greece
- **Italy:** The Arbëreshë communities in southern Italy (Calabria, Sicily, Basilicata, Molise, Puglia), descendants of medieval Albanian emigrants
- **North Macedonia:** Albanian-speaking communities in the western part of the country
- **Diaspora:** Significant communities in Turkey, the United States, Canada, Australia, Germany, Switzerland, and other Western European countries

## Official Status

Albanian (based substantially on the Tosk dialect) is the official language of Albania and one of the official languages of Kosovo and North Macedonia. Standard Albanian (Shqipja Standarde), codified in the 1972 Congress of Orthography held in Tirana, drew heavily from Tosk Albanian phonological and morphological norms, though it incorporates elements from both major dialect groups. The language is also recognized as a minority language in several neighboring countries including Montenegro, Serbia, and Italy.

## Historical Background

Albanian is one of the oldest living Indo-European languages and occupies a unique position within the family as the sole surviving member of its own distinct branch. It has no close relatives among the Indo-European languages, making it an isolate within the family -- comparable in this respect to Greek and Armenian, which also form independent branches. The precise position of Albanian within Indo-European has been debated for centuries, with proposed connections to Illyrian, Thracian, and Dacian, though none of these hypotheses has been conclusively proven.

The earliest written attestation of Albanian dates to the 15th century. The "Formula e Pagëzimit" (Baptismal Formula) of 1462 is the oldest known Albanian sentence, and the "Meshari" (Missal) of Gjon Buzuku, published in 1555, is the earliest substantial text. Despite the late emergence of written records, linguistic evidence -- including a deep stratum of inherited Indo-European vocabulary and archaic grammatical features -- suggests that Albanian has been spoken continuously since antiquity.

Tosk Albanian has been particularly influential in the formation of the modern standard language. During the communist period in Albania (1944-1991), the regime promoted a unified standard based predominantly on Tosk, reflecting the southern origins of much of the political leadership. The 1972 orthographic congress formalized this standard, and it remains the basis of official Albanian today, used in education, government, media, and literature throughout Albania and Kosovo.

## Linguistic Significance

Albanian is of considerable interest to historical linguists because of its archaic features and its role in understanding the Indo-European language family. It preserves certain ancient phonological and morphological patterns that have been lost in other branches. At the same time, Albanian has been heavily influenced by contact with Latin, Greek, Slavic, Turkish, and Italian over the centuries, resulting in a complex layered vocabulary that reflects the region's turbulent history.

The Albanian lexicon contains substantial borrowings from Latin (dating from the Roman period), Greek (both ancient and modern), South Slavic languages (Serbian, Macedonian, Bulgarian), Ottoman Turkish, and Italian. Despite this extensive borrowing, the core grammatical structure and basic vocabulary remain distinctly Albanian and Indo-European in origin.
