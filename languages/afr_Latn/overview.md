# Afrikaans (afr_Latn)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Afrikaans
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** afr
- **Script:** Latin (Latn)
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Germanic > West Germanic
- **Native Speakers:** Approximately 7.2 million
- **Total Speakers:** Approximately 16 million (including L2 speakers)
- **Primary Regions:** South Africa, Namibia

## Official Status

Afrikaans is one of the eleven official languages of South Africa, as enshrined in the post-apartheid Constitution of 1996. In Namibia, Afrikaans functions as a recognized national language and is widely used as a lingua franca, particularly in urban areas and in commerce, despite English being the sole official language. Historically, Afrikaans also held co-official status in South West Africa (now Namibia) during the period of South African administration.

## Geographic Distribution

The majority of Afrikaans speakers reside in South Africa, where the language is concentrated in the Western Cape, Northern Cape, and parts of the Eastern Cape, Free State, Gauteng, and North West provinces. In Namibia, Afrikaans is spoken throughout the country but is especially prevalent in Windhoek and the southern regions. Smaller diaspora communities of Afrikaans speakers can be found in Australia, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, Canada, and the United States.

## History and Development

Afrikaans evolved from the Dutch vernacular spoken by colonial settlers who arrived at the Cape of Good Hope beginning in 1652 under the auspices of the Dutch East India Company (VOC). Over the following centuries, the language diverged significantly from its European Dutch parent through contact with indigenous Khoisan languages, Malay (brought by slaves from Southeast Asia), Portuguese creoles, and various Bantu languages. This contact situation accelerated the simplification of Dutch grammatical structures, particularly the loss of grammatical gender, case inflections, and complex verb conjugation patterns.

The emergence of Afrikaans as a distinct language rather than a Dutch dialect is generally dated to the mid-to-late 18th century, although the exact point of divergence is debated among linguists. The first Afrikaans language movement (Eerste Afrikaanse Taalbeweging) began in 1875 with the founding of the Genootskap van Regte Afrikaners (Society of True Afrikaners) in Paarl, which published the first Afrikaans newspaper, Die Afrikaanse Patriot, and campaigned for recognition of Afrikaans as a written language distinct from Dutch.

A second language movement (Tweede Afrikaanse Taalbeweging) in the early 20th century pushed for the standardization and official recognition of Afrikaans. In 1925, Afrikaans replaced Dutch as an official language of the Union of South Africa alongside English. The first complete Afrikaans Bible translation appeared in 1933, a milestone that significantly bolstered the language's prestige and standardization.

During the apartheid era (1948-1994), Afrikaans became associated with the ruling National Party and the apartheid government, which led to significant political controversy. The Soweto uprising of 1976 was in part a protest against the compulsory use of Afrikaans as a medium of instruction in Black schools. This historical association continues to influence attitudes toward the language in post-apartheid South Africa.

## Sociolinguistic Context

Despite its political history, Afrikaans is spoken by a diverse population. The majority of native Afrikaans speakers are in fact Coloured (mixed-race) South Africans, followed by white Afrikaners. The language also has a substantial number of Black South African speakers, particularly in the Western Cape and Northern Cape. In contemporary South Africa, efforts are underway to reposition Afrikaans as an inclusive language that belongs to all its speakers, regardless of racial or ethnic background. Afrikaans maintains a vibrant literary tradition, a significant media presence, and active academic and cultural institutions.
