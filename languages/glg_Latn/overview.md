# Galician (Galego)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Galician (Galego)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: glg
- **ISO 639-1 code**: gl
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Italic > Romance > Western Romance > West Iberian > Galician-Portuguese
- **Script**: Latin
- **Speakers**: Approximately 2.4 million (with varying degrees of fluency and daily use)
- **Primary region**: Galicia (Comunidade Autonoma de Galicia), northwestern Spain
- **Other regions**: Galician-speaking diaspora communities in Latin America (especially Argentina, Uruguay, Venezuela, Cuba), Western Europe, and small border areas of Asturias, Leon, and Zamora (known as "falas galegas" or Galician-speaking enclaves)
- **Official status**: Co-official language with Spanish (Castilian) in the Autonomous Community of Galicia, recognized by the Spanish Constitution and the Statute of Autonomy of Galicia (1981)

## Historical Background

Galician shares its origins with Portuguese in the medieval language known as Galician-Portuguese (galego-portugues), which was spoken across the territory of the former Kingdom of Galicia and the County of Portugal from roughly the 9th to the 14th century. This common ancestor was one of the great literary languages of medieval Iberia, celebrated especially for its lyric poetry tradition, the cantigas. The Cantigas de Santa Maria, compiled under King Alfonso X of Castile, and the secular cantigas de amigo and cantigas de amor composed by troubadours, represent one of the richest bodies of medieval European poetry.

The political separation between Portugal and Galicia in the 12th century, when the County of Portugal achieved independence, set the two languages on divergent paths. While Portuguese evolved as the language of an independent state, gaining prestige through its use in administration, literature, and eventually global exploration and colonization, Galician remained subordinate to Castilian within the Kingdom of Spain. From the 14th and 15th centuries onward, Galician entered a period of decline known as the Seculos Escuros (Dark Centuries), during which it was largely excluded from written and formal use. Castilian became the language of administration, the Church, and the educated classes, while Galician persisted primarily as a spoken language among the rural population.

## Revival and Normalization

The 19th century saw the beginning of a cultural and linguistic revival known as the Rexurdimento (Resurgence), led by literary figures such as Rosalia de Castro, Eduardo Pondal, and Manuel Curros Enriquez. Rosalia de Castro's "Cantares gallegos" (1863) is considered a landmark in the recovery of Galician as a literary language.

The 20th century brought further progress, interrupted by the repression of regional languages under the Franco dictatorship (1939-1975). After the transition to democracy, Galician gained co-official status in Galicia in 1981. The Lei de Normalizacion Linguistica (Linguistic Normalization Law) of 1983 established a framework for the promotion and protection of the language in education, media, and public administration.

Today, the Real Academia Galega (Royal Galician Academy) and the Instituto da Lingua Galega serve as the primary normative and research institutions for the language. Despite progress, Galician faces challenges including declining intergenerational transmission, competition with Spanish in urban areas, and ongoing debates about the relationship between Galician and Portuguese that manifest in different orthographic proposals (reintegracionismo versus the official norm based on Spanish orthographic conventions).

## Sociolinguistic Situation

Current sociolinguistic surveys indicate that while a majority of the Galician population understands Galician, active daily use has been declining, particularly among younger urban speakers. The language remains strongest in rural areas and among older generations. Language policy, education in Galician, and the presence of Galician in media (including the public broadcaster Television de Galicia and Radio Galega) continue to be central issues in Galician society and politics.
