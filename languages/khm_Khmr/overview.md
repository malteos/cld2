# Khmer (ភាសាខ្មែរ)

## General Information

Khmer, known natively as ភាសាខ្មែរ (phiasa Khmae), is the language of the Khmer people and the official language of the Kingdom of Cambodia. It is classified under ISO 639-3 with the code **khm** and under ISO 639-1 as **km**. Khmer is the most widely spoken member of the Austroasiatic language family, which also includes Vietnamese and Mon. Within Austroasiatic, Khmer belongs to the Mon-Khmer branch, specifically the Khmeric group that also encompasses several closely related minority languages spoken in parts of mainland Southeast Asia.

## Speaker Population and Geographic Distribution

Khmer has approximately 16 million native speakers, making it the second most spoken Austroasiatic language after Vietnamese. The vast majority of speakers reside in Cambodia, where Khmer serves as the sole official language and is used in government, education, media, and daily communication throughout the country.

Significant Khmer-speaking communities also exist outside Cambodia. In Vietnam, an estimated one million ethnic Khmer speakers live in the Mekong Delta region, particularly in the provinces of Tra Vinh, Soc Trang, and An Giang, where the population is sometimes referred to as Khmer Krom. In Thailand, Khmer-speaking communities are found in the northeastern provinces bordering Cambodia, including Surin, Buriram, and Sisaket, where a dialect known as Northern Khmer or Khmer Surin is spoken by several hundred thousand people. Diaspora communities in France, the United States, Australia, and Canada further extend the global reach of the language.

## Writing System

Khmer is written using the Khmer script (អក្សរខ្មែរ), an abugida derived from the Brahmi script through the intermediary of the Pallava script of southern India. In an abugida system, each consonant character carries an inherent vowel that can be modified or suppressed using diacritical marks and dependent vowel signs. The Khmer script consists of 33 consonant characters divided into two series (the first series and the second series), each associated with different inherent vowel values. There are also a substantial number of dependent vowel signs, independent vowels, and additional diacritics used to represent the full range of Khmer phonology.

The Khmer script is written from left to right. Unlike Thai and Lao, which are historically related scripts, Khmer uses spacing between clauses and sentences rather than between individual words. The script is also used to write Pali and Sanskrit in religious and scholarly contexts. The Unicode block for Khmer occupies the range U+1780 to U+17FF, with an additional block for Khmer symbols at U+19E0 to U+19FF.

## Historical Background

The history of the Khmer language is conventionally divided into several periods. Old Khmer, attested from the earliest inscriptions, represents the language as it was used during the pre-Angkorian and Angkorian periods. The oldest known Khmer inscription dates to the 7th century CE, found at Angkor Borei in southern Cambodia, making Khmer one of the earliest recorded languages in Southeast Asia. These early inscriptions are predominantly found on stone stelae and document administrative, religious, and royal matters.

During the Angkor period (roughly the 9th to 15th centuries), the Khmer Empire was one of the most powerful states in Southeast Asia, and Old Khmer was the language of an extensive literary and bureaucratic tradition. Hundreds of inscriptions from this era survive at temples throughout Cambodia, including the monumental complexes at Angkor Wat and Angkor Thom. Sanskrit was used alongside Old Khmer in many inscriptions, reflecting the strong influence of Indian culture and Hinduism on the Khmer court.

Middle Khmer covers the period from the decline of Angkor in the 15th century through approximately the 18th century, during which the language underwent significant phonological and morphological changes, including the loss of the register system that had distinguished the two consonant series. Modern Khmer, the form of the language spoken today, emerged from these transformations and is characterized by a relatively isolating morphology, a complex vowel system, and significant influence from Pali, Sanskrit, Thai, and more recently French and English.

## Official Status and Modern Use

Khmer is enshrined as the official language of Cambodia in the national constitution. It serves as the medium of instruction at all levels of education and is the primary language of government, judiciary, and mass media within the country. The Royal Academy of Cambodia (Ratchabandittayasaphea) is responsible for standardizing Khmer grammar, orthography, and vocabulary. Efforts to promote literacy and preserve the language continue to be a focus of national policy, particularly in rural areas where access to education has historically been limited.
