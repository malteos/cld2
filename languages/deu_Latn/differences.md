# Distinguishing German from Closely Related Languages

## German vs. Dutch (Most Confusable)

Dutch and German share West Germanic roots and substantial lexical overlap, making them the most confusable pair. Key differences for identification:

- **Digraphs ij/ei**: Dutch uses the digraph "ij" where German uses "ei". Dutch "vrij" corresponds to German "frei", Dutch "wijn" to German "Wein". The presence of "ij" strongly indicates Dutch.
- **Doubled vowels**: Dutch regularly doubles vowels to mark long sounds in closed syllables: "naam" (name), "boom" (tree), "maan" (moon). German does not systematically double vowels this way, instead relying on vowel length rules and occasional lengthening-h ("Bahn", "Sohn").
- **Articles**: Dutch has two grammatical genders reflected in articles: "de" (common) and "het" (neuter). German has three genders with the articles "der" (masculine), "die" (feminine), and "das" (neuter), plus full case declension ("dem", "den", "des").
- **Verb placement and morphology**: While both languages use verb-second order in main clauses, Dutch infinitives end in "-en" or "-n" (e.g., "lopen"), whereas German infinitives also end in "-en" or "-n" but with different stems ("laufen"). Dutch past participles frequently use "ge-" with a "-t" or "-d" ending ("gelopen"), while German favors "-t" or "-en" ("gelaufen").
- **Word-final devoicing spelling**: Dutch writes voiced consonants even when devoiced ("hond", "bed"), while German spelling more often reflects pronunciation ("Hund", "Bett").

## German vs. Luxembourgish

Luxembourgish (Lëtzebuergesch) descends from Moselle Franconian dialects closely related to German. Distinguishing features:

- Luxembourgish uses "ë" (e with diaeresis) extensively, a character absent from standard German orthography.
- The digraph "éi" in Luxembourgish replaces German "ei" in many words.
- Luxembourgish lacks the eszett (ß) entirely, using "ss" instead.
- French and Portuguese loanwords appear far more frequently in Luxembourgish due to the country's multilingual environment ("Merci", "Pardon" used natively).
- Luxembourgish plurals and verb conjugations diverge noticeably: "Kanner" (children) vs. German "Kinder".

## German vs. Yiddish

Yiddish evolved from medieval High German but incorporated substantial Hebrew, Aramaic, and Slavic vocabulary. When written in Latin script (YIVO romanization):

- Yiddish contains Hebrew-origin words absent from German: "mishpokhe" (family), "sholem" (peace), "kholem" (dream).
- Slavic loanwords like "kashe" (porridge) or "nebech" (unfortunately) do not occur in German.
- Yiddish romanization uses spellings like "kh" for the voiceless velar fricative, "sh" for the postalveolar fricative, and "ey" where German writes "ei".
- Yiddish lacks the German case system almost entirely, having simplified to a nominative-accusative distinction with minimal dative marking.

## German vs. Afrikaans

Afrikaans developed from Dutch colonial dialects and simplified considerably:

- Afrikaans has almost no inflection: one article "die" for all nouns, no case marking, and drastically simplified verb conjugation ("ek is", "jy is", "hy is").
- Double negation is standard in Afrikaans: "Ek kan nie swem nie" (I cannot swim). German uses single negation.
- Afrikaans lacks umlauts (ä, ö, ü) and the eszett (ß), both of which are diagnostic for German.
- Afrikaans vocabulary shows English and Malay/Khoi influence absent in German: "baie" (very/many, from Malay), "piesang" (banana).

## German vs. Danish

Danish belongs to the North Germanic (Scandinavian) branch, making it structurally more distant:

- Danish uses "en" (common) and "et" (neuter) as indefinite articles, appended as suffixed definite articles ("huset" = the house). German never suffixes articles.
- Danish lacks grammatical case entirely (no dative, genitive marking on articles).
- The Danish letters "æ" and "ø" replace German "ä" and "ö" but are distinct characters with different orthographic conventions.
- Danish vocabulary diverges substantially despite some shared roots: "at spise" (to eat) vs. German "essen".

## German vs. Swedish

Swedish, another North Germanic language, shares the suffixed definite article system with Danish:

- Swedish uses "en" and "ett" as indefinite articles and suffixes "-en"/"-et" for definite forms ("bilen" = the car). German uses free-standing articles.
- Swedish has "å" as an additional vowel letter not found in German.
- Swedish lacks the German case system; genitive is marked only with "-s" appended to nouns.
- Swedish compound nouns exist but use linking morphemes ("-s-", "-e-") differently from German.

## Diagnostic Features That Uniquely Identify German

Several features, taken together, reliably identify a text as German rather than any of the above languages:

1. **The eszett (ß)**: No other language in active use employs this character. Its presence is virtually diagnostic of German. It appears after long vowels and diphthongs: "Straße", "Fuß", "heißen".

2. **Umlaut usage (ä, ö, ü)**: While other languages use diacritics, the specific combination of all three umlauts with German morphological patterns (plural formation: "Haus/Häuser", "Buch/Bücher"; subjunctive: "wäre", "könnte") is unique.

3. **Massive compound nouns**: German forms compounds without spaces or hyphens to a degree unmatched by related languages: "Geschwindigkeitsbegrenzung" (speed limit), "Krankenversicherungskarte" (health insurance card). Dutch also compounds but less aggressively, and Afrikaans tends toward shorter formations.

4. **Case system reflected in articles**: The four-case system (nominative, accusative, dative, genitive) produces article variation unseen in neighboring languages: "der Mann" (nom.), "den Mann" (acc.), "dem Mann" (dat.), "des Mannes" (gen.). No other language in this comparison set retains this level of article inflection.

5. **Capitalization of all nouns**: German capitalizes every noun, not just proper nouns. This convention is shared only with Luxembourgish among the languages discussed and is immediately visible in running text.

6. **Function words**: Certain high-frequency words are distinctly German: "und" (and), "nicht" (not), "aber" (but), "auch" (also), "schon" (already). These differ from their Dutch ("en", "niet", "maar", "ook", "al"), Danish ("og", "ikke", "men", "også", "allerede"), and Swedish ("och", "inte", "men", "också", "redan") equivalents.
