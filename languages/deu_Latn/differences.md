# German vs confusable languages

Within the CommonLID Germanic set, German is relatively easy to separate from English, Dutch, Afrikaans and Frisian thanks to `ß`, noun capitalisation, and V2/verb-final syntax. The most realistic confusions are with Dutch (historically the closest continental neighbour) and with Luxembourgish or Low German varieties, which are out of this set.

## deu ↔ nld (Dutch)

- `ß` appears in German only; Dutch never uses it.
- Dutch has `ij` digraph and long doubled vowels `aa/ee/oo/uu`; German uses `ie`/`ei` and single or umlauted vowels.
- Initial `sch-` exists in both, but German adds `sp-`/`st-` clusters that Dutch lacks at /ʃ/ realisation (Dutch writes *sp-, st-* with /s/).
- Dutch clusters `zw-` (*zwart*) and `tw-` (*twee*) correspond to German `schw-`/`zw-` (*schwarz*, *zwei*); Dutch pronouns *het, de, een, niet, ook, zijn* vs German *das, der/die, ein, nicht, auch, sein*.
- Subordinate clauses in German are strictly verb-final; Dutch allows more variation in clause-final verb cluster order.

## deu ↔ eng (English)

- German capitalises every noun; English capitalises only proper nouns.
- Presence of `ß` or `ä/ö/ü` rules out English.
- German has no Saxon genitive `'s` attached to random noun phrases, and no *do*-support.

## deu ↔ afr / fry

- Afrikaans has no case system, no `ß`, no `ä/ö/ü`; diagnostic negation *nie … nie*.
- Frisian uses circumflex vowels `â, ê, û`; lacks `ß` and common-noun capitalisation.
