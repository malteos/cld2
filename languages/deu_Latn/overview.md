# German (Deutsch)

## General Information

German, known natively as Deutsch, is a West Germanic language belonging to the Indo-European language family. Its ISO 639-3 code is **deu**. German is written using the Latin script, supplemented by the unique letter Eszett (sharp s) and three umlauted vowels. With approximately 130 million native speakers, German ranks among the most widely spoken languages in Europe and the world.

## Geographic Distribution and Official Status

German holds official language status in several European countries. It is the sole official language of **Germany** and **Austria**, and one of the official languages of **Switzerland**, **Liechtenstein**, **Luxembourg**, and the autonomous province of **South Tyrol** (Alto Adige) in northern Italy. In Switzerland, German is spoken by roughly two-thirds of the population alongside French, Italian, and Romansh. Luxembourg recognizes German alongside Luxembourgish and French as its three official languages. Beyond these core regions, German-speaking communities exist in parts of Belgium, Poland, Romania, Namibia, Brazil, and other countries with historical ties to German emigration or colonial influence.

Within the European Union, German is the most widely spoken native language and serves as one of the working languages of several EU institutions. It also holds significance as a language of science, philosophy, literature, and theology.

## Language Family

German belongs to the West Germanic branch of the Germanic subfamily within Indo-European. Its closest major relatives include Dutch, Afrikaans, English, Frisian, and Luxembourgish. The High German consonant shift, which occurred roughly between the 3rd and 9th centuries, is the principal phonological development that distinguishes High German dialects from Low German and other West Germanic languages.

## Historical Development

The history of the German language is traditionally divided into three major periods.

**Old High German (circa 750--1050)** represents the earliest documented stage. Texts from this era include the Hildebrandslied, Merseburg Incantations, and translations of religious works. Old High German was not a unified language but rather a collection of related dialects spoken across the southern and central parts of the Germanic-speaking territory. The completion of the Second Sound Shift during this period is a defining characteristic.

**Middle High German (circa 1050--1350)** saw the flourishing of courtly literature, including the works of Walther von der Vogelweide, Wolfram von Eschenbach, and Hartmann von Aue. The Nibelungenlied, one of the most celebrated works of medieval German literature, also dates from this era. While still lacking a single written standard, Middle High German exhibited greater dialectal convergence in literary language compared to its predecessor.

**Early New High German (circa 1350--1650)** marks the transition toward a more unified written form. Martin Luther's translation of the Bible into German in the early 16th century played a pivotal role in shaping a common literary language accessible across regional boundaries. The invention of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg further accelerated the spread of a standardized written German.

## Standardization

Modern Standard German, or Hochdeutsch, emerged gradually through centuries of literary, administrative, and educational practice. The publication of Konrad Duden's orthographic dictionary in 1880 provided a widely accepted reference for spelling conventions. In 1901, the Second Orthographic Conference in Berlin established a unified set of spelling rules for the entire German-speaking world. The most recent major reform, the German orthography reform of 1996, introduced simplified spelling rules and was officially adopted across Germany, Austria, and Switzerland, though it remains a subject of ongoing discussion. Today, Hochdeutsch serves as the standard variety used in education, media, government, and formal communication, while a rich landscape of regional dialects continues to thrive in everyday spoken use.
