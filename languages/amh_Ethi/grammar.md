# Amharic (amh_Ethi) - Grammar

## Typological Profile

Amharic is a fusional language with significant agglutinative features, characteristic of the Semitic language family. It employs the Semitic root-and-pattern morphological system, where triconsonantal (and sometimes quadriconsonantal) roots carry core lexical meaning, and vowel patterns, prefixes, suffixes, and infixes modify grammatical categories. The basic word order is Subject-Object-Verb (SOV), which is typologically unusual for Semitic languages (most of which are VSO or SVO) and is attributed to Cushitic substrate influence from prolonged contact with Oromo, Agaw, and other Cushitic languages of Ethiopia.

## Phonology

### Consonants

Amharic has a rich consonant inventory that includes a distinctive set of ejective (glottalized) consonants, a hallmark feature shared with other Ethiopian languages (both Semitic and Cushitic). The ejective consonants are:

- **p'** (ጵ) - ejective bilabial stop
- **t'** (ጥ) - ejective alveolar stop
- **ch'** (ጭ) - ejective postalveolar affricate
- **ts'** (ጽ) - ejective alveolar affricate
- **k'** (ቅ) - ejective velar stop

Gemination (consonant length) is phonemic in Amharic and plays a crucial role in distinguishing meaning. For example: አለ (ale, "he said") vs. አለ (allE, "he exists/there is"), where the geminated /l/ changes the meaning entirely. Nearly every consonant in Amharic can be geminated. Gemination is not marked in the standard Ethiopic orthography, which presents challenges for learners and computational processing.

### Vowels

Amharic has a seven-vowel system: /a/, /u/, /i/, /e/, /o/, /ə/ (schwa, the first order vowel), and /a/ (open). The schwa vowel (the default "first order" in the Ethiopic script) is extremely frequent and serves as a default or epenthetic vowel in many contexts.

### Stress and Intonation

Word stress in Amharic is generally not phonemic and tends to fall on a penultimate or final syllable, though the patterns are complex and interact with morphology. Sentence intonation plays an important role in distinguishing declarative, interrogative, and exclamatory sentences.

## Noun System

### Gender

Amharic has a two-gender system: masculine and feminine. Gender assignment is partly semantic (natural gender for animate nouns) and partly arbitrary for inanimate nouns. Grammatical gender affects verb agreement, adjective agreement, pronoun selection, and the form of the definite article.

### Number

Plurality is marked in several ways:
- Regular suffix **-oCH** (ኦች): መምህር (mamhir, "teacher") > መምህሮች (mamhiroCH, "teachers")
- Broken (internal) plurals inherited from Semitic: መጽሐፍ (mats'haf, "book") > መጻሕፍት (mats'ahift, "books")
- Some nouns use both strategies depending on register

### Definiteness

The definite article in Amharic is a suffix attached to the noun:
- Masculine: **-u** / **-w** (e.g., ልጅ "child" > ልጁ "the child [masc.]")
- Feminine: **-wa** / **-itu** (e.g., ልጅ "child" > ልጅዋ "the child [fem.]")

There is no indefinite article, though the numeral አንድ (and, "one") can function as an indefinite marker in some contexts.

### Case

Amharic marks grammatical relations through a combination of word order, postpositions, and case-like particles:
- The accusative is marked with the suffix **-n** on definite direct objects: መጽሐፉን (mats'hafun, "the book [ACC]")
- Genitive/possessive relationships use the prefix **ye-** (የ): የኢትዮጵያ (ye-Ityop'iya, "of Ethiopia")
- Other grammatical relations are expressed through postpositions: በ (be, "in/by"), ለ (le, "for/to"), ከ (ke, "from"), ወደ (wede, "toward")

## Verb System

The Amharic verb system is arguably the most complex component of the grammar and constitutes the core of Semitic morphological complexity in the language.

### Root-and-Pattern System

Verbs are built on consonantal roots (typically triconsonantal) combined with vowel patterns and affixes. For example, from the root s-b-r ("break"):
- ሰበረ (sebere) - "he broke" (perfective)
- ይሰብራል (yisebiral) - "he breaks / will break" (imperfective)
- ስበር (siber) - "break!" (imperative)
- መስበር (mesber) - "to break" (infinitive)

### Derived Stems

Like other Semitic languages, Amharic has a system of derived verbal stems that modify the base meaning:
- **Causative** (a- prefix): ሰበረ (sebere, "he broke") > አሰበረ (asebere, "he caused to break")
- **Passive/Reflexive** (te- prefix): ተሰበረ (tesebere, "it was broken / it broke [intrans.]")
- **Reciprocal** (te-...-a-): ተሳሰቡ (tesasebu, "they broke each other")
- **Frequentative/Intensive** (gemination): ሰባበረ (sebabere, "he smashed repeatedly")

### Subject Agreement

Amharic verbs obligatorily agree with their subject in person, number, and gender. The perfective (past) uses suffixes, while the imperfective (non-past) uses a combination of prefixes and suffixes:

**Perfective (past) of ሰበረ (sebere, "broke"):**
- 1sg: ሰበርኩ (seberku) - "I broke"
- 2sg.m: ሰበርክ (seberк) - "you (m.) broke"
- 2sg.f: ሰበርሽ (sebersh) - "you (f.) broke"
- 3sg.m: ሰበረ (sebere) - "he broke"
- 3sg.f: ሰበረች (seberech) - "she broke"
- 1pl: ሰበርን (sebern) - "we broke"
- 2pl: ሰበራችሁ (seberachihu) - "you (pl.) broke"
- 3pl: ሰበሩ (seberu) - "they broke"

### Object Pronoun Incorporation

A distinctive feature of Amharic is the incorporation of object pronouns directly into the verb form. Both direct and indirect object pronouns can be suffixed to the verb, and sometimes both appear simultaneously:
- ሰበረው (seberew) - "he broke it"
- ሰበረልኝ (seberelign) - "he broke (it) for me"
- ሰበረልኝው (seberelignew) - "he broke it for me"

This incorporation creates very long verb forms that encode subject, object, benefactive, and other grammatical information in a single word.

### Gerundive / Converb Constructions

One of the most distinctive syntactic features of Amharic is its extensive use of converb (gerundive) constructions for clause chaining. In connected discourse, only the final verb in a sentence is fully conjugated; preceding verbs appear in a converb form that agrees with the subject but depends on the final verb for tense/aspect:

- ተነስቶ ልብሱን ለብሶ ወጣ (tenesto libsun lebso weta) - "Having risen and having put on his clothes, he went out"

This clause-chaining strategy is fundamental to Amharic narrative and expository prose and gives the language its characteristic sentence structure with long sequences of dependent converbs leading to a single main verb.

### Auxiliary Verbs and Compound Tenses

Amharic uses several auxiliary constructions for complex tense/aspect distinctions:
- **Progressive:** verb gerund + ነው (new, "is"): እየሰበረ ነው (iyesebere new, "he is breaking")
- **Perfect:** verb gerund + auxiliary: ሰብሮ ነበር (sebro neber, "he had broken")
- **Habitual past:** imperfective + ነበር (neber, "was"): ይሰብር ነበር (yisebir neber, "he used to break")

## Syntax

### Word Order

The canonical word order is SOV, with modifiers generally preceding their heads:
- Adjectives precede nouns: ትልቅ ቤት (tillik bet, "big house")
- Genitive constructions precede nouns: የእኔ ቤት (ye-ene bet, "my house")
- Relative clauses precede nouns: የመጣው ሰው (yemettaw sew, "the man who came")
- Postpositions follow nouns: ቤት ውስጥ (bet wist', "in the house")

### Relative Clauses

Relative clauses in Amharic are formed by using the relative prefix የ- (ye-) attached to the verb and are placed before the head noun. The relative verb agrees with the head noun in gender and number, and the entire construction is one of the more complex aspects of Amharic syntax.

### Negation

Negation is formed by the circumfix **al-...-m** around the verb:
- ይሰብራል (yisebiral, "he breaks") > አይሰብርም (ayisebirim, "he doesn't break")
- ሰበረ (sebere, "he broke") > አልሰበረም (alseberem, "he didn't break")

### Question Formation

Yes/no questions can be formed by intonation alone or by adding question particles. Content questions use question words that typically appear in the position of the constituent being questioned, before the verb.
