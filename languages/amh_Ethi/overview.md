# Amharic (amh_Ethi) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Amharic (amarinja / amharigna)
- **Autonym:** አማርኛ (Amarinja)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** amh
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** am
- **Script:** Ethiopic (Ge'ez / Fidel) - an abugida writing system
- **Language Family:** Afro-Asiatic > Semitic > South Semitic > Ethiopic (Ethiopian Semitic)
- **Speakers:** Approximately 57 million (32 million native speakers, 25 million L2 speakers)
- **Resource Level:** Mid-resource language

## Geographic Distribution

Amharic is the most widely spoken language of Ethiopia and serves as the working language of the Ethiopian federal government. It is predominantly spoken in the Amhara Region and in the capital city, Addis Ababa, but due to its role as a lingua franca, it is used across much of the country. Significant Amharic-speaking diaspora communities exist in the United States (particularly in Washington D.C., which has the largest Ethiopian population outside of Ethiopia), Israel, Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany, Sweden, and various countries in the Middle East. In Israel, the Beta Israel (Ethiopian Jewish) community maintains Amharic as a heritage language alongside Hebrew.

## Official Status

Amharic holds the status of working language of the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia. While the 1994 Ethiopian constitution grants all Ethiopian languages equal state recognition, Amharic serves as the language of federal government administration, the military, and the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church. It is also the medium of instruction in primary schools in many regions, though this has been shifting toward regional languages under Ethiopia's ethnic federalism policy. Amharic is one of the official working languages of the African Union, whose headquarters are in Addis Ababa.

## Historical Background

Amharic is descended from Ge'ez (Ethiopic), the classical language of Ethiopian civilization, which itself belongs to the South Semitic branch of the Afro-Asiatic language family. While Ge'ez ceased to be a spoken vernacular by approximately the 10th century, it continued as the literary and liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church and remains so to this day.

The earliest known Amharic texts date to the 14th century, with the royal songs of Emperor Amda Seyon I (r. 1314-1344) being among the oldest surviving compositions. However, Amharic likely existed as a spoken language for several centuries before these written records. During the medieval period, Amharic gradually replaced Ge'ez as the language of the royal court and administration.

The Amharic literary tradition expanded significantly in the 19th century under Emperor Tewodros II and his successors. The introduction of the printing press in the late 19th century and the establishment of modern schools in the early 20th century further accelerated the development of Amharic as a literary and administrative language. Emperor Haile Selassie's reign (1930-1974) saw the widespread promotion of Amharic as the national language of Ethiopia.

Modern Amharic has been significantly influenced by borrowings from Ge'ez (particularly in religious and formal registers), Italian (from the brief Italian occupation of 1936-1941), English (in technical, scientific, and modern vocabulary), and Arabic (particularly in trade and religious terminology). The language has developed a rich tradition of poetry, prose, drama, and journalism, and Amharic literature constitutes one of the most substantial literary traditions in sub-Saharan Africa.

## Dialects

Amharic is relatively homogeneous compared to many languages of its size, but several dialectal varieties are recognized. The major dialect areas include Gondar (northwestern), Gojjam (western), Wollo (northeastern), Shewa/Shoa (central, including the Addis Ababa variety), and Menz. The Addis Ababa variety, rooted in the Shewa dialect, is generally considered the prestige variety and serves as the basis for Standard Amharic used in media, education, and government. Dialectal differences are primarily in phonology and lexicon rather than grammar, and mutual intelligibility is high across all varieties.
