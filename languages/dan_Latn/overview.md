# Danish (dansk) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Danish (dansk)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: dan
- **ISO 639-1 code**: da
- **Script**: Latin (with special characters: ae, oe, aa)
- **Approximate speakers**: ~6 million
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Germanic > North Germanic (Scandinavian) > East Scandinavian

## Language Family Classification

Danish belongs to the East Scandinavian branch of the North Germanic (also known as Scandinavian or Norse) languages within the broader Germanic family of Indo-European languages. Its closest relatives are Swedish and Norwegian, particularly Norwegian Bokmal, with which it shares an exceptionally high degree of mutual intelligibility in written form. The East Scandinavian grouping (Danish and Swedish) contrasts with the West Scandinavian branch (Norwegian Nynorsk, Icelandic, Faroese), though this historical classification is somewhat misleading in the modern context because Norwegian Bokmal developed heavily under Danish influence during the centuries of the Danish-Norwegian union and is in many respects closer to Danish than to Swedish.

## Geographic Distribution

Danish is spoken primarily in the following regions:

- **Denmark**: The national language of Denmark, spoken by approximately 5.8 million people as a first language across the entire country.
- **Greenland**: Danish serves as a co-official language alongside Kalaallisut (Greenlandic). It is widely used in administration, higher education, and media.
- **Faroe Islands**: Danish holds co-official status with Faroese. It is taught in schools from an early age and is used extensively in government and official communication.
- **Schleswig-Holstein (Germany)**: A recognized minority language in the German state of Schleswig-Holstein, particularly in the region of Southern Schleswig (Sydslesvig), where a Danish-speaking minority of approximately 50,000 people resides. The Danish minority has its own schools, churches, and cultural institutions.
- **Diaspora communities**: Smaller Danish-speaking communities exist in the United States (particularly in Iowa and Wisconsin, legacy of 19th-century immigration), Canada, Argentina, Australia, and other countries.

## Official Status

Danish is the sole official language of the Kingdom of Denmark, which constitutionally comprises Denmark proper, the Faroe Islands, and Greenland. In the Faroe Islands, Danish is co-official with Faroese and is a compulsory subject in schools. In Greenland, Danish is co-official with Kalaallisut. Within the European Union, Danish is one of the 24 official languages. It is also one of the working languages of the Nordic Council.

## Historical Development

Danish evolved from Old Norse, the common ancestor of all North Germanic languages, spoken by the Norse peoples during the Viking Age (roughly 800-1100 CE). The earliest distinctively Danish texts date from the 13th century, though runic inscriptions in a transitional Old Norse-Old Danish form exist from earlier periods. The separation of Danish from Swedish and Norwegian was a gradual process driven by political, geographic, and social factors.

Key periods in the development of Danish include:

- **Old Danish (800-1525)**: Gradual divergence from Old Norse, development of distinctive features such as the weakening of consonants and the emergence of the stoed (glottal constriction).
- **Middle Danish (1100-1525)**: Significant phonological changes, loss of the Old Norse case system, and growing influence from Middle Low German due to Hanseatic trade.
- **Early Modern Danish (1525-1700)**: The Reformation brought the first Danish Bible translation (1550), which helped standardize the language. The period of Danish-Norwegian union (1380-1814) meant that Danish served as the written language of Norway, profoundly influencing the development of Norwegian Bokmal.
- **Modern Danish (1700-present)**: Continued standardization, influence from French and later English, and ongoing phonological changes that have made spoken Danish notably different from its written form.

The extensive Low German influence during the medieval period, particularly through the Hanseatic League, left a deep imprint on Danish vocabulary and even some aspects of grammar, distinguishing it from the more conservative Icelandic and Faroese. This influence also affected Swedish and Norwegian but is particularly pronounced in Danish.

## Writing System

Danish uses the Latin alphabet with 29 letters. The standard Danish alphabet includes all 26 letters of the basic Latin alphabet plus three additional vowel letters at the end: AE (ae), OE (oe), and AA (aa). These three letters are alphabetized after Z in that order. The letter AA replaced the older digraph Aa/aa by an orthographic reform in 1948, though the older spelling is still used in some proper names (such as the city of Aalborg, which officially reverted from Aalborg to Aalborg in 1984).
