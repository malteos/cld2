# Slovak (slk_Latn) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Slovak (slovenčina, slovenský jazyk)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** slk
- **ISO 639-1 code:** sk
- **Script:** Latin (Latn)
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > West Slavic > Czech-Slovak
- **Native speakers:** Approximately 5.2 million
- **Total speakers:** Approximately 7 million (including L2 speakers)

## Geographic Distribution

Slovak is the official language of the Slovak Republic (Slovensko), where it serves as the sole state language. It is spoken natively by the vast majority of Slovakia's population of approximately 5.4 million people.

Significant Slovak-speaking diaspora communities exist in several countries:

- **Czech Republic** - The largest diaspora community, numbering several hundred thousand speakers, facilitated by the historical ties of the former Czechoslovakia and the high degree of mutual intelligibility between Czech and Slovak.
- **United States** - Concentrated primarily in Pennsylvania, Ohio, New Jersey, and other industrial states of the Northeast and Midwest, with immigration dating back to the late 19th and early 20th centuries.
- **Canada** - Communities in Ontario, Alberta, and other provinces.
- **Hungary** - Historical Slovak minority communities in northern Hungary, particularly near the Slovak border.
- **Serbia** - A recognized minority language in the Vojvodina autonomous province, where Slovak-speaking communities (known as "Dolnozemski Slovaci" or Lowland Slovaks) have existed since the 18th century.
- **Other countries** - Smaller communities in the United Kingdom, Ireland, Germany, Austria, Australia, and Argentina.

## Classification and Genetic Relationships

Slovak belongs to the West Slavic branch of the Slavic language family, alongside Czech, Polish, Sorbian (Upper and Lower), and Kashubian. Within West Slavic, Slovak is most closely related to Czech, forming the Czech-Slovak subgroup. The two languages share a high degree of mutual intelligibility, though this has been gradually decreasing among younger generations born after the dissolution of Czechoslovakia in 1993.

Slovak also shares certain features with South Slavic languages, particularly Slovenian and Croatian, which has led some linguists to consider it a transitional language between the West and South Slavic branches. This is reflected in features such as the preservation of certain archaic Slavic forms and phonological patterns.

## History of Codification

The codification of the Slovak literary language is a relatively modern development that unfolded in two major stages:

### Anton Bernolak (1762-1813)

The first systematic codification of Slovak was undertaken by Anton Bernolak, a Catholic priest and linguist. In 1787, he published "Dissertatio philologico-critica de litteris Slavorum" and subsequently "Grammatica Slavica" (1790), which established the first standard Slovak language based on the Western Slovak dialect spoken in the Trnava region. Bernolak's codification was adopted primarily by Catholic intellectuals and writers, but it did not achieve universal acceptance among all Slovak speakers, particularly Protestants who continued to use Biblical Czech.

### Ludovit Stur (1815-1856)

The definitive codification of modern standard Slovak was accomplished by Ludovit Stur, a leading figure of the Slovak national movement. In 1843, together with Jozef Miloslav Hurban and Michal Miloslav Hodza, Stur proposed a new standard based on the Central Slovak dialect, which was considered the most distinctively Slovak and the most widely understood across dialect regions. Stur published his codification in "Nauka reci slovenskej" (1846). This standard was later refined by Martin Hattala in cooperation with Hodza in the 1852 reform, which brought the orthography closer to etymological principles and to Czech spelling conventions. The Stur-based standard, with subsequent modifications, remains the foundation of modern literary Slovak.

## Status and Standardization

Modern standard Slovak is regulated by the Ludovit Stur Institute of Linguistics of the Slovak Academy of Sciences (Jazykovedny ustav Ludovita Stura Slovenskej akademie vied). The language has full institutional support, with its use mandated in government, education, media, and public life by the State Language Act (Zakon o statnom jazyku). Slovak is also one of the 24 official languages of the European Union.
