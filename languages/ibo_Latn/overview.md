# Igbo (Asusu Igbo) - Language Overview

## Classification

- **Language name**: Igbo (Asusu Igbo)
- **ISO 639-3**: ibo
- **ISO 639-1**: ig
- **Family**: Niger-Congo > Atlantic-Congo > Volta-Niger > Igboid
- **Script**: Latin (with diacritics: subdots and tone marks)
- **Speakers**: ~45 million (L1), with significant diaspora communities
- **Region**: Southeastern Nigeria (primarily Anambra, Imo, Abia, Enugu, Ebonyi, parts of Delta, Rivers, and Benue states)

## Status and Use

Igbo is one of the three major languages of Nigeria, alongside Hausa and Yoruba. It holds official status at the regional level and is recognized in the Nigerian constitution as one of the country's principal languages. English remains Nigeria's sole national official language, but Igbo is used in education, media, government, and daily life throughout southeastern Nigeria. The language is taught in primary and secondary schools in the Igbo-speaking states and is offered as a subject in the West African Examinations Council (WAEC) standardized tests.

Despite its large speaker base, Igbo faces sociolinguistic pressure from English, particularly in urban centers like Lagos, Port Harcourt, and Abuja, where younger generations increasingly use English or Nigerian Pidgin as their primary language. UNESCO has flagged concerns about intergenerational transmission, and various cultural organizations, including the Society for Promoting Igbo Language and Culture (SPILC), actively work to promote its continued use.

## Dialect Diversity

Igbo is characterized by remarkable dialectal diversity. Major dialect clusters include Owerre (Owerri), Onitsha, Nnewi, Ngwa, Afikpo, Nsukka, Wawa, Ika, and Ikwerre, among many others. The differences between dialects can be significant, spanning phonology, vocabulary, and tonal patterns. Some peripheral dialects, such as Ekpeye and Ikwerre, are sometimes classified as separate Igboid languages rather than Igbo dialects.

Standardized Igbo (Central Igbo or "Igbo Izugbe") was developed in the mid-20th century as a common literary form, drawing primarily from the central dialects around Owerri and Umuahia. This standardized form is used in education, formal media, and religious contexts. However, many speakers continue to use their local dialects in everyday conversation, and the relationship between standard Igbo and regional varieties remains an active area of sociolinguistic discussion.

## Writing System History

The earliest written records of Igbo date to the early 19th century, when European missionaries, notably the Church Missionary Society (CMS), began developing orthographies for the language. Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a Yoruba-born linguist and bishop, contributed early work on Igbo transcription. The Lepsius Standard Alphabet was initially used, followed by various mission-specific systems.

A major milestone came with the work of the International Institute of African Languages and Cultures in the 1920s-1930s, which produced a unified orthography. The Onwu orthography, officially adopted in 1961, became the standard and introduced the subdotted characters (i, o, u with dots below) that distinguish Igbo's expanded vowel inventory. This system replaced earlier diacritical conventions (such as umlauts or underlines) and remains the basis of modern Igbo writing.

Tone marking in written Igbo has been inconsistent historically. While tone is phonemic and essential for meaning, most published Igbo text omits tone marks, relying on context for disambiguation. Academic and pedagogical texts sometimes mark tones, using acute accent for high tone and grave accent for low tone, with unmarked syllables typically interpreted as carrying a default tone depending on the convention used.

## Digital Presence

Igbo has a growing digital footprint, with a Wikipedia edition, localized interfaces for major platforms, and increasing use in social media. Machine translation support has improved through projects like Masakhane, which focuses on NLP for African languages. However, the language remains under-resourced compared to global languages, and the subdotted characters can pose challenges for text processing in systems with limited Unicode support.
