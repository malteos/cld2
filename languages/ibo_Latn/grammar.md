# Igbo Grammar

## Word Order

Igbo is an SVO (Subject-Verb-Object) language. The basic declarative sentence places the subject before the verb and the object after it:

- **Obi zụrụ akwụkwọ** ("Obi bought a book") - Subject(Obi) + Verb(zụrụ) + Object(akwụkwọ)
- **Ada na-eri nri** ("Ada is eating food") - Subject(Ada) + Auxiliary(na) + Verb(eri) + Object(nri)

Adjectives and demonstratives typically follow the noun they modify, while possessive constructions use the associative marker "nke" or direct juxtaposition.

## Typological Profile

Igbo is primarily an isolating (analytic) language with some agglutinative features. Grammatical relationships are expressed more through word order, particles, and auxiliaries than through bound morphology. However, the verbal system does exhibit agglutinative tendencies, with prefixes and suffixes (verbal extensions) modifying verb stems to express causative, applicative, reciprocal, and other derived meanings.

## Tone System

Igbo is a register tone language with two level tones: high (H) and low (L). Additionally, a phonological process called downstep causes a high tone to be realized at a lower pitch than a preceding high tone. Tone is lexically and grammatically significant:

- **akwa** (H-H) = "cloth"
- **akwa** (H-L) = "cry/weeping"
- **akwa** (L-H) = "egg"
- **akwa** (L-L) = "bed/bridge"

Tone also plays grammatical roles, marking distinctions such as declarative vs. interrogative mood, and differentiating verb tenses. In the verbal system, tone patterns interact with prefixes and suffixes to encode tense, aspect, and polarity. For example, the difference between the simple past and certain other tenses may be signaled purely by tonal patterns on the verb and its affixes.

When tones are marked orthographically, the acute accent (a) represents high tone and the grave accent (a) represents low tone. However, most everyday Igbo text is written without tone marks.

## Vowel Harmony

Igbo exhibits a system of ATR (Advanced Tongue Root) vowel harmony. The eight vowels are divided into two sets:

- **+ATR (expanded)**: i, e, u, o
- **-ATR (retracted)**: i (subdotted), a, u (subdotted), o (subdotted)

Within a word root, all vowels typically belong to the same ATR set. Suffixes and prefixes harmonize with the root vowels. For example:

- **isi** (head) - +ATR vowels
- **isi** (with subdotted i, to cook) - -ATR vowels
- The verbal suffix "-re" (completive) becomes "-ra" or "-ro" (with subdot) depending on the ATR class of the verb root.

The vowel "a" patterns with the -ATR set. This harmony system affects all affixation and is a fundamental structural feature of the language.

## Pronominal System

Igbo has a rich set of personal pronouns distinguished by person and number:

| Person | Singular | Plural |
|--------|----------|--------|
| 1st | m / mu | anyi |
| 2nd | gi / i | unu |
| 3rd | ya / o | ha |

Pronouns also have distinct forms depending on their grammatical function (subject, object, possessive). The third-person singular subject pronoun "o" (high tone) contrasts with the impersonal/indefinite "a" or "e" used in passive-like constructions. Emphatic and reflexive forms exist as well, built with the element "onwe" (self): "onwe m" (myself), "onwe gi" (yourself), etc.

## Noun System

Igbo does not have grammatical gender in the European sense. There are no noun classes marked by prefixes in the way Bantu languages operate. However, there is a semantic distinction between animate and inanimate nouns that affects pronoun reference and certain syntactic constructions. Human nouns are referred to with "onye" (who/person) in relative clauses, while non-human nouns use "nke" (which/that).

Igbo lacks definite and indefinite articles. Definiteness is conveyed through context, demonstratives ("nke a" = this, "nke ahu" = that), or the suffix "-a"/"-ahu" attached to the noun. Plurality can be indicated by the prefix "ndi" before human nouns ("ndi mmadu" = people) or by context and quantifiers for non-human nouns.

## Verb System and Serial Verb Constructions

The Igbo verb system makes extensive use of auxiliaries and verbal prefixes to express tense, aspect, and mood:

- **na-** (progressive/habitual): "O na-agu akwukwo" (He is reading / He reads)
- **ga-** (future): "O ga-abu onye nkuzi" (He will be a teacher)
- **na-e-** / **na-a-** (habitual, with harmonizing vowel): "Ha na-eri nri" (They eat food)

Serial verb constructions (SVCs) are highly productive in Igbo. Multiple verbs can be strung together in a single clause without overt coordination or subordination:

- **O were mma bee ji** ("He took a knife and cut yam") - literally "He took knife cut yam"
- **O gara ahia zuta akwa** ("She went to market and bought cloth") - literally "She went market bought cloth"

These constructions share a single subject and describe closely related or sequential events.

## Negation

Negation in Igbo is expressed through tonal changes on the verb and/or the addition of the negative suffix "-ghi"/"-gho" (harmonizing with ATR):

- **O biara** ("He came") vs. **O biaghi** ("He did not come")
- **O na-abia** ("He is coming") vs. **O anaghi abia** ("He is not coming")

The negative particle "anaghi" is used with progressive/habitual constructions. For imperative negation, "e...la" frames the verb: "Emela!" (Don't do it!).

## Complementation and Subordination

Subordinate clauses are introduced by conjunctions such as "na" (that), "mgbe" (when), "ebe" (where), "maka na" (because), and "ka" (so that/in order to). Relative clauses follow the head noun and are often unmarked or introduced by the relative pronoun "nke."

## Question Formation

Yes/no questions are formed primarily through intonation (rising pitch at the end) without structural change. Content questions use interrogative words such as "gini" (what), "onye" (who), "ebe" (where), "mgbe" (when), "etu" (how), and "maka gini" (why). These interrogative words may appear in situ or at the beginning of the clause.
