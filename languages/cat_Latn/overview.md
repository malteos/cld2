# Catalan (cat_Latn)

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Catalan (Catala)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** cat
- **ISO 639-1 code:** ca
- **Script:** Latin
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Italic > Romance > Western Romance > Occitano-Romance
- **Approximate speakers:** ~10 million (including second-language speakers)
- **Native speakers:** ~4.1 million

## Geographic Distribution

Catalan is spoken across a discontinuous territory historically known as the "Paisos Catalans" (Catalan Countries), spanning multiple states and administrative regions:

- **Catalonia (Catalunya):** The principal region of Catalan speakers, encompassing northeastern Spain with Barcelona as its capital. This is the demographic and cultural heartland of the language, with approximately 6 million speakers including second-language users.
- **Valencia (Comunitat Valenciana):** The variety spoken here is known as Valencian (Valencia), and while it is linguistically considered part of the Catalan language continuum, it has its own normative authority (Acadèmia Valenciana de la Llengua). Approximately 2 million speakers reside in Valencia.
- **Balearic Islands (Illes Balears):** The islands of Mallorca, Menorca, Eivissa (Ibiza), and Formentera each have their own dialectal varieties of Catalan. Around 750,000 people live in the Balearic Islands.
- **Andorra:** Catalan is the sole official language of this small principality nestled in the Pyrenees between Spain and France. Andorra is the only sovereign state where Catalan holds exclusive official status.
- **Northern Catalonia (Catalunya del Nord):** The historical Catalan-speaking area within France, corresponding to most of the department of Pyrenees-Orientales (Roussillon). Catalan speakers here number in the tens of thousands, though the language has been in significant decline due to French language policies.
- **La Franja (Franja de Ponent):** A strip of territory in eastern Aragon, Spain, where Catalan is traditionally spoken alongside Spanish.
- **L'Alguer (Alghero):** A city on the northwest coast of Sardinia, Italy, where a distinct variety of Catalan has been spoken since the 14th century. The Alguerese dialect is one of the most endangered varieties.

## Official Status

- **Official language:** Andorra (sole official language)
- **Co-official language:** Catalonia, Valencia, and the Balearic Islands (alongside Spanish)
- **Recognized minority language:** In parts of Aragon (La Franja), Pyrenees-Orientales (France), and Alghero (Sardinia, Italy)

## Historical Background

Catalan emerged as a distinct Romance language between the 8th and 10th centuries in the northeastern Iberian Peninsula and southern France. It developed from Vulgar Latin spoken in the territories of the former Roman province of Tarraconensis, heavily influenced by the Frankish administration of the Marca Hispanica (Spanish March), which was established as a buffer zone between the Carolingian Empire and Al-Andalus.

The language experienced a golden age during the medieval period, particularly between the 13th and 15th centuries. This era saw the expansion of the Crown of Aragon across the Mediterranean, bringing Catalan to the Balearic Islands, Valencia, Sardinia, Sicily, and even parts of Greece. Major literary figures of this period include Ramon Llull (1232-1316), widely regarded as the father of Catalan literature, and Ausias March (1400-1459), one of the greatest medieval poets writing in a Romance language.

The decline of Catalan began with the dynastic union of the Crown of Aragon and the Crown of Castile in 1479, and accelerated after the War of the Spanish Succession (1701-1714), when the Bourbon king Philip V abolished Catalan institutions and imposed Spanish through the Nueva Planta decrees. The language experienced a revival in the 19th century through the Renaixença cultural movement, and efforts at standardization culminated in the work of Pompeu Fabra in the early 20th century, who established modern Catalan orthography and grammar.

During the Franco dictatorship (1939-1975), Catalan was banned from public life, education, and media. Since the restoration of democracy and the establishment of autonomous communities, Catalan has undergone a significant recovery, supported by immersion education programs, public media, and legislative protection. Today, Catalan is a vibrant language with a strong presence in education, media, literature, and digital spaces, though it faces ongoing challenges from the dominance of Spanish and the effects of globalization.
