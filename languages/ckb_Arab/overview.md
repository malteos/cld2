# Central Kurdish / Sorani (کوردیی ناوەندی)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name**: Central Kurdish (Sorani) / کوردیی ناوەندی / سۆرانی
- **ISO 639-3 Code**: ckb
- **ISO 639-1**: ku (shared with Kurmanji)
- **Language Family**: Indo-European > Indo-Iranian > Iranian > Western Iranian > Northwestern Iranian > Kurdish
- **Script**: Modified Arabic script (Sorani alphabet / ئەلفوبێی سۆرانی)
- **Approximate Speakers**: 6-8 million native speakers
- **Geographic Regions**: Iraqi Kurdistan (Erbil, Sulaymaniyah, Halabja, parts of Kirkuk and Diyala), western Iran (Iranian Kurdistan / Kurdistan Province, Kermanshah Province, Ilam Province, parts of West Azerbaijan Province)
- **Official Status**: Official language of Iraq (alongside Arabic); official language of the Kurdistan Region of Iraq

## Overview

Central Kurdish, commonly known as Sorani (سۆرانی), is a Kurdish language variety belonging to the Northwestern Iranian branch of the Indo-European language family. The name "Sorani" derives from the historical principality of Soran in what is now the Erbil Governorate of Iraqi Kurdistan. The language is also referred to simply as Kurdish (کوردی) by its speakers, though linguists distinguish it from Northern Kurdish (Kurmanji) and Southern Kurdish.

Sorani is written using a modified Arabic script that has been adapted to represent Kurdish phonology. Unlike standard Arabic script, the Sorani alphabet includes additional characters to represent sounds not found in Arabic, such as ڕ (a trilled/tapped r), ڵ (a velarized l), ێ (a specific vowel), ۆ (an o-like vowel), ڤ (v), پ (p), چ (ch), ژ (zh), and گ (g). Crucially, Sorani Kurdish writes all vowels explicitly, unlike Arabic which typically omits short vowels in standard text. This makes the Sorani writing system more phonemically transparent than Arabic or Persian writing.

## Geographic Distribution

The primary area of Sorani usage spans the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, particularly the governorates of Sulaymaniyah (سلێمانی) and Erbil (هەولێر), as well as significant parts of Kirkuk (کەرکوک) and Halabja (هەڵەبجە). In Iran, Sorani is spoken in the Kurdistan Province (centered on Sanandaj/سنە), parts of Kermanshah Province, and areas of West Azerbaijan Province around the cities of Mahabad (مەهاباد) and Bukan (بۆکان). There are also significant diaspora communities in Europe, North America, and Australia.

## Historical Background

The development of Sorani as a literary and administrative language accelerated in the early 20th century. During the British Mandate period in Iraq (1920s-1930s), Kurdish was recognized as an official language in Kurdish-majority areas, which promoted the standardization of Sorani orthography. The poet Haji Qadir Koyi (حاجی قادری کۆیی, 1817-1897) was instrumental in establishing Sorani as a literary language, and subsequent writers and intellectuals continued this work through newspapers, literary journals, and educational materials.

The short-lived Republic of Mahabad (1946) in Iranian Kurdistan used Sorani as its official language, producing newspapers, textbooks, and government documents in the language. Although the republic lasted only one year, it had a lasting impact on Kurdish linguistic consciousness and standardization efforts.

Following the establishment of the Kurdistan Region of Iraq as an autonomous entity after 1991, and its constitutional recognition in the 2005 Iraqi Constitution, Sorani gained full official status. It is now used in government, education, media, and all public domains within the Kurdistan Region. Kurdish-language media, including television channels, radio stations, newspapers, and websites, have flourished, further standardizing the language.

## Dialectal Variation

Within Sorani, there is dialectal variation between the Sulaymaniyah dialect and the Erbil (Hawleri) dialect, as well as differences between Iraqi and Iranian varieties. The Sulaymaniyah dialect is generally considered the literary standard, though the Erbil dialect has also gained prestige due to Erbil's role as the capital of the Kurdistan Region. Iranian Sorani varieties (sometimes called Mukri after the Mukri confederation) show some phonological and lexical differences from Iraqi Sorani, partly due to influence from Persian.

## Current Status

Today, Sorani is a vibrant language with a growing body of literature, active media presence, and use in higher education. Several universities in the Kurdistan Region teach in Sorani, and there are ongoing efforts to develop technical and scientific terminology. The language faces challenges from the dominance of Arabic in Iraq and Persian in Iran, as well as from English in digital and academic contexts, but it remains the primary language of daily communication for millions of speakers.
