# Breton (Brezhoneg)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Breton (Brezhoneg)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: bre
- **ISO 639-1 code**: br
- **Script**: Latin
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Celtic > Insular Celtic > Brythonic
- **Approximate speakers**: 200,000-300,000 (estimates vary; UNESCO and Ofis Publik ar Brezhoneg surveys suggest around 210,000 active speakers as of recent counts, with a larger number of passive speakers)
- **Geographic region**: Brittany (Breizh) in northwestern France
- **Endangerment status**: UNESCO classifies Breton as "severely endangered"

## Language Family and Relatives

Breton belongs to the Brythonic (or Brittonic) branch of the Celtic language family, which itself is part of the larger Indo-European family. Its closest living relatives are Welsh (Cymraeg) and Cornish (Kernewek). All three descend from Common Brythonic, spoken in ancient Britain before and during the Roman period. Within the Brythonic branch, Breton is sometimes grouped more closely with Cornish in a "Southwestern Brythonic" subgroup, distinct from Welsh which represents the "Western Brythonic" branch. Despite the geographic separation, Breton retains significant structural and lexical similarities with both Welsh and Cornish, including shared features like initial consonant mutations and a similar core vocabulary.

The other major branch of Celtic, the Goidelic languages (Irish, Scottish Gaelic, and Manx), shares some broader Celtic features with Breton but diverged much earlier. Breton is the only Celtic language spoken on the European continent today.

## Geographic Distribution

Breton is spoken primarily in the western part of Brittany, historically known as "Lower Brittany" (Breizh-Izel), which encompasses the departments of Finistere (Penn-ar-Bed), Cotes-d'Armor (Aodoù-an-Arvor), Morbihan (Mor-bihan), and the western part of Ille-et-Vilaine (Il-ha-Gwilen) and Loire-Atlantique (Liger-Atlantel). The eastern part of Brittany, "Upper Brittany" (Breizh-Uhel), has historically been Gallo-speaking (a Romance language) rather than Breton-speaking.

Traditional dialect areas include Leoneg (Leon), Tregerieg (Tregor), Kerneveg (Cornouaille), and Gwenedeg (Vannetais). The Vannetais dialect is the most divergent from the others. Modern standard Breton attempts to bridge these dialect differences.

## History

Breton was brought to the Armorican peninsula (present-day Brittany) by Britons migrating from southwestern Britain, primarily from Cornwall and Devon, during the 5th to 7th centuries CE. This migration was driven by various factors, including pressure from Anglo-Saxon expansion and possibly plague. The migrants brought their Brythonic language, which gradually replaced the local Latin-based speech and any remaining Gaulish elements.

During the medieval period, Breton was the dominant language of western Brittany and was used at court and in literature. The oldest known Breton text is the Leiden Manuscript, dating to the late 8th or early 9th century. After the union of Brittany with France in 1532, French gradually gained prestige and administrative dominance. From the French Revolution onward, active policies of linguistic centralization suppressed regional languages, including Breton.

The 20th century saw the most dramatic decline in Breton speakers, particularly after World War I and especially after World War II, when the French government's education policies actively discouraged the use of Breton in schools. The phrase "Il est interdit de cracher par terre et de parler breton" (It is forbidden to spit on the ground and to speak Breton) symbolizes this period of repression.

## Official Status

France has not officially recognized Breton or any other regional language as an official language at the national level. France ratified no minority language charter, and the French Constitution states that "the language of the Republic is French." However, Breton has gained some regional recognition. The Diwan immersion school network, established in 1977, provides Breton-medium education. Bilingual programs exist in both public (Div Yezh) and Catholic (Dihun) school systems. The Ofis Publik ar Brezhoneg (Public Office for the Breton Language) was created in 1999 to promote the language.

## Current Situation

Despite revitalization efforts, the number of native speakers continues to decline as the older generation of fluent speakers passes away. Most fluent speakers are over 60 years of age. However, there is a growing movement of younger speakers who have learned Breton through immersion schools or adult learning programs. Breton-language media includes the television channel France 3 Breizh, radio stations such as Radio Kerne and Radio Kreiz Breizh, and various publications. The language has a presence on the internet, with Breton Wikipedia (Wikipedia e brezhoneg) and social media communities actively using the language.
