# North Azerbaijani (azj_Latn)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** North Azerbaijani (Azerbaycan dili)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** azj
- **Language Family:** Turkic > Common Turkic > Oghuz > Western Oghuz
- **Script:** Latin (Azerbaijani Latin alphabet, 32 letters)
- **Number of Speakers:** Approximately 9.2 million (North Azerbaijani specifically); total Azerbaijani speakers including South Azerbaijani exceed 25 million
- **Primary Region:** Republic of Azerbaijan
- **Other Regions:** Parts of Georgia (especially Kvemo Kartli and Kakheti), Russia (Dagestan), and diaspora communities in Turkey, Iran, Ukraine, and Central Asia
- **Official Status:** Official language of the Republic of Azerbaijan

## Classification

North Azerbaijani belongs to the Oghuz branch of the Turkic language family, making it closely related to Turkish, Turkmen, and Gagauz. Within the Oghuz branch, Azerbaijani and Turkish form the Western Oghuz subgroup and share a high degree of mutual intelligibility. North Azerbaijani is distinguished from South Azerbaijani (azb), which is spoken primarily in northwestern Iran and uses a modified Arabic script. The two varieties differ in phonology, vocabulary (South Azerbaijani has more Persian and Arabic loanwords, North Azerbaijani more Russian), and script, but they remain mutually intelligible.

## Script History

The history of the Azerbaijani writing system reflects the region's complex political history and has undergone four major transitions:

1. **Arabic script (pre-1929):** For centuries, Azerbaijani was written using a modified Arabic script, consistent with the broader Islamic cultural sphere of the region. Literary works from the medieval period, including those of Nizami Ganjavi and Fuzuli, were composed using this script.

2. **First Latin script period (1929-1939):** As part of Soviet language reforms and the broader Latinization movement across Turkic-speaking Soviet republics, a Latin-based alphabet was adopted in 1929. This was part of the Yanalif (New Alphabet) movement promoted by the All-Union Central Committee of the New Turkic Alphabet.

3. **Cyrillic script (1939-1991):** In 1939, under Stalin's directive, the Latin alphabet was replaced with a Cyrillic-based alphabet. This change was part of a broader Sovietization policy that aimed to strengthen ties between the Soviet republics and Russia while weakening pan-Turkic connections. The Cyrillic alphabet was used throughout the Soviet period and became deeply embedded in education, publishing, and official communication.

4. **Modern Latin script (1991-present):** Following the dissolution of the Soviet Union and Azerbaijan's independence in 1991, the country transitioned back to a Latin-based alphabet. The modern Azerbaijani Latin alphabet was officially adopted by law in 1991, with a gradual transition completed by the early 2000s. The current alphabet consists of 32 letters, including several distinctive characters such as Gg (with breve), Ss (with cedilla), and the letter Schwa.

## Sociolinguistic Context

North Azerbaijani serves as the primary language of education, government, media, and daily communication in the Republic of Azerbaijan. It is the sole official language of the country and is used in all domains of public life. The language has a robust literary tradition, a well-developed standard variety based on the Baku dialect, and is supported by extensive media, publishing, and digital resources.

The language has been influenced by Persian and Arabic through centuries of cultural contact, and by Russian during the Soviet period. In the post-independence era, there has been a conscious effort to replace Russian and Persian loanwords with native Turkic equivalents or neologisms, though many remain in everyday use. English loanwords have also entered the language in recent decades, particularly in technology, business, and popular culture.

North Azerbaijani has several regional dialects, including the Baku, Shamakhi, Ganja, Sheki, Mugan, and Lankaran dialects, but the standard literary language based on the Baku dialect is used in formal contexts throughout the country.
