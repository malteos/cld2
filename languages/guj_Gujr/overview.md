# Gujarati (ગુજરાતી)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Gujarati (ગુજરાતી)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: guj
- **ISO 639-1 code**: gu
- **Script**: Gujarati script (ગુજરાતી લિપિ)
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Indo-Iranian > Indo-Aryan > Western Indo-Aryan
- **Speakers**: Approximately 55-60 million native speakers
- **Writing direction**: Left to right

## Geographic Distribution

Gujarati is primarily spoken in the state of Gujarat in western India, where it serves as the official state language. It is also widely spoken in the adjacent union territories of Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu. Significant Gujarati-speaking populations exist in Mumbai (Maharashtra), Rajasthan, and Madhya Pradesh within India.

The Gujarati diaspora has established substantial communities worldwide. The United Kingdom hosts one of the largest overseas Gujarati populations, particularly in Leicester, London, and other major cities. In the United States, Gujarati communities are concentrated in New Jersey, New York, Texas, California, and Illinois. East African countries including Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania have historically significant Gujarati-speaking communities dating from colonial-era migrations. Fiji also has a notable Gujarati-speaking population descended from indentured laborers brought during the British colonial period. Other countries with significant Gujarati-speaking populations include Canada, Australia, South Africa, and various Gulf states.

## Official Status

Gujarati holds official language status at both state and national levels in India. It is the official language of the state of Gujarat and is one of the 22 languages listed in the Eighth Schedule of the Indian Constitution. This designation grants it recognition as a scheduled language of India, ensuring its use in official communications, education, and legal proceedings within Gujarat.

## Historical Development

Gujarati evolved from Old Gujarati, also known as Gurjar Apabhramsha or Old Western Rajasthani, which was spoken roughly between the 10th and 14th centuries. Old Gujarati itself descended from Shauraseni Prakrit through the Apabhramsha stage of Middle Indo-Aryan languages. The language shares its early history with Rajasthani, as both descended from the same Apabhramsha ancestor and began diverging around the 15th century.

The literary history of Gujarati stretches back to at least the 12th century. Early Gujarati literature was predominantly composed by Jain scholars and poets. The Jain narrative literature, known as rasa, prabandha, and phagu compositions, forms some of the earliest literary works in the language. Notable among the early poets is Hemachandra (12th century), a Jain polymath who composed important grammatical and literary works.

The medieval period (15th-17th centuries) saw the flourishing of devotional poetry in Gujarati. The poet Narsinh Mehta (1414-1481), revered as the Adi Kavi (first poet) of Gujarati literature, composed bhajans and padas that remain deeply influential. His composition "Vaishnav Jan To" was famously adopted by Mahatma Gandhi as a favorite hymn. The bhakti movement produced numerous other significant poets including Mirabai, Akha, and Premanand.

The modern period of Gujarati literature began in the 19th century under the influence of British colonial education and printing technology. Narmad (Narmadshankar Lalshankar Dave) is considered the pioneer of modern Gujarati literature and poetry. The 20th century witnessed the emergence of Gujarati as a language of political discourse, largely through the writings and speeches of Mahatma Gandhi, who was a native Gujarati speaker from Porbandar. Gandhi published extensively in Gujarati through journals such as Navjivan and Harijan, making Gujarati a language of national importance during the independence movement.

## Cultural Significance

Gujarati is the mother tongue of Mahatma Gandhi, the father of the Indian nation, as well as Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Muhammad Ali Jinnah, and other prominent figures in South Asian history. The language has a rich tradition in commerce, trade, and entrepreneurship, reflecting the historically mercantile culture of Gujarat. Gujarati literature has produced several notable figures including Govardhanram Tripathi, Kanaiyalal Munshi, Pannalal Patel (who received the Jnanpith Award), and Rajendra Shah. The language continues to thrive as a vibrant medium for literature, film, theater, journalism, and digital media.
