# Ancient Greek (Archaia Hellenike / Arkhaia Hellenike)

## Language Identity

- **Language name**: Ancient Greek
- **Native name**: Arkhaia Hellenike (Ἀρχαία Ἑλληνική)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: grc
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Hellenic > Greek
- **Script**: Greek (polytonic orthography)
- **Speakers**: Historical language with no native speakers. Widely studied in academia, theology, classics, philosophy, and linguistics. Estimated hundreds of thousands of active learners and scholars worldwide.

## Geographic and Historical Scope

Ancient Greek was spoken across the eastern Mediterranean world from roughly the 2nd millennium BCE through the medieval period. Its primary regions of use included mainland Greece, the Aegean islands, Crete, Cyprus, the western coast of Asia Minor (modern Turkey), Magna Graecia (southern Italy and Sicily), and eventually the entire Hellenistic world following the conquests of Alexander the Great.

### Historical Periods

Ancient Greek is conventionally divided into several major periods:

- **Mycenaean Greek** (c. 1600--1100 BCE): The earliest attested form, written in the Linear B syllabary. Known primarily from administrative tablets found at Knossos, Pylos, and Mycenae.
- **Archaic Greek** (c. 800--500 BCE): The period of Homer and Hesiod. Multiple dialects flourished, including Ionic, Aeolic, Doric, and Arcado-Cypriot. The Greek alphabet was adopted from the Phoenician script during this era.
- **Classical Greek** (c. 500--323 BCE): The golden age of Athens. The Attic dialect became the literary standard. This period produced the works of Plato, Aristotle, Sophocles, Euripides, Thucydides, and Demosthenes.
- **Koine Greek** (c. 323 BCE--330 CE): The common dialect that emerged from the Attic standard and spread across the Hellenistic world. It is the language of the New Testament, the Septuagint, and much Hellenistic literature and philosophy.
- **Byzantine/Medieval Greek** (c. 330--1453 CE): The continuation of Koine into the Eastern Roman Empire, gradually evolving toward Modern Greek while maintaining an archaizing literary tradition.

## Cultural and Scholarly Importance

Ancient Greek holds a singular position in Western intellectual history. It is the original language of foundational works in philosophy (Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, Epicureans, and Neoplatonists), science and mathematics (Euclid, Archimedes, Hippocrates, Galen), history (Herodotus, Thucydides, Polybius, Plutarch), and literature (Homer, the tragedians, Pindar, Sappho). The Christian New Testament was composed in Koine Greek, making the language essential for biblical scholarship and theology.

Greek contributed an enormous number of roots, prefixes, and suffixes to modern scientific, medical, and technical terminology in virtually every European language. Concepts such as democracy (demokratia), philosophy (philosophia), theater (theatron), and mathematics (mathematike) derive directly from Greek.

## Relationship to Modern Greek

Modern Greek (Nea Hellenike, ISO 639-3: ell) is the direct descendant of Ancient Greek through the Koine and Byzantine stages. The two share core vocabulary and basic grammatical structures, but differ substantially in phonology, morphology, and syntax. Key differences include:

- **Phonology**: Ancient Greek had a pitch accent system with contrastive vowel length; Modern Greek has a stress accent and no vowel length distinction. Several ancient diphthongs and vowel distinctions merged (iotacism).
- **Morphology**: Ancient Greek preserved the dative case, dual number, optative mood, and infinitive constructions, all of which are absent or vestigial in Modern Greek.
- **Orthography**: Ancient Greek uses the polytonic system with breathing marks, multiple accent types, and iota subscript; Modern Greek uses the monotonic system with a single accent mark.
- **Vocabulary**: While much core vocabulary is shared, Modern Greek has borrowed from Turkish, Italian, French, and English, and many ancient words have shifted in meaning or fallen out of use.

Despite these differences, the continuity of the Greek language tradition is remarkable, and educated speakers of Modern Greek can often comprehend passages of Ancient Greek, particularly Koine texts.
