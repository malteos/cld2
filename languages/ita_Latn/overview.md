# Italian (Italiano)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Italian (Italiano)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: ita
- **Script**: Latin (Latn)
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Italic > Romance > Italo-Dalmatian
- **Native speakers**: ~65 million
- **Total speakers**: ~85 million (including L2 speakers)

## Geographic Distribution

Italian is the official language of Italy, Switzerland (co-official with German, French, and Romansh), San Marino, and Vatican City. It is also an official language of the European Union and holds recognized minority language status in parts of Slovenia and Croatia (Istria). Significant Italian-speaking diaspora communities exist in the United States, Argentina, Brazil, Canada, Australia, Germany, Belgium, and France. Historically, Italian served as a lingua franca throughout the Mediterranean basin during the Renaissance period, particularly in commerce and the arts.

## Historical Development

Modern standard Italian is based primarily on the Tuscan dialect, specifically the literary Florentine variety used by the great writers of the fourteenth century: Dante Alighieri (Divine Comedy), Francesco Petrarch (Canzoniere), and Giovanni Boccaccio (Decameron). This literary tradition established the foundation for a common written language centuries before political unification. The "questione della lingua" (language question) was a prolonged debate spanning several centuries about which form of the language should serve as the national standard. Alessandro Manzoni's revision of his novel "I Promessi Sposi" (The Betrothed) in the nineteenth century, rewritten in contemporary Florentine, was a landmark in the standardization process.

At the time of Italian unification in 1861, only an estimated 2.5% of the population actually spoke standard Italian in daily life. The vast majority communicated in regional languages and dialects. Mass education, military conscription, internal migration, and eventually radio and television were the key forces that spread standard Italian throughout the peninsula during the twentieth century.

## Dialectal Diversity

Italy possesses extraordinary linguistic diversity. The traditional "dialects" of Italy are, in many cases, distinct Romance languages in their own right rather than mere variants of standard Italian. The major dialectal groupings include Gallo-Italic varieties (Piedmontese, Lombard, Ligurian, Emilian-Romagnol) in the north, Venetian in the northeast, Tuscan in the center, Central Italian varieties (Romanesco, Umbrian, Marchigiano), and Southern Italian varieties (Neapolitan, Sicilian, Calabrian, Apulian). Sardinian is classified as a separate Romance language, as are Friulian and Ladin. The degree of mutual intelligibility between some of these varieties and standard Italian can be quite limited.

## Sociolinguistic Status

Today, standard Italian is the dominant language of education, media, government, and formal communication throughout Italy. However, regional varieties and local dialects continue to be spoken, particularly among older generations and in informal settings. Code-switching between standard Italian and regional varieties is common. Italian maintains significant international prestige in the domains of music, cuisine, fashion, design, and the visual arts.
