# Italian Grammar

## Word Order

Italian follows a basic Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) word order, though it permits considerable flexibility due to its rich inflectional morphology. Topic-fronting and focus constructions allow objects and other constituents to appear sentence-initially. Pro-drop is standard: subject pronouns are typically omitted because the verb conjugation indicates the subject (e.g., "parlo" = "I speak" without the pronoun "io"). Subject pronouns are used primarily for emphasis or disambiguation.

## Morphological Typology

Italian is a fusional language. Grammatical information such as person, number, tense, mood, and aspect is expressed through suffixes that simultaneously encode multiple categories. Nouns and adjectives inflect for gender and number, while verbs inflect for person, number, tense, mood, and aspect.

## Nominal System

### Gender and Number

Italian has two grammatical genders: masculine and feminine. There is no neuter gender. Number distinguishes singular and plural. There is no case system; grammatical relationships are expressed through word order and prepositions.

Typical noun endings:
- Masculine singular: -o (libro), plural: -i (libri)
- Feminine singular: -a (casa), plural: -e (case)
- Masculine/Feminine singular: -e (padre/madre), plural: -i (padri/madri)

Some nouns have irregular plurals, and certain nouns change gender between singular and plural (e.g., "il braccio" > "le braccia").

### Articles

Italian has definite and indefinite articles that agree in gender and number with the noun they modify. The choice of article form depends on the initial sound of the following word.

**Definite articles**: il, lo, la, l', i, gli, le
**Indefinite articles**: un, uno, una, un'

Prepositions contract with definite articles to form articulated prepositions (preposizioni articolate): di + il = del, a + la = alla, in + i = nei, su + gli = sugli, etc.

### Adjective Agreement

Adjectives agree with the noun they modify in gender and number. They may appear before or after the noun, though post-nominal position is the default. Some adjectives change meaning depending on position (e.g., "un grande uomo" = a great man; "un uomo grande" = a big/tall man).

## Verbal System

The Italian verbal system is highly complex, with three conjugation classes identified by infinitive endings: -are (first conjugation, e.g., parlare), -ere (second conjugation, e.g., vedere), and -ire (third conjugation, e.g., dormire). The third conjugation has a subclass that inserts -isc- in certain forms (e.g., finire > finisco).

### Moods and Tenses

**Indicative mood** (statements of fact):
- Presente (present): parlo, parli, parla, parliamo, parlate, parlano
- Imperfetto (imperfect): parlavo, parlavi, parlava, parlavamo, parlavate, parlavano
- Passato prossimo (present perfect): ho parlato, hai parlato, ha parlato...
- Passato remoto (simple past): parlai, parlasti, parlo, parlammo, parlaste, parlarono
- Trapassato prossimo (pluperfect): avevo parlato...
- Trapassato remoto (past anterior): ebbi parlato...
- Futuro semplice (simple future): parlero, parlerai, parlera, parleremo, parlerete, parleranno
- Futuro anteriore (future perfect): avro parlato...

**Subjunctive mood** (doubt, wish, hypothesis):
- Presente: che io parli, che tu parli, che lui parli...
- Imperfetto: che io parlassi, che tu parlassi...
- Passato: che io abbia parlato...
- Trapassato: che io avessi parlato...

**Conditional mood**: parlerei, parleresti, parlerebbe, parleremmo, parlereste, parlerebbero

**Imperative mood**: parla (tu), parli (Lei), parliamo (noi), parlate (voi), parlino (Loro)

### Compound Tenses and Auxiliaries

Compound tenses are formed with the auxiliary verbs "avere" (to have) or "essere" (to be) plus the past participle. Transitive verbs typically take "avere," while intransitive verbs of motion and change of state, reflexive verbs, and reciprocal verbs take "essere." When "essere" is used, the past participle agrees in gender and number with the subject.

### Irregular Verbs

Many of the most frequent Italian verbs are irregular, including essere (to be), avere (to have), andare (to go), fare (to do/make), dire (to say), dare (to give), stare (to stay/be), venire (to come), potere (to be able), volere (to want), dovere (to have to), sapere (to know), and bere (to drink).

## Pronoun System

### Clitic Pronouns

Italian makes extensive use of clitic pronouns, which are unstressed pronouns that attach to the verb. Direct object clitics: mi, ti, lo, la, ci, vi, li, le. Indirect object clitics: mi, ti, gli, le, ci, vi, gli/loro. Clitics precede conjugated verbs but attach to infinitives, gerunds, and imperative forms. Double clitic combinations follow specific ordering rules (e.g., "me lo dai" = you give it to me).

### The Partitive "ne"

The clitic pronoun "ne" functions as a partitive (meaning "of it/them," "some," or "any") and also replaces prepositional phrases introduced by "di." It is a distinctive feature of Italian syntax: "Ne voglio tre" (I want three of them), "Ne parliamo domani" (We'll talk about it tomorrow).

## Relative Clauses

Italian uses "che" as the general relative pronoun for both subject and direct object relative clauses. For oblique relative clauses, "cui" (preceded by a preposition) or "il quale / la quale / i quali / le quali" (inflected forms) are used.
