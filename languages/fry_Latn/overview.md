# Western Frisian (fry)

## Summary

Western Frisian (endonym: *Frysk*) is an Indo-European language of the West Germanic branch (Indo-European → Germanic → West → Anglo-Frisian → Frisian). ISO codes: 639-1 `fy`, 639-2 `fry`, 639-3 `fry`. It is written in the Latin script. The language has approximately 400,000–500,000 native speakers, concentrated in the Dutch province of Friesland (*Fryslân*), with smaller diaspora communities in the Netherlands and North America. It is a co-official regional language of Friesland alongside Dutch. Within the Frisian branch, Western Frisian is by far the largest member; Saterland (East) Frisian and North Frisian are separate languages spoken in Germany.

## Writing system

Western Frisian uses the 26-letter Latin alphabet augmented with circumflex vowels `â, ê, ô, û` (long/lowered vowels, as in *hûn* 'dog', *wêr* 'where', *skoalle*, *wâld*), an acute `é` for stress (*fjúr*, *sée*), and a diaeresis only in loanwords. The digraphs `ea, ie, oa, ue, eu, ui` mark specifically Frisian diphthongs (*read* 'red', *brief* 'letter', *boat* 'boat'). Only proper nouns and sentence-initial words are capitalised. Punctuation follows standard Dutch/Western norms.

## History

The modern written standard was codified in the 19th and 20th centuries; the 1980 spelling reform standardised circumflex and digraph use. Despite heavy bilingual contact with Dutch, Frisian preserves distinctive Anglo-Frisian features that make it the continental language most historically related to English.
