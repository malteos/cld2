# Irish (Gaeilge) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Irish (Gaeilge)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: gle
- **ISO 639-1 code**: ga
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Celtic > Insular Celtic > Goidelic
- **Script**: Latin
- **Estimated speakers**: ~1.7 million with some ability (Republic of Ireland census); approximately 70,000-80,000 daily native speakers
- **Regions**: Ireland (especially Gaeltacht areas), Northern Ireland, diaspora communities (United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia)
- **Official status**: First official language of the Republic of Ireland (under Article 8 of the Constitution), official and working language of the European Union (since 2007, full status from 2022)

## Geographic Distribution

Irish is spoken primarily in Ireland, with the strongest concentration of native speakers found in the Gaeltacht regions. These officially designated Irish-speaking areas are located along the western seaboard and include parts of counties Galway (Conamara and the Aran Islands), Donegal (Gaoth Dobhair, Cloch Cheann Fhaola), Kerry (Corca Dhuibhne, Uíbh Ráthach), Cork (Múscraí, Oileán Chléire), Mayo (Tourmakeady, Achill), Waterford (An Rinn), and Meath (Ráth Cairn, Baile Ghib). Outside of the Gaeltacht, a growing urban Irish-speaking community exists in cities such as Dublin, Belfast, Galway, and Cork, often centered around Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools) and cultural organizations.

In Northern Ireland, Irish has recognition under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages and has gained increased institutional support in recent decades, particularly in Belfast where a vibrant Irish-speaking community has developed in the west of the city.

## Historical Background

Irish is one of the oldest vernacular literatures in Europe, with the earliest written records dating to the 6th century in the form of Ogham inscriptions, followed by Old Irish manuscripts from the 7th and 8th centuries. The literary tradition spans over 1,500 years, encompassing the great medieval manuscript traditions including the Book of Leinster, the Book of the Dun Cow, and the Yellow Book of Lecan.

The language went through several historical stages: Primitive Irish (up to the 4th century), Old Irish (6th-10th centuries), Middle Irish (10th-12th centuries), Early Modern Irish or Classical Irish (13th-17th centuries), and Modern Irish (17th century to present). During the Classical period, a standardized literary language was maintained by the bardic schools across Ireland and Gaelic Scotland.

The decline of Irish accelerated dramatically under British colonial rule. The Penal Laws, the displacement of the Irish-speaking aristocracy, the spread of English as the language of administration, commerce, and social advancement, and most devastatingly the Great Famine of 1845-1852, all contributed to a catastrophic reduction in the number of Irish speakers. The Famine disproportionately affected Irish-speaking areas in the west, through death and emigration, reducing the population of these regions by as much as half.

## Revival Efforts

The revival of Irish began in earnest with the founding of Conradh na Gaeilge (the Gaelic League) in 1893. Following Irish independence in 1922, the new state adopted Irish as its first official language and undertook extensive efforts to revive its use. These included making Irish a compulsory subject in schools, establishing Gaeltacht development agencies, founding the Irish-language radio station Raidio na Gaeltachta (1972) and television station TG4 (1996), and supporting Irish-language publishing and cultural activities.

Today, the language continues to face challenges despite institutional support. The number of daily speakers in Gaeltacht communities continues to decline, though counterbalanced to some degree by the growth of Irish-medium education (Gaelscoileanna) outside the Gaeltacht and increased interest among younger urban speakers. The 20-Year Strategy for the Irish Language 2010-2030 represents the most recent comprehensive government plan for language revitalization. Digital media, social media communities, and online learning platforms such as Duolingo have also contributed to growing international interest in the language.

## Dialects

Modern Irish has three main dialect groups corresponding to the provinces of Munster (Gaeilge na Mumhan), Connacht (Gaeilge Chonnacht), and Ulster (Gaeilge Uladh). These dialects differ in pronunciation, vocabulary, and some grammatical features. An Caighdean Oifigiuil (the Official Standard), established in the 1950s, provides a standardized written form used in official documents and education, though it draws primarily from Connacht Irish.
