# Irish (Gaeilge) - Grammar

## Word Order

Irish is a VSO (Verb-Subject-Object) language, one of a relatively small number of languages worldwide with this basic word order. The verb always comes first in a declarative sentence:

- **Léann an cailín an leabhar.** (Reads the girl the book = "The girl reads the book.")
- **Cheannaigh Seán carr nua.** (Bought Seán car new = "Seán bought a new car.")

Adjectives generally follow the noun they modify, and the genitive noun follows the noun it modifies. This produces a head-initial structure throughout the syntax.

## Morphological Type

Irish is a fusional language with a rich inflectional morphology. One of its most distinctive features is the system of initial mutations, where the beginning of a word changes depending on its grammatical context. This makes Irish morphology notable for operating at the left edge of the word, unlike most fusional languages that rely primarily on suffixes.

## Phonology

### Broad and Slender Consonant Distinction

The most fundamental principle of Irish phonology is the distinction between broad (velarized) and slender (palatalized) consonants. Every consonant in Irish is either broad or slender, and this distinction is phonemic, meaning it can change the meaning of a word:

- **bó** [boː] ("cow") vs **beo** [bʲoː] ("alive")
- **fear** [fʲar] ("man") vs **far** [far] (broad f)

This distinction is reflected in the spelling rule known as **caol le caol agus leathan le leathan** ("slender with slender and broad with broad"), meaning that the vowels on either side of a consonant must agree: both must be slender vowels (e, i) or both must be broad vowels (a, o, u). This orthographic rule is fundamental to reading and writing Irish and gives Irish text its characteristic visual appearance.

### Vowel System

Irish has both short and long vowels. Long vowels are marked with a síneadh fada (acute accent): á, é, í, ó, ú. The vowel system interacts extensively with the broad/slender consonant system, with many vowel combinations serving primarily to indicate consonant quality rather than representing distinct vowel sounds.

## Initial Mutations

Irish has two main initial mutations that are central to its grammar:

### Lenition (Séimhiú)

Lenition historically represented a weakening of the initial consonant and is now marked by adding an **h** after the consonant:

| Base | Lenited |
|------|---------|
| b → | bh |
| c → | ch |
| d → | dh |
| f → | fh (silent) |
| g → | gh |
| m → | mh |
| p → | ph |
| s → | sh |
| t → | th |

Lenition occurs in many grammatical contexts: after the singular definite article with feminine nouns (**an bhean** - "the woman"), after possessive adjectives **mo, do, a** (his), after certain prepositions, in the past tense of verbs, after vocative particle **a**, and in many other environments.

### Eclipsis (Urú)

Eclipsis involves placing a consonant before the initial consonant of a word, with the prefixed consonant being the one pronounced:

| Base | Eclipsed |
|------|----------|
| b → | mb |
| c → | gc |
| d → | nd |
| f → | bhf |
| g → | ng |
| p → | bp |
| t → | dt |
| vowel → | n- (before vowels) |

Eclipsis occurs after the plural definite article in the genitive (**na gcapall** - "of the horses"), after the preposition **i** ("in") (**i mBaile Átha Cliath** - "in Dublin"), after the number **seacht** ("seven") and others, and in various other grammatical environments.

## Noun System

### Gender

Irish has two grammatical genders: masculine and feminine. Gender affects the form of the article, adjective agreement, pronoun reference, and the application of initial mutations. There are some tendencies (most nouns ending in a broad consonant are masculine, many ending in a slender consonant or in -óg, -eog are feminine), but gender must largely be learned with each noun.

### Cases

Historically, Irish had five cases: nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, and vocative. In modern standard Irish, the system has been simplified considerably:

- **Nominative/Accusative** (ainmneach): used for the subject and direct object
- **Genitive** (ginideach): used to show possession, after compound prepositions, and after verbal nouns; the genitive often involves significant changes to the noun's form
- **Vocative** (gairmeach): used for direct address, preceded by the particle **a** and triggering lenition: **a Sheáin!** ("O Seán!")
- **Dative** (tabharthach): largely merged with the nominative in the standard language, but preserved in some dialects, particularly Munster Irish

### Declensions

Irish nouns are divided into five declensions, each with different patterns for forming the genitive singular, nominative plural, and genitive plural. The declension system interacts with gender and determines the specific forms a noun takes.

## Verb System

### Conjugations

Irish verbs belong to one of two conjugations. The first conjugation consists primarily of monosyllabic verbs (or verbs with a root of one syllable), while the second conjugation consists of polysyllabic verbs, typically ending in **-igh** or **-aigh**.

### Synthetic and Analytic Forms

Irish verbs have two types of conjugation:

- **Synthetic forms**: the subject pronoun is incorporated into the verb ending. Example: **léimid** ("we read" - first person plural marker **-mid** is part of the verb)
- **Analytic forms**: the verb takes a general ending and the subject is expressed as a separate pronoun. Example: **léann siad** ("they read")

The balance between synthetic and analytic forms varies by dialect; Munster Irish retains more synthetic forms, while the standard language uses a mix.

### Tenses and Moods

Irish has the following tenses and moods: present (láithreach), past (caite), future (fáistineach), imperfect/past habitual (gnáthchaite), conditional (coinníollach), imperative (modh ordaitheach), subjunctive (modh foshuiteach - increasingly rare), and the present habitual.

### Dependent and Independent Forms

Many irregular verbs, and the copula, have two distinct forms: an **independent form** (used at the beginning of a positive statement) and a **dependent form** (used after verbal particles such as **ní** (negation), **an** (question), **go** (that), **nach** (that not)):

- Independent: **Tá mé** ("I am"), **Chonaic mé** ("I saw")
- Dependent: **Ní fhuil mé** / **Níl mé** ("I am not"), **Ní fhaca mé** ("I did not see")

### Irregular Verbs

Irish has approximately 11 irregular verbs that are among the most frequently used in the language: **bí** (be), **abair** (say), **beir** (catch/bring), **clois/cluin** (hear), **déan** (do/make), **faigh** (get), **feic** (see), **ith** (eat), **tabhair** (give), **tar** (come), **téigh** (go).

## The Copula (An Chopail)

Irish has two verbs meaning "to be": the copula **is** and the substantive verb **bí** (which conjugates as **tá** in the present tense). They are used in distinct grammatical contexts:

- **Tá** (substantive verb): used for descriptions of state, location, and temporary conditions. **Tá sé fuar** ("It is cold" - describing the weather now).
- **Is** (copula): used for identification, classification, and permanent characteristics. **Is múinteoir í** ("She is a teacher").

This distinction is a fundamental aspect of Irish grammar and has no direct equivalent in English.

## Prepositional Pronouns (Forainmneacha Réamhfhoclacha)

Irish prepositions conjugate when combined with personal pronouns, creating fused forms called prepositional pronouns. This is a distinctive feature of Irish and the other Celtic languages:

| | ag (at) | ar (on) | le (with) |
|---|---------|---------|-----------|
| mé | agam | orm | liom |
| tú | agat | ort | leat |
| sé | aige | air | leis |
| sí | aici | uirthi | léi |
| muid | againn | orainn | linn |
| sibh | agaibh | oraibh | libh |
| siad | acu | orthu | leo |

These prepositional pronouns are essential to expressing possession (**Tá leabhar agam** - "I have a book", literally "A book is at-me"), emotions (**Tá áthas orm** - "I am happy", literally "Happiness is on-me"), and many other concepts.

## Verbal Noun and Progressive Aspect

Irish expresses the progressive (continuous) aspect using the construction **ag** + verbal noun: **Tá mé ag léamh** ("I am reading", literally "I am at reading"). This construction, while superficially similar to the English progressive, uses the preposition **ag** ("at") combined with the verbal noun form of the verb. The verbal noun is also used after many other prepositions to express different aspectual meanings: **tar éis** + verbal noun for the perfect ("having just done"), **ar tí** + verbal noun for the prospective ("about to do").
