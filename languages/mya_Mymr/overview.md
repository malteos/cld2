# Burmese (mya_Mymr) -- Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Burmese (မြန်မာဘာသာ)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** mya
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** my
- **Script:** Myanmar (Burmese) script (ISO 15924: Mymr)
- **Language Family:** Sino-Tibetan > Tibeto-Burman > Lolo-Burmese > Burmish
- **Native Speakers:** ~33 million
- **Total Speakers:** ~43 million (including L2 speakers)
- **Official Status:** Official language of Myanmar (Burma)

## Geographic Distribution

Burmese is the official and national language of the Republic of the Union of Myanmar (formerly Burma). It is the mother tongue of the Bamar (Burman) people, who constitute approximately two-thirds of Myanmar's population. The language is spoken throughout the country, with the highest concentration of native speakers in the central Irrawaddy River basin, including the major cities of Yangon (Rangoon), Mandalay, and Naypyidaw (the capital since 2006).

Significant Burmese-speaking diaspora communities exist in Thailand, Malaysia, Singapore, the United States (particularly in California, New York, and Texas), Australia, the United Kingdom, Japan, and South Korea. Refugees and migrant workers have established Burmese-speaking enclaves in border regions of neighboring countries, particularly along the Thai-Myanmar border.

Within Myanmar, Burmese serves as a lingua franca among the country's diverse ethnic groups, which number over 135 officially recognized nationalities. While many of these groups maintain their own languages, Burmese is widely used for interethnic communication, government administration, education, and media.

## Historical Development

The history of the Burmese language can be traced through inscriptional evidence dating back to the early 12th century. The earliest known Burmese inscription is the Myazedi inscription (also known as the Rajakumar inscription), dated to 1113 CE, found near Bagan (Pagan). This quadrilingual inscription, written in Burmese, Pali, Mon, and Pyu, provides crucial evidence for the historical development of the language and its script.

The development of Burmese is conventionally divided into several periods:

- **Old Burmese (11th--16th centuries):** Characterized by a more complex consonant cluster system, different vowel qualities, and a writing system closer to its Mon script ancestor. Old Burmese texts are primarily inscriptions on stone and metal.
- **Middle Burmese (16th--18th centuries):** A transitional period during which many of the sound changes that distinguish modern Burmese from Old Burmese took place, including the simplification of initial consonant clusters and shifts in vowel quality.
- **Modern Burmese (18th century--present):** The language as it is spoken and written today, with a significant distinction between the literary (formal) register and the colloquial (spoken) register.

## Script

The Myanmar script is an abugida derived from the Mon script, which in turn descended from the Pallava script of South India, ultimately tracing back to the Brahmi script. The script is characterized by its circular and rounded letterforms, a feature attributed to the historical use of palm leaves as a writing surface -- straight lines would tear the leaves along their fibers.

The script consists of 33 consonant letters, each carrying an inherent vowel /a/. Vowels other than the inherent /a/ are represented by diacritical marks placed above, below, before, or after the consonant. The script also includes medial consonant signs, tone marks, and various punctuation symbols. Myanmar numerals (၀-၉) are used alongside or in place of Arabic numerals.

## Sociolinguistic Situation

Burmese exhibits a notable degree of diglossia, with distinct literary (စာဘာသာ, sa-basa) and colloquial (သဘာသာ, tha-basa) registers. The literary register is used in formal writing, news broadcasts, official speeches, and academic contexts, while the colloquial register is used in everyday conversation. The two registers differ in vocabulary, grammar (particularly in verb particles and sentence-final markers), and to some extent in pronunciation.

Major dialect divisions include Standard Burmese (based on the Mandalay-Yangon dialect continuum), Arakanese/Rakhine (sometimes considered a separate language), Intha, Tavoyan (Dawei), and Beik (Merguese). Standard Burmese, based on the speech of the central plains, is the variety used in education, government, and media throughout the country.
