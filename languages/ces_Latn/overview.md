# Czech (cestina / cestina)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Czech (cestina)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: ces
- **ISO 639-1 code**: cs
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > West Slavic > Czech-Slovak
- **Script**: Latin with diacritics (hacek and carky system)
- **Approximate speakers**: ~10.7 million native speakers
- **Geographic regions**: Czech Republic (Czechia), with diaspora communities in Slovakia, Austria, Germany, the United States, and other countries
- **Official status**: Official language of the Czech Republic; official language of the European Union

## Historical Development

Czech has a rich literary and linguistic history stretching back to the medieval period. The earliest written records in Czech date to the 13th century, with glosses and short texts appearing in otherwise Latin documents. The language underwent significant development during the 14th century under the reign of Charles IV, when Prague became a major European cultural center and Czech was used in administration, literature, and scholarship alongside Latin and German.

One of the most transformative figures in Czech linguistic history is Jan Hus (c. 1370-1415), the religious reformer and rector of Charles University in Prague. Hus proposed a radical simplification of Czech orthography, replacing the cumbersome digraphs and trigraphs used to represent Czech sounds with single letters modified by diacritical marks. His system introduced the hacek (a small caron placed above consonants such as c, s, z, r, n, d, t) and the carka (an acute accent placed above vowels to indicate length). This innovation, codified in the treatise De Orthographia Bohemica (attributed to Hus), became the foundation of modern Czech spelling and influenced the orthographic systems of many other Slavic languages, including Slovak, Slovenian, and Croatian.

The period following the Battle of White Mountain in 1620 and the subsequent Habsburg domination led to a severe decline in the use of Czech in public life, education, and literature. German became the dominant language of administration and higher culture in the Czech lands, and Czech was increasingly confined to rural and lower-class usage. This period of linguistic marginalization lasted roughly two centuries.

## Czech National Revival

The Czech National Revival (Ceske narodni obrozeni), spanning approximately from the late 18th century to the mid-19th century, was a cultural and linguistic movement that sought to restore Czech as a language of scholarship, literature, and public life. Key figures in the revival include Josef Dobrovsky, who wrote a systematic grammar of Czech and is often called the "patriarch" of Slavic studies, and Josef Jungmann, who compiled the first comprehensive Czech-German dictionary and worked to expand Czech vocabulary to cover modern scientific and intellectual concepts.

The revivalists drew heavily on older literary Czech, particularly the language of the Kralice Bible (1579-1593), as a model for the renewed standard language. This decision gave modern standard Czech a somewhat archaic character compared to everyday spoken Czech, a gap that persists to this day in the distinction between spisovna cestina (standard/literary Czech) and obecna cestina (Common Czech, the colloquial form used in everyday speech, especially in Bohemia).

## Modern Czech

Following the establishment of Czechoslovakia in 1918, Czech became the primary official language of the new state. The close relationship between Czech and Slovak was formally recognized, and for much of the 20th century the two languages were considered by some to be variants of a single "Czechoslovak" language, though this view was always contested by Slovak linguists and nationalists.

After the peaceful dissolution of Czechoslovakia in 1993 (the "Velvet Divorce"), Czech became the sole official language of the newly independent Czech Republic (Czechia). Today, Czech is a vibrant language used in all domains of modern life, including government, education, science, media, technology, and the arts. It is one of the 24 official languages of the European Union and is protected and promoted by the Institute of the Czech Language (Ustav pro jazyk cesky) of the Czech Academy of Sciences.
