# Czech vs. Other Languages: Differential Diagnostics

## Czech vs. Slovak (CRITICAL CONFUSABLE PAIR)

Czech and Slovak are the most closely related pair of Slavic languages and are largely mutually intelligible. They share the same language subgroup (West Slavic, Czech-Slovak branch), similar grammar, and a great deal of vocabulary. During the Czechoslovak era (1918-1993), speakers of both languages were regularly exposed to each other through media, education, and daily life. Distinguishing Czech from Slovak is one of the most challenging tasks in Slavic language identification and requires attention to specific orthographic, phonological, and lexical markers.

### Orthographic Differences (Most Reliable for Text-Based ID)

#### The r character (STRONGEST SINGLE MARKER)
The letter **r** (r with hacek) exists ONLY in Czech. Slovak does not have this character or the sound it represents. Any text containing r is almost certainly Czech. Common Czech words with r include:
- tri (three), ctyri (four), rijen (October)
- reka (river), more (sea), dvorek (yard)
- potreba (need), pritel (friend), priklad (example)
- prikaz (command), naproti (opposite), verejny (public)
- Frequency: r appears in approximately 1.5-2% of all characters in Czech text.

#### The u character (STRONG MARKER)
Czech uses **u** (u with ring above/krouzek) for the long /uu/ sound in non-initial positions. Slovak does not use this character. Slovak instead has **o** (o with circumflex/vokan) for a diphthong /uo/ that historically corresponds to the same Proto-Slavic source.
- Czech: dum (house) vs. Slovak: dom
- Czech: kuze (skin) vs. Slovak: koza
- Czech: duvod (reason) vs. Slovak: dovod
- Czech: puvod (origin) vs. Slovak: povod

#### The e character (STRONG MARKER)
Czech uses **e** (e with hacek) to represent /je/ or to trigger palatalization. Slovak does not have this character.
- Czech: mesto (city) vs. Slovak: mesto (same spelling but different pronunciation)
- Czech: deti (children), vetsi (bigger), nejlepsi (best)
- Czech: pekne (nicely), some (dream)

Note: While Slovak also has words spelled with "mesto", the character e as a standalone diacritical letter triggering consonant changes is a Czech phenomenon.

#### Slovak o (NOT in Czech)
Slovak uses **o** (o with circumflex) which does not exist in Czech. This is a strong reverse marker - its presence indicates Slovak, not Czech.
- Slovak: dom (he carries), kost (bone), noc (night)

#### Slovak a, l, r, o (Slovak-only diacritics)
Slovak has several diacritical characters not found in Czech:
- **a** with circumflex (a) - does not exist in standard Czech
- **l** with acute (l) - syllabic long l, not in Czech
- **r** with acute (r) - syllabic long r (not to be confused with Czech r with hacek!)
- **o** with circumflex (o) - as discussed above

The presence of any of these characters rules out Czech.

### Phonological/Orthographic Pattern Differences

#### Czech ou vs. Slovak uo/o
The Czech diphthong "ou" frequently corresponds to Slovak "o" (circumflex o):
- Czech: mouka (flour) vs. Slovak: muka
- Czech: louka (meadow) vs. Slovak: luka
- Czech: koupit (to buy) vs. Slovak: kupit

#### Czech vy- vs. Slovak vy-
Both languages use the prefix vy-, but Slovak more frequently uses the variant vy- where Czech has different forms:
- Czech: vzduch (air) vs. Slovak: vzduch (same here)
- But different prefixation patterns exist in many verb pairs.

#### Consonant Group Differences
- Czech frequently uses "str" clusters: stromy (trees), stredni (middle)
- Slovak "ia", "ie", "iu" diphthongs are absent from Czech: Slovak "biely" (white) vs. Czech "bily"; Slovak "kvetina" has "ie" in some forms

### Grammatical Differences

- Czech has more complex declension patterns with more paradigm types
- Czech preserves the vocative case more robustly than Slovak
- Some case endings differ: Czech instrumental plural "-mi"/"-y" vs. Slovak "-mi"/"-ami"
- Czech past tense uses auxiliary in 1st and 2nd person; Slovak has dropped the auxiliary in some constructions
- Different preposition usage in some contexts: Czech "v" (in) vs. Slovak "v" (same but different phonological behavior)

## Czech vs. Polish

Czech and Polish are both West Slavic languages but belong to different sub-branches (Czech-Slovak vs. Lechitic). While they share many structural features, their orthographic systems are quite different, making text-based distinction relatively straightforward.

### Orthographic System Differences
- **Czech uses hacek (caron)**: c, s, z, r, d, t, n
- **Polish uses kreska (acute) and other marks**: s (acute s), z (acute z/dot z), c (acute c), n (acute n), l (stroke l), a (ogonek a), e (ogonek e)
- Polish uses **ogonek** (a, e) which does not exist in Czech
- Polish uses **kreska** on consonants (s, c, z, n) where Czech uses hacek
- Polish uses **l** (l with stroke) for /w/; Czech does not have this character
- Czech **r** does not exist in Polish
- Czech **e** does not exist in Polish
- Czech **u** does not exist in Polish

### Common Distinguishing Features
- Polish uses digraphs extensively: sz, cz, rz, ch, dz, dz (with marks). Czech avoids digraphs in favor of single diacritical characters.
- Polish has nasal vowels (a, e); Czech does not.
- Polish word-final devoicing is reflected in some spellings; Czech maintains voicing distinction.
- Different vocabulary despite shared roots: Czech "vzduch" (air) vs. Polish "powietrze"; Czech "vlak" (train) vs. Polish "pociag"

## Czech vs. Croatian/Serbian

Czech and Croatian/Serbian (South Slavic) are more distantly related. Croatian uses a Latin-script orthography that shares some diacritical marks with Czech (c, s, z) but lacks Czech r, e, u, d, t, n. Croatian also has **dz** (d with stroke) which Czech does not have.

### Key Differences
- Croatian lacks hacek on d, t, n (uses dj/d for the d sound, and does not have t/n sounds)
- Croatian has **dz** (d with stroke); Czech does not
- Croatian does not use y; Czech uses y extensively
- Different vocabulary and grammatical structures as South vs. West Slavic
- Croatian does not have r or u (ring)

## Czech vs. Slovenian

Slovenian is a South Slavic language that uses fewer diacritical marks than Czech. Slovenian uses c, s, z (same hacek characters as Czech) but lacks r, e, u, d, t, n. Slovenian preserves the dual number, which Czech has lost.

### Key Differences
- Slovenian has only three hacek characters: c, s, z
- Slovenian does not have r, e, u (ring), or the palatalized d, t, n
- Slovenian has a dual number for 2 items; Czech does not
- Different word stock despite some shared Slavic roots

## Summary of Diagnostic Features for Czech Identification

### Near-Certain Czech Markers (any one is sufficient)
1. **r** (r with hacek) - exists ONLY in Czech
2. **u** (u with ring above) - exists ONLY in Czech
3. **e** (e with hacek) used as a true diacritical vowel triggering palatalization

### Strong Czech Indicators (combination needed)
4. Slavic-origin month names (leden, unor, brezen, etc.)
5. Word "protoze" (because)
6. Diphthong "ou" in native words
7. High frequency of hacek characters combined with "y"
8. Function words: "je", "jsou", "jeste", "take", "pouze", "ovsem"

### Ruling Out Confusables
- Presence of o (circumflex o), l (acute l), r (acute r) -> Slovak, NOT Czech
- Presence of ogonek (a, e), l (stroke l), kreska-only consonants -> Polish, NOT Czech
- Presence of dz (stroke d) with limited hacek set -> Croatian, NOT Czech
- Only c, s, z haceks with dual number forms -> Slovenian, NOT Czech

These diagnostic features, used in combination, allow reliable separation of Czech from all its potential confusables in text-based language identification.
