# Czech vs. Other Languages: Differential Diagnostics

## Czech vs. Slovak (CRITICAL CONFUSABLE PAIR)

Czech and Slovak are the most closely related pair of Slavic languages and are largely mutually intelligible. They share the same language subgroup (West Slavic, Czech-Slovak branch), similar grammar, and a great deal of vocabulary. During the Czechoslovak era (1918-1993), speakers of both languages were regularly exposed to each other through media, education, and daily life. Distinguishing Czech from Slovak is one of the most challenging tasks in Slavic language identification and requires attention to specific orthographic, phonological, and lexical markers.

### Orthographic Differences (Most Reliable for Text-Based ID)

#### The ř character (STRONGEST SINGLE MARKER)
The letter **ř** (r with háček) exists ONLY in Czech. Slovak does not have this character or the sound it represents. Any text containing recurring ř is almost certainly Czech (a single ř may be a Czech name quoted inside Slovak text). Common Czech words with ř include:
- tři (three), čtyři (four), říjen (October)
- řeka (river), moře (sea), dveře (door)
- potřeba (need), přítel (friend), příklad (example)
- příkaz (command), přes (across), veřejný (public)
- Frequency: ř makes up roughly 1% of all letters in Czech text.

#### The ů character (STRONG MARKER)
Czech uses **ů** (u with ring above/kroužek) for the long /uː/ sound in non-initial positions. Slovak does not use this character. Slovak often has **ô** (o with circumflex/vokáň, the diphthong /uo/) or plain **o** in the corresponding words, which go back to the same Proto-Slavic source.
- Czech: dům (house) vs. Slovak: dom
- Czech: kůže (skin) vs. Slovak: koža
- Czech: důvod (reason) vs. Slovak: dôvod
- Czech: původ (origin) vs. Slovak: pôvod
- Czech: kůň (horse) vs. Slovak: kôň

#### The ě character (STRONG MARKER)
Czech uses **ě** (e with háček) to represent /jɛ/ or to mark palatalization of d, t, n. Slovak does not have this character; the Slovak cognates are written with plain e (or ie).
- Czech: město (city) vs. Slovak: mesto
- Czech: děti (children) vs. Slovak: deti; Czech: větší (bigger) vs. Slovak: väčší; Czech: nejlepší (best) vs. Slovak: najlepší
- Czech: pěkně (nicely) vs. Slovak: pekne; Czech: věc (thing) vs. Slovak: vec
- Very frequent Czech words with ě: mě, něco, není, ještě, děti, věc

Note: Because ě is simply absent from Slovak orthography, recurring ě is a near-certain Czech marker. With diacritics stripped, Czech "mesto" and Slovak "mesto" become identical and this cue is lost.

#### Slovak ô (NOT in Czech)
Slovak uses **ô** (o with circumflex) which does not exist in Czech. This is a strong reverse marker - its presence indicates Slovak, not Czech.
- Slovak: kôň (horse), stôl (table), môže (can), vôbec (at all) vs. Czech: kůň, stůl, může, vůbec

#### Slovak ä, ľ, ĺ, ŕ, ô (Slovak-only diacritics)
Slovak has several diacritical characters not found in Czech:
- **ä** (a with umlaut/dve bodky) - päť (five), opäť (again), väčší (bigger); does not exist in Czech
- **ľ** (l with caron/mäkčeň) - soft l, as in ľudia (people), pokiaľ (if/as far as); not in Czech
- **ĺ** (l with acute) - syllabic long l, as in vĺča (wolf cub); not in Czech
- **ŕ** (r with acute) - syllabic long r, as in vŕba (willow) (not to be confused with Czech ř with háček!)
- **ô** (o with circumflex) - as discussed above

The recurring presence of any of these characters rules out Czech.

### Phonological/Orthographic Pattern Differences

#### Czech ou vs. Slovak ú
The Czech diphthong "ou" frequently corresponds to Slovak long "ú":
- Czech: mouka (flour) vs. Slovak: múka
- Czech: louka (meadow) vs. Slovak: lúka
- Czech: koupit (to buy) vs. Slovak: kúpiť
- Czech: jsou (they are) vs. Slovak: sú
- Czech 3pl -ou (budou, nesou) vs. Slovak -ú (budú, nesú)

#### Prefix vy-/vý- (shared, not diagnostic)
Both languages use the prefix vy-/vý- (Czech vypadat, výsledek; Slovak vyzerať, výsledok), so the prefix itself does not discriminate. The words built on it, however, often differ:
- Czech: vzduch (air) vs. Slovak: vzduch (same here)
- Czech: výsledek vs. Slovak: výsledok (Czech -ek vs. Slovak -ok)

#### Consonant and Vowel Group Differences
- Czech frequently uses "stř" clusters: stromy (trees, shared), střední (middle; Slovak stredný)
- Slovak "ia", "ie", "iu" diphthongs are absent from Czech: Slovak "biely" (white) vs. Czech "bílý"; Slovak "miesto" vs. Czech "místo"; Slovak "kvietok" vs. Czech "kvítek"
- Slovak infinitives end in -ť (byť, robiť); Czech in -t (být, dělat)
- Slovak "dz/dž" in native words (medzi, cudzí) vs. Czech "z/c" (mezi, cizí)

### Grammatical Differences

- Czech has more complex declension patterns with more paradigm types
- Czech preserves the vocative case more robustly than Slovak
- Some case endings differ: Czech masculine/neuter instrumental plural "-y"/"-i" (hrady, městy, muži) vs. Slovak "-mi"/"-ami" (hradmi, mestami, mužmi); Czech instrumental singular "-em" vs. Slovak "-om" (s přítelem / s priateľom); Czech genitive plural "-ů" vs. Slovak "-ov" (studentů / študentov)
- Czech feminine adjective gen./dat./loc. singular "-é" vs. Slovak "-ej" (české republiky / slovenskej republiky)
- Czech verbal nouns in "-ní/-tí" vs. Slovak "-nie/-tie" (řešení / riešenie)
- Present 1st person singular: Czech often "-u/-i" (píšu, chci, můžu) vs. Slovak always "-m" (píšem, chcem, môžem)
- Reflexive particle: Czech "se" vs. Slovak "sa"

## Czech vs. Polish

Czech and Polish are both West Slavic languages but belong to different sub-branches (Czech-Slovak vs. Lechitic). While they share many structural features, their orthographic systems are quite different, making text-based distinction relatively straightforward.

### Orthographic System Differences
- **Czech uses háček (caron)**: č, š, ž, ř, ď, ť, ň
- **Polish uses kreska (acute) and other marks**: ś, ź, ż (dot), ć, ń, ł (stroke), ą, ę (ogonek), ó
- Polish uses **ogonek** (ą, ę) which does not exist in Czech
- Polish uses **kreska** on consonants (ś, ć, ź, ń) where Czech uses háček
- Polish uses **ł** (l with stroke) for /w/; Czech does not have this character
- Czech **ř** does not exist in Polish (Polish writes the cognate sound as the digraph "rz")
- Czech **ě** does not exist in Polish
- Czech **ů** does not exist in Polish
- Polish **ó** exists but means /u/; Czech ó is a rare long /oː/. Czech length marks á, é, í, ú, ý do not exist in Polish

### Common Distinguishing Features
- Polish uses digraphs extensively: sz, cz, rz, ch, dz, dź, dż. Czech avoids digraphs (except ch) in favor of single diacritical characters.
- Polish has nasal vowels (ą, ę); Czech does not.
- Polish word-final devoicing is reflected in some spellings; Czech maintains voicing distinction.
- Different vocabulary despite shared roots: Czech "vzduch" (air) vs. Polish "powietrze"; Czech "vlak" (train) vs. Polish "pociąg"

## Czech vs. Croatian/Serbian

Czech and Croatian/Serbian (South Slavic) are more distantly related. Croatian uses a Latin-script orthography that shares some diacritical marks with Czech (č, š, ž) but lacks Czech ř, ě, ů, ď, ť, ň and all length marks. Croatian also has **đ** (d with stroke) and **ć** (c with acute), which Czech does not have.

### Key Differences
- Croatian lacks háček on d, t, n (it writes the palatal sounds as đ/dž, ć and nj)
- Croatian has **đ** (d with stroke) and **ć**; Czech does not
- Croatian does not use y; Czech uses y and ý extensively
- Croatian has no vowel length marks (á, é, í, ú, ý) in normal writing
- Different vocabulary and grammatical structures as South vs. West Slavic
- Croatian does not have ř or ů (ring)

## Czech vs. Slovenian

Slovenian is a South Slavic language that uses fewer diacritical marks than Czech. Slovenian uses č, š, ž (same háček characters as Czech) but lacks ř, ě, ů, ď, ť, ň and the length marks. Slovenian preserves the dual number, which Czech has lost.

### Key Differences
- Slovenian has only three háček characters: č, š, ž
- Slovenian does not have ř, ě, ů (ring), the palatal ď, ť, ň, or á/é/í/ó/ú/ý in standard spelling
- Slovenian has a dual number for 2 items; Czech does not
- Different word stock despite some shared Slavic roots

## Summary of Diagnostic Features for Czech Identification

### Near-Certain Czech Markers (any one, recurring, is sufficient)
1. **ř** (r with háček) - exists ONLY in Czech
2. **ů** (u with ring above) - exists ONLY in Czech
3. **ě** (e with háček) - absent from Slovak, Polish and South Slavic orthographies

### Strong Czech Indicators (combination needed)
4. Slavic-origin month names (leden, únor, březen, etc.)
5. Word "protože" (because)
6. Diphthong "ou" in native words and endings (jsou, budou, mouka)
7. High frequency of háček characters combined with "y"/"ý"
8. Function words: "je", "jsou", "ještě", "také", "pouze", "ovšem", "jsem", "když"

### Ruling Out Confusables
- Presence of ô (circumflex o), ä, ľ, ĺ, ŕ -> Slovak, NOT Czech
- Presence of ogonek (ą, ę), ł (stroke l), kreska-only consonants (ś, ć, ź, ń) -> Polish, NOT Czech
- Presence of đ (stroke d) or ć with limited háček set and no length marks -> Croatian, NOT Czech
- Only č, š, ž háčeks with dual number forms -> Slovenian, NOT Czech

### Diacritic-free text
If a text has had all diacritics stripped (plain ASCII, e.g. "prilis zlutoucky kun" for "příliš žluťoučký kůň"), the letter markers above disappear. Rely instead on function words (jsem, jsou, se, jako, kdyz, protoze, nebo, neni) versus Slovak (som, su, sa, ako, ked, pretoze, alebo, nie je), on endings (Czech -ou, -em, genitive plural -u from -ů vs. Slovak -u from -ú, -om, -ov), and on month names.

These diagnostic features, used in combination, allow reliable separation of Czech from all its potential confusables in text-based language identification.
