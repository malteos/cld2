# Czech Grammar

## Morphological Type

Czech is a fusional (inflecting) language with a rich morphological system. Grammatical information such as case, number, gender, person, tense, aspect, and mood is encoded primarily through suffixes and stem alternations, with a single suffix often carrying multiple grammatical categories simultaneously. Czech morphology is among the most complex of the modern Slavic languages, with numerous declension and conjugation paradigms and extensive allomorphic variation.

## Phonology

### Vowels

Czech has 10 vowel phonemes: 5 short vowels and their 5 long counterparts. Vowel length is phonemic and is marked orthographically with an acute accent (carka):

- Short: a, e, i/y, o, u
- Long: a (aa), e (ee), i/y (ii/yy), o (oo), u/u (uu)

The letter e (e with hacek) represents a special vowel that historically derives from a iotated e and triggers palatalization of the preceding consonant (de -> /dje/, te -> /tje/, ne -> /nje/).

The letter u (u with ring above) represents a long /uu/ that developed historically from a diphthong. It occurs only in non-initial positions; word-initially, long u is written u (u with acute).

### Consonants

Czech has approximately 25 consonant phonemes. The most notable feature is the phoneme /r/ (written r), which is a raised alveolar trill unique to Czech among the world's languages. It is often described as a simultaneous trill and fricative. Other notable consonant features include:

- A full set of palatalized consonants: d, t, n (written with hacek)
- The hacek series: c (/ts/), s (/sh/), z (/zh/), r (/rzh/)
- Syllabic consonants: both /r/ and /l/ can function as syllable nuclei, allowing words with no vowels such as "krk" (neck), "vlk" (wolf), "prst" (finger), "smrt" (death), and the famous tongue-twister "Strc prst skrz krk" (Stick a finger through the throat)

### Stress

Word stress in Czech is fixed on the first syllable and is not phonemic. Monosyllabic prepositions attract stress from the following word, forming a single prosodic unit (e.g., "na stole" is stressed on "na").

## Noun System

### Gender

Czech has three grammatical genders: masculine, feminine, and neuter. The masculine gender is further subdivided into animate and inanimate categories, which differ in their declension patterns (most notably in the accusative and nominative plural). This gives Czech effectively four declension genders.

### Number

Czech distinguishes singular and plural. There are vestiges of the old dual number in a few words (e.g., body forms for paired body parts like "ruce" - hands, "oci" - eyes).

### Cases

Czech has seven grammatical cases, each with distinct morphological forms and syntactic functions:

1. **Nominativ (Nominative)**: Subject of the sentence. "Student cte knihu." (The student reads a book.)
2. **Genitiv (Genitive)**: Possession, partitive, negation, after certain prepositions (z, do, od, bez, u). "kniha studenta" (the student's book)
3. **Dativ (Dative)**: Indirect object, beneficiary. "Dal knihu studentovi." (He gave the book to the student.)
4. **Akuzativ (Accusative)**: Direct object, direction toward. "Vidim studenta." (I see the student.)
5. **Vokativ (Vocative)**: Direct address. "Studente!" (Student!)
6. **Lokal (Locative/Prepositional)**: Location, always used with a preposition (v, na, o, po, pri). "o studentovi" (about the student)
7. **Instrumental**: Means, instrument, accompaniment, after prepositions (s, pred, za, nad, pod, mezi). "se studentem" (with the student)

### Declension Paradigms

Czech has numerous declension paradigms depending on gender, animacy, and the phonological shape of the stem. The traditional grammar recognizes paradigm types named after prototypical nouns:

- Masculine animate: "pan" (gentleman), "muz" (man), "predseda" (chairman), "soudce" (judge)
- Masculine inanimate: "hrad" (castle), "stroj" (machine)
- Feminine: "zena" (woman), "ruze" (rose), "pisen" (song), "kost" (bone)
- Neuter: "mesto" (city), "more" (sea), "kure" (chicken), "staveni" (building)

Adjectives also decline for case, number, and gender, agreeing with the noun they modify. There are hard and soft adjective paradigms.

## Verb System

### Aspect

The most fundamental category of the Czech verb system is aspect. Nearly every Czech verb belongs to an aspectual pair:

- **Imperfective (nedokonavy vid)**: Expresses ongoing, repeated, or habitual actions. "psat" (to write, imperfective)
- **Perfective (dokonavy vid)**: Expresses completed, single, or bounded actions. "napsat" (to write/to have written, perfective)

Aspectual pairs are formed through prefixation (psat -> napsat), suffixation (koupit -> kupovat), or suppletion (vzit -> birat). The choice of aspect interacts with tense to produce the full range of temporal meanings.

### Tense

Czech has three tenses:

- **Present (pritomny cas)**: Formed by conjugation endings added to the present stem. Used with imperfective verbs for ongoing present actions. Perfective verbs in the present tense form express future meaning.
- **Past (minuly cas)**: Formed with the l-participle plus auxiliary "byt" (to be) in the present tense (the auxiliary is dropped in the 3rd person). "Psal jsem." (I was writing/wrote.) "Psal." (He was writing/wrote.)
- **Future (budouci cas)**: For imperfective verbs, formed with the auxiliary "budu" (I will be) plus the infinitive. "Budu psat." (I will write/be writing.) For perfective verbs, the morphological present tense expresses future meaning. "Napisu." (I will write.)

### Mood

- **Indicative**: The default mood for statements of fact.
- **Conditional (podminovaci zpusob)**: Formed with the particle "by" (which conjugates: bych, bys, by, bychom, byste, by) plus the l-participle. "Psal bych." (I would write.)
- **Imperative (rozkazovaci zpusob)**: Formed from the present stem with special endings. "Pis!" (Write!)

### Conjugation

Czech verbs are conjugated for person (1st, 2nd, 3rd) and number (singular, plural). There are several conjugation classes based on the present tense stem vowel pattern.

### Verbal Nouns and Participles

Czech has several non-finite verb forms: the infinitive (ending in -t or -ci), the l-participle (used in past tense and conditional), the passive participle (ending in -n/-t), and present and past transgressive forms (adverbial participles, now largely archaic in everyday speech).

## Syntax

### Word Order

Czech has basic SVO (Subject-Verb-Object) word order, but this order is very flexible due to the case system, which marks grammatical relations morphologically rather than positionally. Word order in Czech is governed primarily by information structure (topic-comment or theme-rheme articulation):

- Known or given information (topic/theme) tends to appear at the beginning of the sentence.
- New or focused information (comment/rheme) tends to appear at the end.
- Contrastive focus can be placed sentence-initially.

This means that all permutations of subject, verb, and object are grammatically possible, with each conveying a different information-structural nuance:

- "Jan cte knihu." (Jan reads a book. - neutral)
- "Knihu cte Jan." (It's Jan who reads the book. - focus on Jan)
- "Jan knihu cte." (Jan does read the book. - emphasis on the action)

### Clitic Placement

Czech has a strict system of clitic placement. Clitic pronouns, auxiliary verbs, and particles must appear in the second position (Wackernagel's position) in the clause. The ordering of clitics within the clitic cluster is also fixed:

1. Auxiliary "by" (conditional)
2. Auxiliary of "byt" (past tense: jsem, jsi, jsme, jste)
3. Reflexive "se" / "si"
4. Dative clitic pronouns (mi, ti, mu, ji, nam, vam, jim)
5. Accusative clitic pronouns (me, te, ho/jej, ji, nas, vas, je)

Example: "Ja bych se ti o tom zminil." (I would have mentioned it to you.)

### Pro-Drop

Czech is a pro-drop (null-subject) language. Because verb conjugation marks person and number, subject pronouns can be and typically are omitted unless needed for emphasis or contrast:

- "Ctu knihu." (I read a book.) - subject pronoun omitted
- "Ja ctu knihu." (I read a book.) - emphatic, contrastive

### Negation

Negation in Czech is formed by prefixing "ne-" directly to the verb: "nevidim" (I don't see), "nepisu" (I don't write). Czech uses negative concord (multiple negation), so negative indefinite pronouns co-occur with the negated verb: "Nikdo nic nevidi." (Nobody sees anything. - literally "Nobody nothing not-sees.")

### Reflexive Constructions

The reflexive particles "se" (accusative) and "si" (dative) are extensively used in Czech for true reflexive actions, reciprocal actions, passive and impersonal constructions, and as inherent parts of many verbs (reflexiva tantum): "myt se" (to wash oneself), "bat se" (to fear), "divat se" (to watch).
