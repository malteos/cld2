# Modern Greek -- Identification Characteristics

## Greek Alphabet

Modern Greek uses the Greek alphabet, consisting of 24 letters. This script is used almost exclusively for the Greek language (with minor historical exceptions), making script-based identification highly reliable.

### Uppercase Letters
Α Β Γ Δ Ε Ζ Η Θ Ι Κ Λ Μ Ν Ξ Ο Π Ρ Σ Τ Υ Φ Χ Ψ Ω

### Lowercase Letters
α β γ δ ε ζ η θ ι κ λ μ ν ξ ο π ρ σ/ς τ υ φ χ ψ ω

Note: The letter sigma has two lowercase forms -- σ (medial) and ς (final, used only at the end of a word). This alternation is a strong identification signal.

## Diacritics

Modern Greek uses the **monotonic system** (adopted officially in 1982), which employs a single diacritical mark:

- **Tonos (΄):** An acute-style accent mark placed over the stressed vowel of every polysyllabic word. Examples: ά, έ, ή, ί, ό, ύ, ώ. The tonos also appears on the diaeresis combinations: ϊ, ϋ (and with accent: ΐ, ΰ).

The older **polytonic system** used three accent marks (acute, grave, circumflex), two breathing marks (smooth, rough), the iota subscript, and the diaeresis, but this system is no longer standard in everyday writing and appears mainly in scholarly, ecclesiastical, or historical texts.

## Character Frequency Patterns

The most frequent letters in Modern Greek text (approximate order):

1. α (alpha) -- ~10%
2. ο (omicron) -- ~7.5%
3. ι (iota) -- ~7.5%
4. ε (epsilon) -- ~7%
5. τ (tau) -- ~7%
6. ν (nu) -- ~6%
7. σ/ς (sigma) -- ~6%
8. η (eta) -- ~5%
9. ρ (rho) -- ~4%
10. π (pi) -- ~3.5%

The high frequency of vowels (α, ο, ι, ε, η, υ, ω together account for approximately 45% of text) reflects the open syllable structure of Greek.

## Morphological Identification Patterns

### Noun Endings

Greek nouns follow predictable declension patterns that serve as strong identification features:

- **Masculine:** -ος, -ης, -ας, -έας (nom sg); -ου, -η, -α (gen sg); -οι, -ες (nom pl)
- **Feminine:** -α, -η, -ος (nom sg); -ας, -ης (gen sg); -ες, -εις (nom pl)
- **Neuter:** -ο, -ι, -μα, -ος (nom sg); -ου, -ιού, -ματος (gen sg); -α, -ια, -ματα (nom pl)

### Verb Endings

Active present tense endings are highly regular and distinctive:

- 1sg: **-ω** (γράφω "I write")
- 2sg: **-εις** (γράφεις "you write")
- 3sg: **-ει** (γράφει "he/she writes")
- 1pl: **-ουμε** (γράφουμε "we write")
- 2pl: **-ετε** (γράφετε "you write")
- 3pl: **-ουν(ε)** (γράφουν "they write")

Middle-passive endings: -ομαι, -εσαι, -εται, -όμαστε, -εστε/-όσαστε, -ονται

### Diminutive Suffixes

Greek makes extensive use of diminutive suffixes, which are characteristic identification features:

- **-άκι** (neuter): σπίτι > σπιτάκι (little house), παιδί > παιδάκι (little child)
- **-ούλα** (feminine): μάνα > μανούλα (mommy), πόρτα > πορτούλα (little door)
- **-ούλης** (masculine): κλέφτης > κλεφτούλης (little thief)
- **-ίτσα** (feminine): αδερφή > αδερφίτσα (little sister)
- **-ούδι** (neuter): αρνί > αρνούδι (little lamb)

### Augment

Past tense verbs often take an augment prefix **ε-** when the stress falls on the augment: γράφω > **έ**γραψα (I wrote), λέω > **εί**πα (I said). This prefix is a strong indicator of Greek.

### Contracted Preposition + Article Forms

The preposition σε (in/at/to) contracts with the definite article to produce distinctive forms: στον, στη(ν), στο, στους, στις, στα. These fused forms are highly frequent and diagnostic for Greek text.

## Punctuation

Modern Greek uses standard European punctuation with two notable exceptions:

- The **semicolon (;)** functions as a **question mark** in Greek.
- The **raised dot (·)** or **ano teleia** functions as a **semicolon** in Greek.

These punctuation conventions can aid language identification, though they are not always reliable in digital text where standard ASCII punctuation may be substituted.
