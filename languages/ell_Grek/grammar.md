# Modern Greek Grammar

## Word Order

Modern Greek has a basic **SVO** (Subject-Verb-Object) word order, but it is relatively free due to its rich inflectional morphology. The case system on nouns and the agreement system on verbs allow speakers to rearrange constituents for pragmatic purposes such as topicalization, focus, and emphasis. VSO and OVS orders are common in spoken Greek. In questions, VSO order is typical. Clitic pronouns are placed before the verb in declarative sentences and after the verb in imperatives.

Examples of word order flexibility:
- SVO: Ο Γιάννης διαβάζει το βιβλίο (John reads the book)
- VSO: Διαβάζει ο Γιάννης το βιβλίο
- OVS: Το βιβλίο το διαβάζει ο Γιάννης

## Phonology

### Vowels

Modern Greek has a simple **five-vowel system**: /a/, /e/, /i/, /o/, /u/. This represents a significant simplification from Ancient Greek, which had a complex system of long and short vowels, diphthongs, and the front rounded vowel /y/. Several historical spellings now represent the same sound:

- /i/: ι, η, υ, ει, οι, υι
- /e/: ε, αι
- /o/: ο, ω
- /a/: α
- /u/: ου

### Consonants

Modern Greek has a rich consonant inventory that includes several distinctive features:

- **Voiced and voiceless fricatives:** /f, v, θ, ð, s, z, x, ɣ/ -- the fricative pairs /θ/-/ð/ (written θ/δ) and /x/-/ɣ/ (written χ/γ before back vowels) are characteristic of Greek.
- **Voiceless stops:** /p, t, k/ -- unaspirated (unlike English).
- **Voiced stops:** /b, d, g/ -- written as digraphs μπ, ντ, γκ respectively.
- **Nasals:** /m, n/ and the velar nasal /ŋ/ (allophone before velars).
- **Liquids:** /l, r/ -- the rhotic /r/ is typically an alveolar tap or trill.
- **Affricate:** /ts/ (written τσ) and /dz/ (written τζ).

### Consonant Clusters

Greek permits a wide range of consonant clusters, particularly in word-initial position. Common initial clusters include /ps/ (ψ), /ks/ (ξ), /pn/ (πν-), /mn/ (μν-), /ft/ (φτ-), /xt/ (χτ-), and /str/ (στρ-). These clusters are often simplified in rapid speech.

### Stress

Stress in Modern Greek is dynamic (non-fixed) and phonemic -- it can distinguish words. Stress falls on one of the last three syllables of a word and is marked in writing with the tonos accent (΄). Every polysyllabic word carries an accent mark. Stress placement can shift during inflection (e.g., ο άνθρωπος "the man" vs. του ανθρώπου "of the man").

## Morphology

Modern Greek is a **fusional** language with rich inflectional morphology on both nouns and verbs.

### Nouns

Nouns inflect for:

- **Gender:** Three genders -- masculine (αρσενικό), feminine (θηλυκό), neuter (ουδέτερο). Gender is largely grammatical rather than semantic and must be learned with each noun.
- **Number:** Singular (ενικός) and plural (πληθυντικός).
- **Case:** Four cases:
  - **Nominative** (ονομαστική): subject of the sentence
  - **Accusative** (αιτιατική): direct object, object of most prepositions
  - **Genitive** (γενική): possession, indirect object, object of some prepositions
  - **Vocative** (κλητική): direct address

The dative case, present in Ancient Greek, has been lost in Modern Greek. Its functions have been taken over by the genitive (for indirect objects) and prepositional phrases with σε + accusative.

#### Noun Declension Patterns

Masculine nouns commonly end in -ος, -ης, -ας, -έας in the nominative singular:
- ο δρόμος (road): δρόμου, δρόμο, δρόμε / δρόμοι, δρόμων, δρόμους
- ο μαθητής (student): μαθητή, μαθητή, μαθητή / μαθητές, μαθητών, μαθητές

Feminine nouns commonly end in -α, -η, -ος in the nominative singular:
- η γλώσσα (language): γλώσσας, γλώσσα / γλώσσες, γλωσσών, γλώσσες
- η τάξη (class): τάξης, τάξη / τάξεις, τάξεων, τάξεις

Neuter nouns commonly end in -ο, -ι, -μα, -ος in the nominative singular:
- το βιβλίο (book): βιβλίου, βιβλίο / βιβλία, βιβλίων, βιβλία
- το μάθημα (lesson): μαθήματος, μάθημα / μαθήματα, μαθημάτων, μαθήματα

### Articles

Modern Greek has both **definite** and **indefinite** articles that agree with the noun in gender, number, and case.

**Definite articles:**

|        | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
|--------|-----------|----------|--------|
| Nom sg | ο         | η        | το     |
| Gen sg | του       | της      | του    |
| Acc sg | τον/το    | τη(ν)   | το     |
| Nom pl | οι        | οι       | τα     |
| Gen pl | των       | των      | των    |
| Acc pl | τους      | τις      | τα     |

**Indefinite articles** (singular only):

|        | Masculine | Feminine | Neuter |
|--------|-----------|----------|--------|
| Nom    | ένας      | μία/μια  | ένα    |
| Gen    | ενός      | μιας/μίας| ενός   |
| Acc    | έναν/ένα  | μία/μια  | ένα    |

### Adjectives

Adjectives agree with their noun in gender, number, and case. They typically precede the noun and are placed between the article and the noun: ο καλός μαθητής (the good student). The most common adjective declension follows the -ος, -η/-α, -ο pattern (e.g., καλός, καλή, καλό). Comparative forms use πιο + adjective (analytic) or the suffix -τερος (synthetic). Superlative forms use ο πιο + adjective or the suffix -τατος.

### Verbs

The Modern Greek verb system is organized primarily around the category of **aspect** rather than tense, distinguishing between:

- **Imperfective (ατελές):** Ongoing, habitual, or repeated action. Uses the present stem.
- **Perfective (τελειωμένο/στιγμιαίο):** Completed or punctual action. Uses the aorist/perfective stem.

#### Tense-Aspect Forms

The main tense-aspect combinations are:

**Imperfective stem:**
- Present: γράφω (I write / I am writing)
- Imperfect: έγραφα (I was writing / I used to write)

**Perfective stem:**
- Simple past (Aorist): έγραψα (I wrote)
- Dependent (Subjunctive): να γράψω (that I write -- used for future, subjunctive, etc.)

**Perfect forms (periphrastic):**
- Present perfect: έχω γράψει (I have written)
- Past perfect: είχα γράψει (I had written)
- Future perfect: θα έχω γράψει (I will have written)

#### Mood and Modality

- **Indicative:** The default mood for statements of fact.
- **Subjunctive:** Formed with the particle **να** + verb. Used for wishes, purpose clauses, after certain verbs (θέλω να -- I want to), and as a general non-finite complement (Greek lacks an infinitive).
- **Imperative:** Direct commands. Has separate perfective and imperfective forms: γράψε! (write! -- once) vs. γράφε! (write! -- keep writing).
- **Future:** Formed with the particle **θα** + verb: θα γράψω (I will write -- perfective), θα γράφω (I will be writing -- imperfective).
- **Conditional:** θα + imperfect: θα έγραφα (I would write).

#### Voice

Modern Greek has two voices:

- **Active (ενεργητική):** ο Γιάννης πλένει το αυτοκίνητο (John washes the car)
- **Middle-Passive (μεσοπαθητική):** το αυτοκίνητο πλένεται (the car is being washed / washes itself)

The middle-passive has both passive meaning and reflexive/middle meaning. **Deponent verbs** (αποθετικά ρήματα) appear only in middle-passive form but have active meaning: έρχομαι (I come), κοιμάμαι (I sleep), σκέφτομαι (I think).

#### Verb Conjugation Example: γράφω (to write)

Present active: γράφω, γράφεις, γράφει, γράφουμε, γράφετε, γράφουν(ε)
Past (aorist): έγραψα, έγραψες, έγραψε, γράψαμε, γράψατε, έγραψαν

## Syntax

### Clitic Pronouns

Weak (clitic) object pronouns are placed **before** the verb in declarative sentences and **after** the verb in imperatives. When both a direct and an indirect object clitic appear, the indirect (genitive) precedes the direct (accusative): Μου το έδωσε (He gave it to me -- literally "Me it he-gave"). Clitic doubling is common in spoken Greek: Τον βλέπω τον Γιάννη (I see John -- literally "Him I-see the John").

### Subordination

Greek relies heavily on finite subordinate clauses introduced by particles and conjunctions rather than infinitival or participial constructions:

- **να** + subjunctive replaces the ancient infinitive: Θέλω να φύγω (I want to leave -- literally "I want that I leave")
- **ότι/πως** introduces declarative complement clauses: Ξέρω ότι είναι εδώ (I know that he is here)
- **αν** introduces conditional clauses: Αν θέλεις, έλα (If you want, come)
- **όταν** introduces temporal clauses: Όταν φύγει, θα σε πάρω (When he leaves, I will call you)

### Negation

Negation uses **δε(ν)** for indicative and **μη(ν)** for subjunctive/imperative:
- Δεν τρώω κρέας (I do not eat meat)
- Μη φεύγεις! (Do not leave!)

### Copula and Existential

The verb **είμαι** (I am) functions as the copula. The existential construction uses **υπάρχει/υπάρχουν** (there is/there are).
