# Modern Greek -- Differences from Related Languages and Varieties

## Modern Greek vs. Ancient Greek

Ancient Greek is the most important comparison for Modern Greek, as the two share the same script and much of the same vocabulary. However, they differ substantially in phonology, morphology, and syntax.

### Phonological Differences

- **Vowel system:** Ancient Greek had a complex vowel system with short/long distinctions, diphthongs, and the front rounded vowel /y/. Modern Greek collapsed this into five simple vowels /a, e, i, o, u/ with no length distinction. Multiple ancient vowels and diphthongs merged into /i/ (iotacism): η, υ, ει, οι, υι all represent /i/ in Modern Greek.
- **Consonants:** Ancient Greek had aspirated stops (φ /pʰ/, θ /tʰ/, χ /kʰ/) which became fricatives in Modern Greek (φ /f/, θ /θ/, χ /x/). The letter β changed from /b/ to /v/, δ from /d/ to /ð/, and γ from /g/ to /ɣ/ (or /j/ before front vowels). Voiced stops /b, d, g/ are now written with digraphs μπ, ντ, γκ.
- **Pitch accent vs. stress accent:** Ancient Greek had a melodic pitch accent; Modern Greek has a dynamic stress accent.

### Morphological Differences

- **Case system:** Ancient Greek had five cases (nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, vocative). Modern Greek has lost the **dative case**, replacing it with genitive clitic pronouns for indirect objects and σε + accusative for other dative functions.
- **Verb system:** Ancient Greek had infinitives, participles in multiple tenses and voices, the optative mood, the dual number, and synthetic perfect/pluperfect forms. Modern Greek has lost the infinitive entirely (replaced by να + subjunctive), reduced participial forms, lost the optative, lost the dual, and uses periphrastic perfect constructions (έχω + invariable past participle).
- **Third declension:** The complex third declension of Ancient Greek has been largely regularized or absorbed into other declension patterns in Modern Greek.

### Syntactic Differences

- Ancient Greek used infinitival constructions extensively; Modern Greek uses finite clauses with να.
- Modern Greek has developed obligatory use of articles in contexts where Ancient Greek did not require them.
- Clitic doubling is a Modern Greek innovation absent from Ancient Greek.

### Diagnostic Features for Identification

The most reliable ways to distinguish Modern from Ancient Greek text include:
- **Monotonic accent** (single accent mark) vs. polytonic (multiple diacritics, breathing marks, iota subscript)
- Modern function words: θα (future), να (subjunctive), δεν/μην (negation), σε (preposition)
- Contracted preposition-article forms: στο, στη, στον
- Absence of infinitive forms and dative case endings
- Modern vocabulary and loanwords (from Turkish, Italian, French, English)

## Modern Greek vs. Cypriot Greek

Cypriot Greek (Κυπριακά) is the principal dialect of Greek spoken in Cyprus. While not a separate language, it differs from Standard Modern Greek in ways relevant to language identification.

### Phonological Differences

- Cypriot Greek preserves geminate (double) consonants, which have been lost in Standard Greek: Cypriot /kkos/ vs. Standard /kos/.
- Palatalization of velars before front vowels is more extensive: /k/ > /tʃ/, /g/ > /dʒ/ (e.g., και pronounced /tʃe/).
- The sibilant /ʃ/ exists in Cypriot but not in Standard Greek.
- Post-alveolar affricates /tʃ/ and /dʒ/ are present in Cypriot Greek.

### Morphological and Lexical Differences

- Different verb forms, especially in the future and imperative (ε-να instead of θα in some constructions).
- Preservation of some archaic forms lost in Standard Greek.
- Distinct lexical items, including borrowings from Turkish, Arabic, French, and English that differ from mainland borrowings: e.g., Cypriot κουμάντο (command, from Italian) vs. Standard εντολή.
- Clitic placement rules differ: Cypriot Greek places clitics after the verb in some contexts where Standard Greek places them before.

### Identification Notes

Written Cypriot Greek is generally identical to Standard Modern Greek, as Standard Greek is used in formal writing, education, and media in Cyprus. Dialectal features appear mainly in informal writing, social media, and dialect literature. When written dialectally, Cypriot Greek may be identified by distinctive spellings reflecting the phonological differences described above.

## Modern Greek vs. Coptic

While Coptic and Greek share a historical connection through the Coptic alphabet (which is derived from the Greek alphabet with additional Demotic Egyptian letters), they are entirely different languages from different language families.

### Script Relationship

- Coptic uses the Greek alphabet supplemented with 6-7 additional letters derived from Demotic Egyptian: ϣ, ϩ, ϫ, ϭ, ϯ, ϥ (and sometimes ⳉ). The presence of these non-Greek characters immediately distinguishes Coptic from Greek text.
- Coptic text that happens to use only standard Greek letters can sometimes superficially resemble Greek, but the word structure, morphology, and vocabulary are completely different.

### Language Family

- Greek belongs to the Indo-European family (Hellenic branch).
- Coptic is the latest stage of the Egyptian language (Afro-Asiatic family).
- They share no genetic relationship; the script similarity is purely due to historical cultural contact.

### Identification

Distinguishing Coptic from Greek is straightforward in practice:
- Coptic-specific letters (ϣ, ϩ, ϫ, ϭ, ϯ, ϥ) are absent in Greek
- Coptic vocabulary, morphology, and syntax are entirely different from Greek
- Coptic has predominantly CVC syllable structure vs. Greek's more open syllables
- Coptic texts often contain Greek loanwords (especially religious terminology), but the surrounding text is clearly non-Greek

## Script-Based Identification

The Greek script is largely unique among modern writing systems. No other major living language uses the Greek alphabet as its primary script. This makes script-based identification of Greek text highly reliable. The main considerations are:

- **Mathematical and scientific notation** uses Greek letters (α, β, γ, δ, π, Σ, Δ, etc.) but this appears as isolated symbols within Latin-script text, not as running Greek prose.
- **Coptic** shares most Greek letters but adds distinctive non-Greek characters.
- **Historical use** of Greek script for other languages (e.g., Bactrian, some Turkish dialects) is not relevant for modern text identification.

The presence of running text in Greek characters, combined with modern function words (και, το, να, δεν, θα, στο) and monotonic accentuation, provides near-certain identification of Modern Greek.
