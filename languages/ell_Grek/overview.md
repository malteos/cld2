# Modern Greek (Ελληνικά)

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Greek / Ελληνικά (Ellinika)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** ell
- **ISO 639-1 code:** el
- **Script:** Greek (Grek)
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Hellenic
- **Native speakers:** Approximately 13 million
- **Total speakers:** Approximately 13.5 million (including L2 speakers)

## Geographic Distribution

Modern Greek is the official language of **Greece** (population ~10.7 million) and **Cyprus** (where it is co-official with Turkish, spoken by ~800,000). Significant Greek-speaking diaspora communities exist in the United States (particularly in New York, Chicago, and Tarpon Springs), Australia (Melbourne has one of the largest Greek populations outside Greece), Canada, Germany, the United Kingdom, South Africa, and throughout Western Europe. Smaller historical communities persist in southern Albania (Northern Epirus), southern Italy (Griko-speaking communities in Calabria and Apulia), Turkey (Istanbul), Egypt (Alexandria), and Georgia.

## Official Status

Modern Greek holds official status in the following contexts:

- **Greece:** Sole official language
- **Cyprus:** Co-official language (alongside Turkish)
- **European Union:** One of 24 official languages
- **Albania:** Recognized minority language in specific municipalities

## Historical Development

The history of the Greek language spans over 3,400 years of continuous documented use, making it one of the longest-attested languages in the world.

### Ancient Greek (c. 1500 BCE -- 300 BCE)

Ancient Greek encompassed multiple dialects, including Attic, Ionic, Doric, and Aeolic. Attic Greek, the dialect of Athens, became the prestige variety and the vehicle for classical literature, philosophy, drama, and historiography. Ancient Greek developed a rich literary tradition that profoundly influenced Western civilization.

### Koine Greek (c. 300 BCE -- 600 CE)

Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Koine ("common") Greek emerged as a lingua franca across the eastern Mediterranean and Near East. Koine was the language of the New Testament and the Septuagint, and it served as the administrative language of the eastern Roman Empire. Koine simplified many features of Classical Attic, reducing the number of vowel distinctions, regularizing verb conjugations, and moving toward analytic constructions.

### Byzantine/Medieval Greek (c. 600 -- 1453 CE)

During the Byzantine Empire, Greek continued to evolve. The spoken language diverged increasingly from the written standard, which attempted to maintain classicizing forms. Medieval Greek saw the loss of the dative case in everyday speech, the development of the modern article system, and the emergence of new verbal constructions that would characterize Modern Greek.

### Early Modern Greek and the Language Question (1453 -- 1976)

After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, Greek persisted under Ottoman rule, maintained by the Orthodox Church and Greek-speaking communities. Following Greek independence in 1821, a fierce debate known as the "Language Question" (Γλωσσικό Ζήτημα) arose between proponents of two competing standards:

- **Katharevousa (Καθαρεύουσα):** A purist, archaizing form that sought to purify the language by restoring classical features. It served as the official state language for much of the 19th and 20th centuries.
- **Demotic (Δημοτική):** The natural spoken language of the people, which had evolved considerably from Ancient Greek.

This diglossia caused significant social and political tension. In 1976, Demotic was officially adopted as the standard language of the Greek state, and in 1982, the monotonic orthographic system replaced the older polytonic system for standard use.

### Modern Standard Greek (1976 -- present)

Modern Standard Greek (Νέα Ελληνικά or Νεοελληνική Κοινή) is based on Demotic but incorporates many learned (Katharevousa) elements, particularly in formal registers, legal language, and scientific terminology. It uses the monotonic accent system with a single accent mark (tonos) to indicate stress. Modern Greek retains the Greek alphabet and maintains strong continuity with its historical forms, though it has undergone significant phonological, morphological, and syntactic changes from the ancient language.
