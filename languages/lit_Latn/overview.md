# Lithuanian (lit_Latn) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Lithuanian (lietuvių kalba)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: lit
- **ISO 639-1 code**: lt
- **Script**: Latin (Latn)
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Baltic > Eastern Baltic
- **Native speakers**: approximately 3 million
- **Total speakers**: approximately 3.5 million (including L2 speakers)

## Classification

Lithuanian belongs to the Eastern Baltic branch of the Baltic language family, which is part of the broader Indo-European language family. Its closest living relative is Latvian, also an Eastern Baltic language. The extinct Old Prussian language belonged to the Western Baltic branch. Together, the Baltic languages form one of the most conservative branches of Indo-European, and Lithuanian in particular is renowned among linguists for preserving numerous archaic features of Proto-Indo-European that have been lost in virtually all other living Indo-European languages.

The relationship between the Baltic and Slavic language families has been a subject of extensive scholarly debate. Some linguists propose a Balto-Slavic intermediate proto-language, while others argue that the similarities between Baltic and Slavic languages result from prolonged geographic contact rather than a shared ancestor distinct from Proto-Indo-European.

## Geographic Distribution

Lithuanian is the official language of the Republic of Lithuania, where it is spoken by the vast majority of the population. Lithuania is a member of the European Union, and Lithuanian is one of the 24 official languages of the EU. The language is also recognized as a minority language in certain regions of neighboring Poland.

Significant Lithuanian-speaking diaspora communities exist in several countries worldwide. The largest communities outside Lithuania are found in:

- **United States**: Historically the largest diaspora, with communities concentrated in Chicago, Cleveland, Pittsburgh, and other cities. Lithuanian immigration to the United States occurred in several waves, notably in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and again after World War II.
- **United Kingdom**: A substantial Lithuanian community has formed since Lithuania's accession to the EU in 2004, making Lithuanian one of the most widely spoken foreign languages in the UK.
- **Ireland**: Similar to the UK, Ireland saw significant Lithuanian immigration after 2004, and Lithuanians represent one of the largest immigrant communities in the country.
- **Germany, Norway, Spain, and other EU countries**: Smaller but notable communities exist throughout Western Europe.
- **Canada, Brazil, Argentina, and Australia**: Historical emigration waves created communities in these countries as well.

## Historical Significance

Lithuanian holds a unique position in Indo-European linguistics due to its exceptional conservatism. The language preserves features of Proto-Indo-European phonology, morphology, and accentuation that have been substantially transformed or lost in other branches of the family. This conservatism was recognized as early as the 19th century by pioneering comparative linguists such as August Schleicher, who famously composed a fable in reconstructed Proto-Indo-European using Lithuanian as a primary reference.

Key archaic features preserved in Lithuanian include the retention of Proto-Indo-European pitch accent (reflected in Lithuanian tonal distinctions), a rich case system with seven cases, and numerous lexical items that closely resemble their reconstructed Proto-Indo-European etyma. For example, Lithuanian words such as "vyras" (man), "avis" (sheep), "sūnus" (son), and "dūmas" (smoke) bear striking resemblance to their Proto-Indo-European reconstructions.

The earliest written records in Lithuanian date from the 16th century. The first known Lithuanian text is a handwritten translation of the Lord's Prayer, the Hail Mary, and the Creed, dating to approximately 1503. The first printed Lithuanian book was the "Catechismusa prasty szadei" (Simple Words of Catechism) by Martynas Mažvydas, published in 1547 in Königsberg.

## Standardization and Modern Usage

Modern Standard Lithuanian is based primarily on the western Aukštaitian (Highland Lithuanian) dialect, specifically the Suvalkija subdialect. The standardization process began in the late 19th century during the Lithuanian national revival, with significant contributions from linguist Jonas Jablonskis, often called the father of modern Lithuanian. The standard language was further codified throughout the 20th century by the State Commission of the Lithuanian Language (Valstybinė lietuvių kalbos komisija), which continues to regulate linguistic norms.

Lithuanian has two main dialectal groups: Aukštaitian (Highland) and Žemaitian (Lowland/Samogitian). The differences between these groups are substantial enough that some scholars have proposed treating Samogitian as a separate language rather than a dialect of Lithuanian.

Today, Lithuanian is used in all domains of public life, including government, education, media, science, and technology. The language has fully adapted to the digital age, with complete support in major operating systems, extensive online content, and active use on social media platforms.
