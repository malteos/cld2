# Dhivehi Script and Language Characteristics

## Thaana Script

The Thaana script (ތާނަ) is the writing system used for Dhivehi. It is unique among the world's scripts in that its consonant letters derive primarily from Arabic-Indic numerals rather than from an earlier alphabetic tradition. Thaana is written from right to left, and vowels are represented by obligatory diacritical marks (fili) placed above or below consonant letters, making it an abugida (alphasyllabary) functionally similar to Arabic script but structurally distinct.

## Vowel Diacritics (Fili)

Vowels in Thaana are written as obligatory diacritical marks above or below consonant letters. Every consonant must carry a vowel diacritic (or a sukun to indicate no vowel). The fili system includes:

| Diacritic | Name | Sound | Position |
|-----------|------|-------|----------|
| َ | abafili | /a/ | above |
| ާ | aabaafili | /aː/ | above |
| ِ | ibifili | /i/ | below |
| ީ | eebeefeeli | /iː/ | below |
| ُ | ubufili | /u/ | above |
| ޫ | ooboofili | /uː/ | above |
| ެ | ebefili | /e/ | above |
| ޭ | eybeyfili | /eː/ | above |
| ޮ | obofili | /o/ | above |
| ޯ | oaboafili | /oː/ | above |
| ް | sukun | (no vowel) | above |

The sukun (ް) marks a consonant with no following vowel, used at the end of syllables and in geminate clusters.

## Writing Direction

Thaana is written exclusively from right to left, consistent with Arabic script. Numerals, however, follow the Western left-to-right convention. When Dhivehi text includes embedded Latin text or numbers, bidirectional text handling is required.

## Common Character Sequences

Certain character sequences appear frequently in Dhivehi text, serving as useful indicators for language identification:

- **ެވެ** (-eve): Common sentence-final copular ending
- **ށެވެ** (-sheve): Another frequent sentence-ending pattern
- **ކުރި** (kuri): Past tense of "to do"
- **ގައި** (gai): Locative postposition, extremely frequent
- **ކަމަށް** (kamah): Complementizer, common in reported speech
- **ތަކެއް** (takeh): Indefinite plural suffix
- **ތައް** (tah): Plural marker for inanimate nouns
- **އެއް** (eh): Indefinite article/numeral "one"
- **ނުވަތަ** (nuvata): Disjunction "or"
- **ވެސް** (ves): Additive particle "also/too"

## Distinguishing Thaana from Arabic Script

Although Thaana is written right-to-left like Arabic, several features readily distinguish the two scripts:

1. **No cursive connection:** Thaana letters do not connect to each other. Each letter stands independently, whereas Arabic letters have initial, medial, final, and isolated forms that connect in a cursive flow.

2. **Unique letter forms:** Many Thaana letters have no visual counterpart in Arabic. Letters such as ށ (shaviyani), ޅ (lhaviyani), ޏ (gnaviyani), ޑ (daviyani), and ޓ (taviyani) are entirely unique to Thaana. These letters, representing sounds specific to Dhivehi phonology, have distinctive shapes not found in any Arabic-derived script.

3. **Mandatory vowel marking:** In Thaana, every consonant must carry a vowel diacritic or sukun. Arabic script typically omits vowel marks (harakat) in everyday writing, adding them only in the Quran, children's books, or ambiguous contexts. The consistent presence of diacritics on every letter is a strong visual indicator of Thaana.

4. **Letter shape simplicity:** Thaana letters are generally simpler in form than Arabic letters. Many Thaana consonants consist of simple geometric shapes (circles, loops, and short strokes), reflecting their origin from numeral forms rather than from a calligraphic tradition.

5. **Dot patterns:** While both scripts use dots to distinguish letters, Thaana's dot usage patterns differ from Arabic. The placement and number of dots follow a different system.

6. **Unicode range:** Thaana occupies a distinct Unicode block (U+0780 to U+07BF), separate from the Arabic block (U+0600 to U+06FF). This provides definitive computational identification.

These features collectively make Thaana one of the most visually distinctive scripts in the world, despite its superficial resemblance to Arabic due to the shared right-to-left directionality and diacritical vowel system.
