# Ilocano (Iloko) Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Ilocano (also spelled Ilokano, Iloko, Iluko)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** ilo
- **Script:** Latin (Latn)
- **Language Family:** Austronesian > Malayo-Polynesian > Philippine > Northern Luzon > Ilocano
- **Speakers:** Approximately 10 million native speakers; up to 2 million additional second-language speakers
- **Vitality:** Not endangered; actively used across multiple domains

## Geographic Distribution

Ilocano is the third most spoken native language in the Philippines, after Tagalog and Cebuano. Its primary homeland is the Ilocos Region in northwestern Luzon, encompassing the provinces of Ilocos Norte, Ilocos Sur, La Union, and Pangasinan (northern part). However, Ilocano speakers have historically migrated throughout northern and central Luzon, making the language a dominant lingua franca across a broad swath of the island.

### Primary Regions in the Philippines

- **Ilocos Region (Region I):** The heartland of the Ilocano people, including the provinces along the western coast of northern Luzon.
- **Cagayan Valley (Region II):** Ilocano settlers have established a strong presence in Cagayan, Isabela, Nueva Vizcaya, and Quirino provinces.
- **Cordillera Administrative Region (CAR):** Used as a trade language and lingua franca among diverse ethnolinguistic groups in the mountainous interior, including Benguet, Mountain Province, and Abra.
- **Central Luzon:** Ilocano communities exist in Tarlac and Nueva Ecija provinces.
- **Mindanao:** Ilocano migrants have settled in various parts of Mindanao, particularly in Cotabato, Davao, and Zamboanga.

### Diaspora Communities

Ilocano is widely spoken in overseas Filipino communities, particularly in:
- **Hawaii, United States:** Ilocanos form the largest Filipino ethnic group in Hawaii, where Ilocano was historically one of the most common non-English languages.
- **California, United States:** Large Ilocano-speaking communities exist in the San Francisco Bay Area, the Central Valley (Stockton, Salinas, Delano), and Los Angeles.
- **Other diaspora locations:** Canada, Saudi Arabia, Hong Kong, Singapore, Italy, and the United Kingdom.

## Sociolinguistic Status

Ilocano functions as a regional lingua franca throughout northern Luzon. It is used in local government, media, education (as a medium of instruction in early grades under the Mother Tongue-Based Multilingual Education policy), religious services, and everyday commerce. Several newspapers, radio stations, and television programs broadcast in Ilocano.

The language has a rich literary tradition dating back to the pre-colonial period, including the epic poem *Biag ni Lam-ang* (Life of Lam-ang), one of the few surviving pre-colonial literary works in Philippine languages. Modern Ilocano literature includes poetry, short stories, novels, and drama.

## Writing System

Ilocano is written using the standard Latin alphabet. Before Spanish colonization, the language was written in a Brahmic-derived script called *kur-itan*, which is no longer in common use. The modern orthography closely follows Spanish-influenced conventions, with predictable spelling-to-pronunciation correspondences. No special diacritical marks are required in standard written Ilocano, although accent marks are occasionally used in dictionaries and linguistic texts to indicate stress patterns.

## Classification Notes

Within the Austronesian language family, Ilocano belongs to the Northern Luzon subgroup of Philippine languages. It is not mutually intelligible with Tagalog, Cebuano, or most other major Philippine languages, though it shares typological features common to the Philippine language type, including a focus/voice system and verb-initial word order.
