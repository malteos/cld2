# Japanese (日本語) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Japanese (日本語, Nihongo)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** jpn
- **ISO 15924 Script Code:** Jpan (composite: Hani + Hira + Kana)
- **Language Family:** Japonic
- **Number of Speakers:** ~125 million native speakers
- **Primary Region:** Japan
- **Official Status:** Official language of Japan (de facto; no constitutionally designated official language)

## Classification

Japanese is classified within the Japonic language family, which also includes the Ryukyuan languages spoken in Okinawa and the surrounding islands. The broader genetic affiliation of Japonic remains one of the most debated topics in historical linguistics. Japanese has been variously proposed as related to Korean (Altaic hypothesis), Austronesian languages, Dravidian languages, and the Ainu language, but none of these proposals has achieved consensus among linguists. Some scholars treat Japanese as a language isolate at the family level, though the existence of the Ryukyuan languages means Japonic itself is not a single-language family.

## Writing System

Japanese employs one of the most complex writing systems in the world, using three distinct scripts simultaneously:

1. **Kanji (漢字):** Logographic characters borrowed from Chinese. The Japanese government's Jouyou Kanji list specifies 2,136 characters for general use, though educated adults typically know several thousand. Kanji are used for content words including most nouns, verb stems, and adjective stems.

2. **Hiragana (ひらがな):** A syllabary of 46 basic characters (plus diacritics and combinations) developed from cursive forms of Chinese characters. Hiragana is used for grammatical particles, verb and adjective inflectional endings (okurigana), native Japanese words not written in kanji, and as furigana (reading guides above kanji).

3. **Katakana (カタカナ):** A syllabary of 46 basic characters developed from components of Chinese characters. Katakana is primarily used for foreign loanwords (gairaigo), onomatopoeia, scientific terms, emphasis, and the names of plants and animals in scientific contexts.

4. **Romaji (ローマ字):** The Latin alphabet is used in limited contexts such as acronyms, brand names, and input methods for typing Japanese on computers.

## History of the Writing System

Japanese was originally an unwritten language. The adoption of Chinese characters began around the 5th century CE through contact with China and Korea. Initially, Chinese characters were used purely for their phonetic values to represent Japanese sounds, a system known as Man'yogana (named after the 8th-century poetry anthology Man'yoshu). Over centuries, the cursive simplification of Man'yogana characters gave rise to Hiragana, while the extraction of character components produced Katakana. By the Heian period (794-1185), the dual kana systems were well established alongside continued use of kanji.

## Typological Features

Japanese is a strongly head-final, agglutinative language with SOV (Subject-Object-Verb) word order. It uses postpositional particles rather than prepositions, has no grammatical gender or articles, and features an elaborate system of honorifics (keigo) that encodes social relationships directly into verbal morphology and lexical choice. The language lacks obligatory number marking on nouns and permits extensive pro-drop (omission of pronouns when recoverable from context). These typological features make Japanese markedly different from Chinese despite the shared use of kanji, and interestingly similar to Korean in grammatical structure despite entirely different writing systems.

## Language Identification Significance

The simultaneous use of three scripts (kanji, hiragana, katakana) in running text is a virtually unique feature of Japanese, making it one of the most readily identifiable languages in computational language identification. The presence of hiragana characters in particular serves as a near-definitive marker distinguishing Japanese text from Chinese.
