# Bulgarian Grammar (bul_Cyrl)

## Word Order

Bulgarian follows a basic **Subject-Verb-Object (SVO)** word order, consistent with most other European languages. However, word order is relatively flexible due to the rich verbal morphology and clitic system that help clarify grammatical roles. Topic-comment structure frequently causes reordering, with the topic fronted to sentence-initial position and the comment (new information) placed later. For example, *Книгата я прочетох вчера* ("The book, I read it yesterday") places the direct object first for topicalization, with the clitic pronoun *я* reinforcing the grammatical relationship.

## Morphological Type

Bulgarian is **analytic** in its nominal system, a feature that makes it unique among Slavic languages. While Proto-Slavic and most modern Slavic languages (Russian, Polish, Czech, Serbian) are highly synthetic, using extensive case inflections on nouns, adjectives, and pronouns, Bulgarian has **lost nearly all case distinctions** on nouns. This shift occurred gradually during the Middle Bulgarian period (12th-15th centuries) and aligns Bulgarian typologically with the other languages of the **Balkan Sprachbund** (Romanian, Albanian, modern Greek), which share similar analytic tendencies.

Instead of case endings, Bulgarian uses **prepositions** to express grammatical relationships that other Slavic languages encode morphologically. For example, where Russian uses the genitive case (*книга учителя* "the teacher's book"), Bulgarian uses the preposition *на* (*книгата на учителя* "the book of the teacher"). Only vestiges of the old case system survive, primarily in the vocative case for nouns (e.g., *Иване!* from *Иван*) and in some pronoun forms.

## Phonology

### Vowels

Bulgarian has **six vowel phonemes**:

| Vowel | IPA | Description | Example |
|-------|-----|-------------|---------|
| а | /a/ | open central | *маса* (table) |
| е | /ɛ/ | open-mid front | *мед* (honey) |
| и | /i/ | close front | *мир* (peace) |
| о | /ɔ/ | open-mid back rounded | *мост* (bridge) |
| у | /u/ | close back rounded | *ум* (mind) |
| ъ | /ɤ/ | close-mid back unrounded | *бъдеще* (future) |

The vowel **ъ** (/ɤ/) is particularly notable and diagnostically important. It represents a close-mid back unrounded vowel (similar to a schwa but higher and further back) that is characteristic of Bulgarian. This vowel does not exist in Russian, Serbian, or Macedonian in the same form, making words containing *ъ* strong indicators of Bulgarian text.

Bulgarian does **not** have phonemic vowel length. All vowels are approximately equal in duration regardless of stress, though unstressed vowels undergo some qualitative reduction (particularly *а* and *о* reducing toward [ɐ], and *е* reducing toward [ɪ]).

### Consonants

Bulgarian has approximately **30 consonant phonemes**, including both palatalized and non-palatalized variants of many consonants, though the extent of palatalization is less systematic than in Russian. Key consonants include:

- **Stops:** /p, b, t, d, k, ɡ/ and their palatalized counterparts
- **Fricatives:** /f, v, s, z, ʃ, ʒ, x/ and palatalized variants
- **Affricates:** /ts, dz, tʃ, dʒ/
- **Nasals:** /m, n/ and palatalized /mʲ, nʲ/
- **Liquids:** /l, r/ and palatalized /lʲ, rʲ/
- **Glides:** /j/

Final devoicing is a regular phonological process: voiced obstruents become voiceless at the end of a word (e.g., *град* /ɡrat/ "city").

### Stress

Bulgarian has **free (mobile) stress**, meaning stress can fall on any syllable of a word and is not predictable from the word's structure. Stress placement can distinguish otherwise identical words and shifts across different forms of the same word. For example: *въ̀лна* "wool" vs. *вълна̀* "wave." Unlike Czech or Polish, there is no fixed stress rule, and unlike Russian, Bulgarian stress patterns are somewhat less irregular, though still unpredictable and must be learned for each word.

## Noun System

### Loss of Cases

The most striking feature of the Bulgarian noun system is the **absence of a case system**. Bulgarian nouns do not inflect for nominative, accusative, genitive, dative, instrumental, or prepositional/locative cases. This is unique among Slavic languages. Grammatical relationships are expressed through:

- **Prepositions:** *на* (of, to), *в/във* (in), *с/със* (with), *от* (from), *за* (for, about), *по* (along, on), *до* (to, until), *при* (at, by), *без* (without), *между* (between)
- **Word order** and **context**

The **vocative case** survives as the sole productive case for nouns, used for direct address: *Иван* > *Иване!*, *жена* > *жено!*, *дете* > *дете!*

### Gender

Bulgarian nouns have **three grammatical genders**:

- **Masculine:** typically ending in a consonant (*град* "city," *учител* "teacher")
- **Feminine:** typically ending in *-а* or *-я* (*жена* "woman," *книга* "book")
- **Neuter:** typically ending in *-о* or *-е* (*дете* "child," *село* "village")

### Definite Article (Suffixed)

One of the most distinctive features of Bulgarian, and a key diagnostic for language identification, is the **postposed (suffixed) definite article**. Unlike most European languages that place the article before the noun, Bulgarian attaches it to the end:

| Gender | Full form | Short form | Example |
|--------|-----------|------------|---------|
| Masculine (subject) | -ът / -ят | -а / -я | *градът* / *града* (the city) |
| Feminine | -та | -та | *жената* (the woman) |
| Neuter | -то | -то | *детето* (the child) |
| Plural | -те | -те | *градовете* (the cities) |

The masculine has two forms: a "full" form (*-ът/-ят*) used for grammatical subjects, and a "short" form (*-а/-я*) used elsewhere. This distinction is maintained in formal writing but is collapsing in spoken language. The suffixed definite article is attached to the first element of a noun phrase: *хубавата книга* ("the beautiful book" -- article on the adjective).

### Number

Nouns inflect for **singular** and **plural**. Masculine nouns have particularly complex plural formation with several patterns: *-и* (*учители*), *-ове* (*градове*), *-а/-я* (*крака* "legs"), and others. A distinctive counting form (used with numerals) exists for masculine nouns: *два града* "two cities" (vs. *градове* "cities").

## Verb System

The Bulgarian verb system is **extremely rich and complex**, far more so than the nominal system. It is one of the most elaborate verb systems among the Slavic languages.

### Tenses

Bulgarian has a large number of tenses, organized into indicative, subjunctive-like, and evidential paradigms:

- **Present tense** (*Пиша* "I write / I am writing")
- **Aorist** (completed past action, a tense lost in most other Slavic languages): *Писах* "I wrote (and finished)"
- **Imperfect** (habitual or ongoing past action): *Пишех* "I was writing / I used to write"
- **Perfect** (compound past, similar to English present perfect): *Писал съм* "I have written"
- **Pluperfect:** *Бях писал* "I had written"
- **Future:** *Ще пиша* "I will write" (formed with the particle *ще*)
- **Future in the past:** *Щях да пиша* "I was going to write"
- **Past future perfect:** *Щях да съм писал* "I would have written"

### Aspect

Like all Slavic languages, Bulgarian verbs have **grammatical aspect** -- perfective and imperfective pairs:

- *Пиша* (imperfective: "write, be writing") vs. *напиша* (perfective: "write [to completion]")
- *Чета* (imperfective: "read") vs. *прочета* (perfective: "read through")

### Evidentiality and Renarrative Mood

Bulgarian possesses a grammaticalized system of **evidentiality** that is rare among European languages. The **renarrative mood** (преизказно наклонение) is used to report events that the speaker did not personally witness. This is formed using the past active participle without the auxiliary verb:

- **Direct (witnessed):** *Той дойде* "He came" (I saw it)
- **Renarrative (reported):** *Той дошъл* "He (reportedly) came" (I was told / I infer)

This evidential distinction is morphologically encoded in the verb system and is not merely a pragmatic or lexical choice, making it one of the most notable typological features of Bulgarian among European languages. It is shared, in somewhat different form, with Turkish and other Balkan languages, reflecting Balkan Sprachbund influence.

### Subjunctive Construction

Bulgarian lacks an infinitive in common use. Where other Slavic languages use the infinitive, Bulgarian uses the **да-construction** (the particle *да* + finite verb):

- Russian: *Хочу читать* ("I want to read" -- with infinitive)
- Bulgarian: *Искам да чета* ("I want [that] I read" -- with finite verb)

This is another analytic feature shared with other Balkan languages.

## Syntax Features

### Clitic Doubling

Bulgarian extensively uses **clitic doubling**, where a clitic pronoun co-occurs with a full noun phrase, particularly for topicalized or definite direct objects:

- *На Иван му казах* ("To Ivan, to-him I said" -- dative clitic doubling)
- *Книгата я прочетох* ("The book, it I read" -- accusative clitic doubling)

Clitic pronouns in Bulgarian have fixed positions within the clause, typically appearing in second position (Wackernagel's position) or immediately before the verb.

### Topic-Comment Structure

Bulgarian syntax is strongly influenced by **information structure**. Given (topic) information tends to precede new (comment/focus) information. Definite and known referents are typically fronted, while indefinite or new information appears postverbally. This interacts with clitic doubling, definiteness marking, and word order flexibility to create a discourse-driven syntax.

### Negative Concord

Bulgarian requires **negative concord**: when a sentence is negated, all indefinite elements must also be negative:

- *Никой нищо не каза* ("Nobody nothing not said" = "Nobody said anything")

Multiple negation is not only grammatical but obligatory in standard Bulgarian.
