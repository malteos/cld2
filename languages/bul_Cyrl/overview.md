# Bulgarian (bul_Cyrl)

## General Information

- **Language name:** Bulgarian (български език, *balgarski ezik*)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** bul
- **ISO 639-1 code:** bg
- **Script:** Cyrillic (Bulgarian variant)
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > South Slavic > Eastern South Slavic
- **Approximate speakers:** 7-8 million native speakers
- **Writing direction:** Left to right

## Geographic Distribution

Bulgarian is spoken primarily in Bulgaria, where it serves as the sole official language of the Republic of Bulgaria. The language is also spoken by significant diaspora communities in Turkey, Serbia, North Macedonia, Greece, Ukraine, Moldova, Romania, and various Western European countries and the Americas. In Turkey, ethnic Bulgarian communities (often referred to as Pomaks when Muslim) maintain the language, particularly in Thrace. Historical Bulgarian-speaking communities exist in parts of Ukraine (notably in the Odessa and Zaporizhzhia regions) and Moldova, where Bulgarian has a degree of regional recognition. Additionally, the worldwide Bulgarian diaspora, estimated at over one million people, maintains varying levels of language use in countries such as Spain, Germany, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Canada.

## Official Status

Bulgarian holds official status at multiple levels. It is the official language of the Republic of Bulgaria as enshrined in the Bulgarian constitution. Since Bulgaria's accession to the European Union in 2007, Bulgarian has been one of the official and working languages of the EU, making it one of 24 official EU languages. The Cyrillic script, as used for Bulgarian, became the third official script of the European Union alongside the Latin and Greek scripts. Bulgarian also has recognized minority language status in certain regions of neighboring countries, particularly in Serbia and Moldova.

## Historical Background

Bulgarian occupies a position of extraordinary historical significance among the Slavic languages. It is directly descended from Old Church Slavonic (also known as Old Bulgarian), the first Slavic language to be written down and the first Slavic literary language. In the 9th century, the Byzantine missionaries Saints Cyril and Methodius created the Glagolitic alphabet to translate religious texts into the Slavic vernacular of the region around Thessaloniki, which formed the basis of Old Church Slavonic. Their disciples, particularly Saint Clement of Ohrid and Saint Naum, later developed the Cyrillic alphabet at the Preslav Literary School in the First Bulgarian Empire during the late 9th and early 10th centuries.

The history of the Bulgarian language is conventionally divided into several periods. Old Bulgarian (9th-11th centuries) corresponds to Old Church Slavonic and was the literary and liturgical language of the medieval Bulgarian state. Middle Bulgarian (12th-15th centuries) saw significant grammatical changes, including the gradual loss of the case system, the development of the suffixed definite article, and other analytic features that distinguish modern Bulgarian from other Slavic languages. The Ottoman period (15th-19th centuries) brought extensive Turkish and Arabic lexical borrowings. The Bulgarian National Revival (18th-19th centuries) was marked by efforts to standardize the modern literary language and purge excessive foreign influence. Modern Bulgarian emerged in the late 19th century following Bulgaria's liberation from Ottoman rule in 1878.

The Cyrillic script itself is a major cultural contribution of the Bulgarian literary tradition. Each year on May 24th, Bulgaria celebrates the Day of Bulgarian Education and Culture and the Slavic Alphabet (Ден на българската просвета и култура и на славянската писменост), honoring the creation of the Slavonic script and its cultural legacy. This tradition underscores Bulgaria's foundational role in the broader cultural heritage of the Slavic-speaking world.

## Typological Profile

Modern Bulgarian is typologically unique among the Slavic languages. While most Slavic languages are highly synthetic (using extensive case inflection on nouns), Bulgarian has undergone a dramatic shift toward analytic grammar. It has lost nearly all of its nominal case inflections, relying instead on prepositions and word order to express grammatical relationships. It has also developed a postposed (suffixed) definite article, a feature shared with the other Balkan languages (Romanian, Albanian, Greek to some extent) as part of the Balkan Sprachbund -- a set of shared features among geographically neighboring but genetically diverse languages. These features make Bulgarian grammar significantly different from Russian, Polish, or Czech, despite their shared Slavic heritage.
