# Esperanto (epo_Latn)

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Esperanto
- **ISO 639-3 code:** epo
- **ISO 639-1 code:** eo
- **Language family:** Constructed language (international auxiliary language)
- **Script:** Latin (with diacritics: ĉ, ĝ, ĥ, ĵ, ŝ, ŭ)
- **Speakers:** Approximately 100,000 to 2,000,000 (estimates vary widely); roughly 1,000 native speakers (denaskaj esperantistoj)
- **Geographic distribution:** Worldwide, with strongest communities in Europe (especially Poland, Germany, France, Hungary, the Netherlands), East Asia (China, Japan, South Korea), and South America (Brazil)
- **Official status:** No country recognizes Esperanto as an official language. UNESCO adopted resolutions in support of Esperanto in 1954 and 1985. It is used as a working language by several international organizations, including the Universal Esperanto Association (Universala Esperanto-Asocio, UEA) and the World Esperanto Youth Organization (Tutmonda Esperantista Junulara Organizo, TEJO).

## History

Esperanto was created by Ludwik Lejzer Zamenhof (known in the community as Doktoro Esperanto), an ophthalmologist from Bialystok in the Russian Empire (now Poland). Growing up in a multiethnic city where speakers of Russian, Polish, German, and Yiddish frequently clashed, Zamenhof became convinced that a shared neutral language could promote peace and understanding between peoples.

Zamenhof published the first textbook of the language, *Unua Libro* (First Book), in 1887 under the pseudonym "Doktoro Esperanto" (Doctor Hopeful). The pseudonym soon became the name of the language itself. The book presented the entire grammar in 16 rules, a dictionary, and sample texts, demonstrating that the language was immediately usable.

In 1905, the first World Esperanto Congress (Universala Kongreso de Esperanto) was held in Boulogne-sur-Mer, France, attracting 688 participants. At this congress, the *Fundamento de Esperanto* was adopted as the foundational document of the language, codifying the basic grammar, vocabulary, and exercises. The Fundamento is considered inviolable, meaning that the 16 basic rules of grammar may never be changed, ensuring long-term stability of the language.

Throughout the early 20th century, Esperanto grew steadily but faced political opposition. The Soviet Union initially supported it but later persecuted Esperantists under Stalin. Nazi Germany classified Esperanto as a tool of Jewish conspiracy, partly because of Zamenhof's Jewish heritage, and Esperantists were targeted during the Holocaust. Despite these setbacks, the language survived and continued to grow.

The Raumism movement (Raŭmismo), named after the Finnish city of Rauma where a TEJO congress was held in 1980, shifted the focus of the Esperanto community from the original goal of making Esperanto a universal second language toward valuing the Esperanto-speaking community as a diaspora culture in its own right. This cultural approach emphasized Esperanto literature, music, and community identity.

## Modern Era

The advent of the internet in the 1990s and 2000s brought a significant revival of Esperanto. Online platforms made it far easier to find other speakers and access learning materials. Key developments include:

- **Lernu!** (lernu.net), a free multilingual platform for learning Esperanto, launched in 2002.
- **Duolingo** introduced an Esperanto course in 2015, which attracted hundreds of thousands of learners.
- **Vikipedio** (Esperanto Wikipedia) has over 300,000 articles, placing it among the larger language editions of Wikipedia.
- Active communities exist on Reddit (r/Esperanto), Telegram, Discord, and other social media platforms.
- **Pasporta Servo**, a hospitality service for Esperanto speakers, facilitates free lodging exchanges worldwide.

The annual Universal Congress of Esperanto continues to attract over 1,000 participants from dozens of countries. Esperanto has a rich literary tradition, with both original works and translations of world literature. Notable Esperanto authors include William Auld (nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature), Julio Baghy, and Claude Piron. The language also has a growing musical scene, podcasts, YouTube channels, and even a Google Translate integration.

Esperanto remains the most successful constructed international auxiliary language in history, with an unbroken tradition of use spanning over 135 years.
