# Distinguishing Esperanto from Similar Languages

## Overview

Esperanto draws much of its vocabulary from Romance languages (French, Italian, Spanish, Latin) and to a lesser extent from Germanic and Slavic languages. This lexical overlap can cause confusion during language identification. However, Esperanto has highly distinctive morphological features that set it apart from all natural languages and most other constructed languages.

## Differences from Romance Languages

### Italian
Italian shares significant lexical material with Esperanto, as both draw heavily from Latin roots. Words like *domo* (house), *libro* (book), *tempo* (time), and *mondo* (world) look similar in both languages. However, Italian lacks the Esperanto diacritics (ĉ, ĝ, ĥ, ĵ, ŝ, ŭ), does not use the -j plural, and has irregular verb conjugations with person/number marking. Italian uses articles *il, lo, la, i, gli, le* versus Esperanto's single *la*. Italian words end in various vowels depending on gender and declension, whereas Esperanto rigidly assigns -o to nouns and -a to adjectives.

### Spanish
Spanish and Esperanto share many cognates (*lerni* vs. *aprender* differ, but *problemo* vs. *problema*, *familio* vs. *familia* are close). Spanish has grammatical gender (el/la), irregular verbs, and person-marked conjugations (hablo, hablas, habla). Spanish uses the inverted question mark and exclamation mark. The letter *j* in Spanish represents /x/ (as in *jugar*), whereas in Esperanto it represents /j/ (as in *jes*). The presence of ñ is diagnostic for Spanish, while ĉ/ĝ/ŝ are diagnostic for Esperanto.

### French
French contributed heavily to Esperanto vocabulary, but French has complex spelling rules, silent letters, nasal vowels, grammatical gender, and irregular verbs - none of which Esperanto has. French uses accents (é, è, ê, ë, à, ù, ç) that are completely different from Esperanto's diacritics. French liaison and elision patterns are absent in Esperanto.

### Portuguese
Portuguese shares some vocabulary with Esperanto but is distinguished by nasal vowels (ã, õ), the cedilla (ç), circumflex and acute accents on vowels (â, ê, ô, á, é, í, ó, ú), and complex verb morphology. Portuguese word order and preposition usage differ significantly.

## Differences from Other Constructed Languages

### Ido (Most Confusable)
Ido is a reformed version of Esperanto created in 1907, and is the language most likely to be confused with Esperanto. Key differences:

- Ido does **not** use the Esperanto diacritics (ĉ, ĝ, ĥ, ĵ, ŝ, ŭ). Instead, Ido uses *ch, j, sh* digraphs. This is the single most diagnostic difference.
- Ido uses **-i** for plural nouns instead of **-oj**: Ido *domi* vs. Esperanto *domoj*.
- Ido uses **-o** for masculine and **-a** for feminine, whereas Esperanto does not systematically mark gender this way.
- Ido uses **-ez** for imperative instead of **-u**.
- Ido uses **-as, -is, -os** for verb tenses (same as Esperanto), but infinitive is **-ar** instead of **-i**.
- Ido lacks the accusative case marker **-n** in most contexts.
- Ido lacks the correlative table system (ki-/ti-/i-/ĉi-/neni-).

**Diagnostic test:** If the text contains ĉ, ĝ, ĵ, ŝ, or ŭ, it is almost certainly Esperanto, not Ido. If it contains -oj/-aj plurals and -n accusatives, it is Esperanto.

### Interlingua
Interlingua (IALA) looks much more like a natural Romance language than Esperanto does. It uses naturalistic vocabulary without the systematic Esperanto affixes. Interlingua has no accusative case, no mandatory word-class endings (-o, -a, -e), and no diacritics. Interlingua uses natural-looking plurals (adding -s) rather than -j. Words like *le* (the), *e* (and), *es* (is) differ from Esperanto *la*, *kaj*, *estas*.

### Volapuk
Volapuk (Volapuk) is an earlier constructed language (1879) with very different phonology and vocabulary. It uses heavily modified roots that are often unrecognizable from their source languages. Volapuk uses umlauts (a, o, u) instead of circumflexes. Its morphology and vocabulary are sufficiently different that confusion with Esperanto is unlikely.

### Lingua Franca Nova (Elefen)
Elefen uses a Romance-like vocabulary without diacritics and with simpler morphology. It uses natural-looking plurals with -s and has no accusative case marking. Its articles (*la*, *un*) and prepositions differ in usage.

## Summary of Diagnostic Features

The following features, in order of diagnostic strength, distinguish Esperanto from all other languages:

1. **Diacritic characters ĉ, ĝ, ĥ, ĵ, ŝ, ŭ** - Nearly unique to Esperanto. Presence of any of these (especially ĉ, ĝ, ŝ) is almost conclusive.
2. **-oj / -aj plural endings** - The -j plural is distinctive; the combination -ojn/-ajn is nearly unique.
3. **-as / -is / -os verb tense endings** applied without person marking (shared only with Ido, but Ido lacks the diacritics).
4. **Correlative system** - Words like *kiu, tiu, ĉiu, neniu, kio, tio, ĉio, nenio* in systematic patterns.
5. **Accusative -n** on nouns, adjectives, and pronouns.
6. **Consistent word-class endings** - Every noun ending in -o, every adjective in -a, every adverb in -e.
7. **High-frequency function words** - *kaj* (and), *estas* (is), *ĉar* (because), *ankaŭ* (also), *ĉu* (question particle).
8. **Systematic affix patterns** - *mal-*, *-ej-*, *-ist-*, *-ig-*, *-iĝ-* appearing regularly throughout text.
