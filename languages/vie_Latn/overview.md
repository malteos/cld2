# Vietnamese (vie_Latn) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Vietnamese (*tiếng Việt*; older/formal *Việt ngữ*)
- **ISO 639-1 code:** vi
- **ISO 639-3 code:** vie
- **Script:** Latin (Latn), in the form called *chữ Quốc ngữ* ("national language script"), with extensive diacritics
- **Language family:** Austroasiatic > Vietic > Viet-Muong. Its closest relative is Muong (*tiếng Mường*).
- **Speakers:** commonly estimated at about 85 million first-language speakers, with several million more second-language speakers among Vietnam's ethnic minorities (figures vary by source and year).

## Geographic Distribution

Vietnamese is the national and official language of the Socialist Republic of Vietnam, used in government, education, media and everyday life. Large diaspora communities exist in the United States (notably California and Texas), France, Australia, Canada, Cambodia, Laos, Germany, Taiwan, South Korea, Japan and the Czech Republic, where Vietnamese was recognized as the language of a national minority in 2013.

## Dialects

Three main dialect regions are usually distinguished: **Northern** (Hanoi), **Central** (e.g. Huế, Nghệ An) and **Southern** (Saigon/Ho Chi Minh City). They differ in tones, initial consonants (e.g. *d, gi, r* pronounced alike in the North; *v* often /j/ in the South) and some vocabulary (*bố/ba* "father", *ngô/bắp* "maize"). The written standard is largely based on Northern pronunciation, and spelling is uniform across regions, so dialect is rarely visible in written text apart from vocabulary.

## Linguistic Profile (brief)

Vietnamese is an analytic, isolating language: words do not inflect, and grammatical meaning is carried by word order and particles. It is **tonal**, with six tones in the Northern standard (*ngang, huyền, sắc, hỏi, ngã, nặng*), five in most Southern varieties where *hỏi* and *ngã* merge. Writing separates every syllable with a space, so even compound words appear as sequences of short tokens (*Việt Nam*, *học sinh*). A large share of the vocabulary is Sino-Vietnamese, borrowed from Chinese over two millennia (the often-quoted figure of around 60% refers mostly to technical and formal vocabulary and should be treated as an approximation).

## History of Writing

- For most of its history Vietnam used **Classical Chinese** (*chữ Hán*) as the written language of administration and scholarship.
- From around the 13th century, **chữ Nôm**, a script built from Chinese characters and newly created characters, was used to write vernacular Vietnamese, especially literature (e.g. Nguyễn Du's *Truyện Kiều*).
- In the 17th century Portuguese, Italian and French Catholic missionaries developed a Latin-based orthography. Alexandre de Rhodes's *Dictionarium Annamiticum Lusitanum et Latinum* (Rome, 1651) is the best-known early codification.
- Under French colonial rule the Latin script was promoted in administration and schools; the traditional Chinese-based civil service examinations ended in 1919. In the 20th century nationalist and literacy movements adopted *chữ Quốc ngữ*, and after 1945 it became the sole national script.

## Standardization and Status

Vietnamese is the official language under the constitution of Vietnam. There is no single academy comparable to European language academies; orthographic norms are set through Ministry of Education regulations and major dictionaries (e.g. the Vietnam Institute of Linguistics' *Từ điển tiếng Việt*). Minor spelling variation persists, for example *i/y* in syllables such as *kỹ/kĩ*, *lý/lí*, and the placement of tone marks on diphthongs (*hoà* vs. *hòa*, *thuý* vs. *thúy*). Digitally, text may be precomposed (NFC, Unicode block Latin Extended Additional) or decomposed with combining marks, and older material may use legacy 8-bit encodings such as TCVN3 or VNI. Informal online text frequently omits all diacritics.
