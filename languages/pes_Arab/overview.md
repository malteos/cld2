# Western Persian / Farsi (pes_Arab)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Western Persian (Farsi)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** pes
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** fa
- **Script:** Perso-Arabic (a modified Arabic script)
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Indo-Iranian > Iranian > Western Iranian > Southwestern Iranian
- **Speakers:** Approximately 110 million (roughly 75-80 million native, 30-35 million L2)
- **Writing Direction:** Right-to-left (RTL)

## Geographic Distribution

Western Persian, commonly known as Farsi, is the dominant language of Iran, where it serves as the sole official language and the primary medium of government, education, media, and literature. It is also widely spoken in parts of Afghanistan (where the closely related variety is called Dari) and Tajikistan (where it is known as Tajik, though typically written in Cyrillic script). Significant diaspora communities exist in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Germany, Sweden, Turkey, and the United Arab Emirates.

Iran itself has approximately 85 million people, the vast majority of whom speak Persian either as a first or second language. Even among ethnic minorities such as Azerbaijanis, Kurds, Lurs, and Baloch, Persian functions as the lingua franca and the language of formal education. In Afghanistan, Dari is one of the two official languages alongside Pashto and is the mother tongue of the Tajik, Hazara, and Aimaq populations, among others. In Tajikistan, the language (Tajik) is the national language and is spoken by the majority of the population.

## Official Status

Persian holds official status in Iran (as Farsi), Afghanistan (as Dari), and Tajikistan (as Tajik). It is also recognized as a minority or regional language in parts of Uzbekistan, Bahrain, and Iraq. Historically, Persian served as a prestigious court language and lingua franca across a vast region stretching from the Ottoman Empire to the Mughal Empire in South Asia. Its influence extended to Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the Indian subcontinent, where it was the administrative language for centuries.

## Historical Background

Persian is one of the world's oldest continuously attested languages, with a literary tradition spanning over 2,500 years. Its history is conventionally divided into three major periods:

**Old Persian** (c. 525-300 BCE) is attested primarily through the royal inscriptions of the Achaemenid Empire, most notably the Behistun Inscription of Darius the Great. Written in Old Persian cuneiform, this stage of the language was the administrative tongue of one of the ancient world's largest empires.

**Middle Persian** (c. 300 BCE - 800 CE), also known as Pahlavi, was the language of the Sasanian Empire and the primary vehicle for Zoroastrian religious literature. It was written in an Aramaic-derived script and represents a significant simplification from Old Persian in terms of morphology.

**New Persian** (c. 800 CE - present) emerged after the Arab conquest of Persia in the 7th century. While the language absorbed a substantial stratum of Arabic vocabulary, its grammar and core structure remained distinctly Iranian. New Persian adopted the Arabic script, supplemented with additional characters to represent sounds not found in Arabic.

## Classical Literature

The classical period of Persian literature (roughly the 9th through 15th centuries) produced some of the world's most celebrated literary works. Ferdowsi's *Shahnameh* (Book of Kings), completed around 1010 CE, is an epic poem of approximately 50,000 couplets recounting the mythological and historical past of Iran. Ferdowsi is credited with helping to preserve the Persian language by deliberately avoiding Arabic loanwords.

Other towering figures include the poets Rumi (Jalal ad-Din Muhammad Balkhi), whose mystical poetry in the *Masnavi* has made him one of the most widely read poets in the world; Hafez, whose *Divan* is a cornerstone of Persian lyric poetry; Sa'di, author of the *Golestan* and *Bustan*; and Omar Khayyam, whose *Rubaiyat* became famous in the West through Edward FitzGerald's translation.

Persian literature has profoundly influenced the literary traditions of Turkish, Urdu, and numerous Central Asian languages, and continues to be a vibrant and evolving tradition in the modern era.

## Modern Persian

Modern Persian has undergone significant standardization efforts, particularly during the 20th century. The Academy of Persian Language and Literature (Farhangestan) in Iran works to coin native Persian equivalents for foreign loanwords and to regulate linguistic standards. Despite these efforts, Persian continues to absorb vocabulary from French, English, and other European languages, especially in the domains of science, technology, and popular culture. Contemporary Persian literature, cinema, and media remain highly productive and internationally recognized.
