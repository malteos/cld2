# Northern Kurdish / Kurmanji (kmr_Latn)

## General Information

- **Language Name:** Northern Kurdish / Kurmanji / Kurmanci
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** kmr
- **Script:** Latin (Latn), with diacritics: C, E, I, S, U, c, e, i, s, u
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Indo-Iranian > Iranian > Western Iranian > Northwestern Iranian > Kurdish
- **Native Speakers:** Approximately 15-20 million
- **Type:** Fusional, SOV word order

## Geographic Distribution

Northern Kurdish, widely known as Kurmanji, is the most spoken variety of the Kurdish language continuum. Its speakers are distributed across a broad geographic area spanning several nation-states. The largest concentration of Kurmanji speakers is found in southeastern Turkey, where the majority of the Kurdish population resides. Significant communities also exist in northern Syria (particularly the Jazira, Kobani, and Afrin regions), northern Iraq (especially in the Duhok and parts of the Erbil governorates), and northwestern Iran (in the provinces of West Azerbaijan and Khorasan).

A substantial Kurmanji-speaking diaspora has formed in Western Europe over the past several decades, driven by political conflict and economic migration. Germany hosts the largest diaspora community, estimated at over one million people. Other significant communities exist in Sweden, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Austria, and the United Kingdom. Smaller diaspora populations are present in countries of the former Soviet Union, particularly Armenia and Georgia, where Kurdish communities have lived for centuries.

## Official Status

Kurmanji holds official status in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, where it is recognized alongside Sorani (Central Kurdish) and Arabic. In Turkey, where the largest number of Kurmanji speakers reside, the language has no official status and was subject to severe legal suppression for much of the 20th century. Restrictions on Kurdish language use in education, broadcasting, and public life were gradually relaxed beginning in the early 2000s, though Kurdish remains excluded from official state functions. In Syria, Kurdish had no recognized legal status under the Ba'athist government, but the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria has since adopted Kurmanji as one of its official languages. In Iran, Kurdish is not an official language at the national level, though it is recognized regionally in some contexts.

## Historical Development

### Origins and Early History

Kurdish belongs to the Northwestern Iranian branch of the Indo-Iranian language family, making it a relative of Persian, Balochi, and the Zaza-Gorani languages. The divergence of Kurdish from other Iranian languages is generally placed in the medieval period, though the precise chronology remains debated among linguists. The earliest attestations of distinctly Kurdish linguistic material appear in texts from the 7th and 8th centuries, but substantial written records do not emerge until considerably later.

### Classical and Pre-Modern Period

The oldest known literary work in Kurmanji is widely considered to be the writings of Ali Hariri (1009-1079), though the dating and attribution of early Kurdish texts remain subjects of scholarly discussion. A more clearly established literary tradition emerges with the work of Ahmad Khani (1650-1707), whose epic poem "Mem and Zin" is regarded as a masterwork of Kurdish literature and an early expression of Kurdish national consciousness. Other notable pre-modern Kurmanji poets include Mala Jaziri (1570-1640) and Faqiye Teyran (1590-1660). These literary works were composed in a modified Arabic script.

### The Bedirxan Latin Alphabet and Modern Standardization

A pivotal development in the history of Kurmanji came in the 1930s when the Bedirxan brothers -- Celadet Ali Bedirxan and Kamuran Ali Bedirxan -- developed a Latin-based alphabet for Kurmanji while in exile in Syria. Celadet Bedirxan promoted this alphabet through the journal "Hawar" (meaning "The Cry"), published in Damascus from 1932 to 1943. The Bedirxan alphabet was adapted from the Turkish Latin alphabet introduced by Ataturk in 1928, with the addition of characters needed for Kurdish phonology, including the circumflexed vowels (e, i, u) and the letters Q, W, and X, which do not appear in Turkish. This alphabet, often referred to as the Hawar alphabet, became the dominant writing system for Kurmanji in Turkey and Syria and is now the standard Latin orthography used in education, publishing, and digital communication across the Kurmanji-speaking world.

### Modern Period

The 20th century was marked by both cultural suppression and resilience for the Kurmanji language. In Turkey, the use of Kurdish was prohibited in public life, education, and broadcasting following the establishment of the Republic in 1923, with enforcement intensifying in subsequent decades. Despite these restrictions, oral literary traditions persisted, and clandestine publishing continued in the diaspora. The founding of the Kurdish Institute of Paris in 1983 and similar institutions provided infrastructure for language preservation and standardization. Since the early 2000s, liberalization in Turkey has allowed limited Kurdish-language broadcasting, private language courses, and some academic programs. In the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, Kurmanji is used in education, media, and government alongside Sorani. Digital media, satellite television, and the internet have played an increasingly important role in the dissemination and standardization of Kurmanji in the 21st century.
