# Latvian (Latviešu valoda)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Latvian (Latviešu valoda)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: lvs
- **ISO 639-1 code**: lv
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Baltic > Eastern Baltic
- **Script**: Latin (with diacritics)
- **Speakers**: Approximately 1.5 million native speakers
- **Primary region**: Latvia (Latvija)
- **Official status**: Official language of the Republic of Latvia; official language of the European Union

## Classification

Latvian belongs to the Eastern Baltic branch of the Baltic language group within the Indo-European family. It is one of only two surviving Baltic languages, the other being Lithuanian. Together, these languages form the most archaic surviving branch of the Indo-European family, preserving many features that have been lost in other branches. The Baltic languages are notable among Indo-European languages for retaining a highly conservative phonological and morphological system, though Latvian has undergone more innovation than Lithuanian in several respects.

Latvian is sometimes further subdivided into Standard Latvian (based on the Central dialect) and Latgalian, spoken in the eastern Latgale region. The status of Latgalian as a separate language or a dialect of Latvian is a matter of ongoing scholarly and political discussion. Standard Latvian is based primarily on the Central (Vidzeme) dialect and was codified in the early twentieth century.

## Geographic Distribution

The vast majority of Latvian speakers reside in Latvia, where Latvian serves as the sole official state language. Latvian-speaking diaspora communities exist in the United States, the United Kingdom, Ireland, Germany, Australia, Canada, Brazil, and Sweden, largely the result of emigration during and after the Second World War, during the Soviet era, and following Latvia's accession to the European Union in 2004. Within Latvia itself, Latvian coexists with Russian, which is spoken by a significant minority of the population, particularly in Riga and the eastern Latgale region.

## Historical Development

The Baltic peoples have inhabited the eastern Baltic coast for thousands of years. The earliest written records in Latvian date from the sixteenth century, with the first known Latvian text being a translation of the Lord's Prayer from 1530, followed by a Catholic catechism in 1585. The development of Latvian as a literary language accelerated during the nineteenth-century National Awakening (Atmoda), when Latvian intellectuals worked to standardize the language and promote its use in education, literature, and public life.

During the period of the independent First Republic of Latvia (1918--1940), Latvian achieved full official status and was standardized. The Soviet occupation (1940--1941, 1944--1991) brought significant Russification pressures, with Russian becoming dominant in many official and public domains. Following the restoration of independence in 1991, Latvian was reestablished as the sole official language, and language policies have since aimed to strengthen its position. The Latvian Language Agency (Latviešu valodas aģentūra) oversees language policy and planning.

## Script and Orthography

Latvian uses the Latin script augmented with several diacritical marks. Long vowels are indicated by macrons (ā, ē, ī, ū), palatalized consonants are marked by cedillas or commas below (ģ, ķ, ļ, ņ), and the postalveolar fricatives and affricate are written with carons (č, š, ž). The Latvian alphabet consists of 33 letters and reflects a largely phonemic orthography, meaning that the written form closely corresponds to pronunciation. This orthographic system was established in the early twentieth century, replacing an older German-influenced spelling system.

## Sociolinguistic Context

Latvian is a vital component of national identity in Latvia. As one of only two living Baltic languages, it holds considerable significance for historical linguists studying the Indo-European language family. Its conservative features, including a rich case system and tonal accent in certain dialects, make it an important language for comparative linguistic research. The language is actively maintained through education policy, media, and cultural institutions, though it faces demographic challenges due to emigration and the presence of a large Russian-speaking minority.
