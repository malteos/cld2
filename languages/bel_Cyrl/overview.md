# Belarusian (bel_Cyrl) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Belarusian (беларуская мова)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: bel
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Slavic > East Slavic
- **Script**: Cyrillic (Belarusian variant)
- **Speakers**: Approximately 5-7 million native speakers
- **Primary region**: Belarus
- **Official status**: Co-official language with Russian in the Republic of Belarus

## Geographic Distribution

Belarusian is the national language of Belarus and is spoken primarily within the borders of the Republic of Belarus. Significant diaspora communities exist in neighboring countries, including Poland (particularly in the Podlasie region), Russia (especially in western regions bordering Belarus such as Smolensk and Bryansk oblasts), Lithuania, Latvia, and Ukraine. Smaller communities of Belarusian speakers can also be found in the United States, Canada, Argentina, Australia, and Western Europe. Despite its official status in Belarus, the language faces significant competition from Russian, which dominates urban life, media, government, and education in practice.

## Historical Background

Belarusian developed from the Old East Slavic language, which was the common ancestor of modern Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian. During the period of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania (13th-18th centuries), the predecessor of modern Belarusian, known as Ruthenian or Old Belarusian, served as the official chancery language of the state. This period produced notable literary and legal documents, including the Statutes of Lithuania, which were written in this language.

The language was significantly influenced by Polish during the centuries of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth (1569-1795), which introduced numerous lexical borrowings and some grammatical features. After the partitions of Poland and incorporation into the Russian Empire, Belarusian was suppressed and Russian influence grew substantially. The modern Belarusian literary language began to take shape in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with key figures such as Frantsishak Bahushevich, Yanka Kupala, and Yakub Kolas playing central roles in its codification and literary development.

## Orthographic Traditions

A distinctive feature of Belarusian linguistics is the existence of two parallel orthographic traditions:

1. **Narkamauka** (Наркамаўка): The official orthography established in 1933 during the Soviet era by the People's Commissariat (Narkamat) of Education. This is the standard used in official documents, education, and state media in Belarus today. It underwent reforms in 1933 that brought Belarusian orthography closer to Russian patterns and was further revised in 2008.

2. **Tarashkevitsa** (Тарашкевіца): The classical orthography based on the norms established by Branislau Tarashkevich in his 1918 grammar. This system is considered by many linguists and cultural activists to more accurately reflect Belarusian phonology and to preserve features that distinguish Belarusian from Russian. It is used by parts of the diaspora, independent media, and cultural organizations.

The differences between the two systems involve spelling conventions, certain morphological forms, and the treatment of loanwords, but they are mutually intelligible and represent the same language.

## Sociolinguistic Situation

The sociolinguistic situation of Belarusian is complex. While it holds co-official status alongside Russian, the actual use of Belarusian has declined significantly over the past century. Russian dominates in urban centers, government administration, business, and most media. Belarusian is more commonly heard in rural areas and among cultural and intellectual communities. The phenomenon of "trasianka" -- a mixed speech combining elements of Belarusian and Russian -- is widespread in everyday communication. Language revitalization efforts have been ongoing, with various civic organizations promoting Belarusian language education and cultural activities, though these efforts face political and social challenges.
