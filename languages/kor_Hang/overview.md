# Korean (한국어 / 조선어) - kor_Hang

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Korean (한국어 *hangugeo* in South Korea; 조선어 *chosono* in North Korea)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** kor
- **Script:** Hangul (한글 / 조선글)
- **Language Family:** Koreanic (sometimes classified as a language isolate)
- **Speakers:** Approximately 77 million native speakers
- **Regions:** South Korea, North Korea, with significant diaspora communities in China (Yanbian Korean Autonomous Prefecture), Japan, the United States, Central Asia (Koryo-saram), and other countries worldwide
- **Official Status:** Official language of South Korea (Republic of Korea) and North Korea (Democratic People's Republic of Korea)

## Language Family and Classification

Korean is the primary member of the Koreanic language family, which also includes the critically endangered Jeju language spoken on Jeju Island. The broader genetic affiliation of Korean has been the subject of extensive scholarly debate. Proposals have linked Korean to the Altaic language family (alongside Turkic, Mongolic, and Tungusic languages), to Japonic languages, or to a Dravidian substrate, but none of these hypotheses has achieved consensus among historical linguists. As a result, Korean is frequently treated as a language isolate for practical classification purposes. The Koreanic family itself, consisting of Korean and Jeju, is well established, but connections beyond this remain speculative.

## Script: Hangul

Hangul is the native script of the Korean language and one of the most systematically designed writing systems in the world. It was created in 1443 under the direction of King Sejong the Great of the Joseon dynasty and promulgated in 1446 through the document *Hunminjeongeum* (훈민정음, "The Correct Sounds for the Instruction of the People"). Prior to Hangul's invention, Koreans relied on Classical Chinese characters (Hanja, 한자) for writing, a system that was largely inaccessible to the common populace due to its complexity and the significant education required to master it.

Hangul was designed with phonological principles in mind. The consonant letters are based on the shape of the speech organs when producing the corresponding sounds, while vowel letters are constructed from combinations of three basic elements representing heaven (a dot, now a short stroke), earth (a horizontal line), and humanity (a vertical line). Letters are grouped into syllable blocks rather than written linearly, giving Korean text its distinctive visual appearance. The system originally comprised 17 consonant and 11 vowel letters; modern Korean uses 14 basic consonants and 10 basic vowels, along with 5 double consonants and 11 compound vowels.

## Historical Development

The Korean language has a documented history spanning over a millennium. It is conventionally divided into Old Korean (pre-10th century), Middle Korean (10th-16th century), and Modern Korean (17th century onward). Old Korean is attested primarily through *idu* and *hyangchal* transcription systems that used Chinese characters to represent Korean sounds and grammar. Middle Korean, the period during which Hangul was created, is well documented and shows significant differences from the modern language in phonology and grammar.

Following Hangul's creation, the script coexisted with Hanja for centuries. During the Japanese colonial period (1910-1945), the Korean language and Hangul faced suppression, fueling their role as symbols of national identity. After liberation, South Korea gradually reduced Hanja usage in favor of Hangul-only writing, while North Korea abolished Hanja entirely. Today, South Korean text occasionally includes Hanja in parentheses for disambiguation of Sino-Korean homonyms, particularly in academic and legal writing, but everyday text is overwhelmingly written in pure Hangul.

## Sociolinguistic Situation

The language exhibits dialectal variation across regions, with the Seoul dialect serving as the basis for Standard Korean (표준어) in South Korea and the Pyongyang dialect informing the standard language (문화어) in North Korea. Decades of division have led to noticeable lexical and stylistic divergence between the two standards, though mutual intelligibility is maintained. Korean's global presence has grown through the Korean Wave (한류, *hallyu*), increasing international interest in learning the language.
