# Croatian (hrv_Latn)

## Language Overview

Croatian (hrvatski jezik) is a South Slavic language of the Indo-European language family, written in the Latin script. Its ISO 639-3 code is **hrv**, and it is classified under the broader Central South Slavic dialect continuum. Croatian serves as the official and literary language of the Republic of Croatia and is one of the 24 official languages of the European Union, a status it gained upon Croatia's accession in 2013.

## Speaker Population and Distribution

Croatian has approximately 5.5 million native speakers, the majority of whom reside in Croatia. Significant Croatian-speaking communities exist in Bosnia and Herzegovina, where Croatian is one of three official languages alongside Bosnian and Serbian. Diaspora communities maintain the language in Austria (particularly Burgenland, where Burgenland Croatian has a long history), Germany, Australia, Canada, the United States, Argentina, Chile, and other countries that received waves of Croatian emigration throughout the 19th and 20th centuries. In total, including second-language speakers, Croatian is used by roughly 6.5 to 7 million people worldwide.

## Historical Development and Standardization

The history of Croatian as a literary language dates back to the medieval period, with early texts appearing in the Chakavian and Shtokavian dialects written in the Latin script, as well as the Glagolitic script used by Croatian churchmen. The earliest known Croatian text in the Latin script is the "Bascanska ploca" (Baska tablet) from around 1100 CE, written in Glagolitic.

Modern standard Croatian is based on the Neo-Shtokavian Ijekavian dialect, codified largely through the work of 19th-century linguists and writers. The Illyrian movement of the 1830s-1850s, led by Ljudevit Gaj, was instrumental in standardizing the orthography and promoting a unified literary language. Gaj introduced a modified Latin alphabet with diacritical marks (the "Gaj's Latin alphabet") that remains the basis of Croatian orthography today.

During the period of Yugoslavia (1918-1991), Croatian was often grouped under the umbrella term "Serbo-Croatian" (srpskohrvatski / hrvatskosrpski), a political and linguistic construct that treated Croatian and Serbian as variants of a single language. This classification was contested by many Croatian linguists and cultural figures who maintained the distinctness of Croatian. Following Croatian independence in 1991, Croatian was affirmed as a separate standard language with its own normative tradition.

## Relationship to Other South Slavic Languages

Croatian is mutually intelligible to a high degree with Serbian and Bosnian, and these three languages share a common structural base in the Neo-Shtokavian dialect. However, Croatian maintains a distinct literary tradition, lexical preferences, and orthographic norms. Croatian favors native Slavic vocabulary and calques over international loanwords, a tendency reinforced by purist language-planning traditions dating back centuries. In contrast, Serbian has historically been more receptive to borrowings from Turkish, French, and Greek.

Croatian is also related to Slovenian, though mutual intelligibility with Slovenian is significantly lower due to greater phonological, morphological, and lexical divergence. Macedonian and Bulgarian, as Eastern South Slavic languages, are more distant still.

## Dialectal Diversity

Croatian is traditionally divided into three major dialect groups: Shtokavian (stokavski), Chakavian (cakavski), and Kajkavian (kajkavski), named after their respective interrogative pronouns for "what" (sto, ca, kaj). The standard language is based on Shtokavian, but Chakavian and Kajkavian remain vibrant in regional use, contributing to the richness of Croatian linguistic heritage.
