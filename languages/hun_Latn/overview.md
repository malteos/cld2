# Hungarian (Magyar) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Hungarian (Magyar)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** hun
- **Script:** Latin (Latn)
- **Language Family:** Uralic > Finno-Ugric > Ugric
- **Native Speakers:** ~13 million
- **Total Speakers:** ~15 million (including L2 speakers)

## Geographic Distribution

Hungarian is the official language of Hungary, where approximately 9.8 million people speak it as their native language. Significant Hungarian-speaking minorities exist throughout the Carpathian Basin due to the historical borders of the Kingdom of Hungary and the territorial changes following the Treaty of Trianon (1920):

- **Romania:** ~1.2 million speakers, concentrated in Transylvania (Erdely), particularly in Szeklerland (Szekelyfold) and cities like Cluj-Napoca (Kolozsvar) and Targu Mures (Marosvasarhely)
- **Slovakia:** ~460,000 speakers, primarily in the southern border regions
- **Serbia:** ~250,000 speakers, mostly in Vojvodina (Vajdasag), centered around Subotica (Szabadka)
- **Ukraine:** ~150,000 speakers, in Transcarpathia (Karpatalja)
- **Austria:** ~25,000 speakers, in Burgenland (Orsegvidek)
- **Slovenia:** ~5,000 speakers, in the Prekmurje region
- **Croatia:** ~15,000 speakers, in eastern Slavonia and Baranya

Diaspora communities also exist in the United States, Canada, Australia, Germany, and the United Kingdom.

## Official Status

Hungarian is the official language of Hungary and one of the 24 official languages of the European Union. It has co-official or recognized minority status in several neighboring countries, including Romania, Slovakia, Serbia, Slovenia, and Austria.

## Uniqueness Among European Languages

Hungarian holds a distinctive position among European languages as one of the very few that does not belong to the Indo-European language family. While surrounded entirely by Indo-European languages (Slavic, Germanic, and Romance), Hungarian is a Uralic language, sharing distant ancestry with Finnish and Estonian. However, the separation between Hungarian and its closest Uralic relatives occurred thousands of years ago, making mutual intelligibility impossible. This isolation has made Hungarian one of the most distinctive languages of Europe, with a vocabulary and grammar that bear little resemblance to those of its neighbors.

## Historical Development

The earliest known Hungarian text is the Halotti Beszed es Konyorges (Funeral Sermon and Prayer), dating from around 1192-1195. The oldest known Hungarian poem, the Omagyar Maria-siralom (Old Hungarian Lament of Mary), dates from approximately 1300.

### Language Reform (Nyelvujitas)

The Hungarian Language Reform (Nyelvujitas) of the late 18th and early 19th centuries was one of the most significant planned language modernization efforts in European history. Led by writer Ferenc Kazinczy and supported by numerous intellectuals, the movement sought to create a modern literary and scientific vocabulary capable of replacing Latin and German, which had dominated Hungarian intellectual and administrative life. The reformers coined thousands of new words through various strategies: reviving archaic terms, borrowing from Hungarian dialects, creating calques from other languages, and inventing entirely new words using Hungarian morphological patterns. It is estimated that approximately 10,000 new words were introduced during this period, of which roughly half remain in everyday use today. The reform was closely tied to the broader Hungarian national awakening and the push for Hungarian to replace Latin as the official language of the Kingdom of Hungary, which was achieved in 1844.

## Classification Notes

Hungarian belongs to the Ugric branch of the Uralic family, along with the Ob-Ugric languages Khanty and Mansi, spoken in western Siberia. The Ugric languages separated from the Finno-Permic branch several thousand years ago, and the divergence between Hungarian and the Ob-Ugric languages is estimated to have occurred around 1000-500 BCE. Despite popular misconceptions, Hungarian is not closely related to Turkish, although both languages share typological features such as agglutination and vowel harmony due to areal contact rather than genetic relationship.
