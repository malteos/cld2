# Russian (rus_Cyrl)

## General Information

- **Language name:** Russian
- **Native name:** русский язык (russkiy yazyk / russkij jazyk)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** rus
- **ISO 639-1 code:** ru
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > East Slavic
- **Script:** Cyrillic (Russian alphabet, 33 letters)
- **Approximate number of speakers:** roughly 150 million native speakers and well over 200 million in total including second-language speakers (estimates vary considerably by source)
- **Primary region:** Russian Federation
- **Other regions:** Belarus, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan (co-official or official status), Ukraine, the Baltic states, Moldova, the Caucasus and Central Asia; large diaspora communities in Israel, Germany, the United States and elsewhere

## Official Status

Russian is the state language of the Russian Federation, an official language of Belarus, and has official or co-official status in Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan. It is one of the six official languages of the United Nations and a working language of many post-Soviet international bodies. Throughout the twentieth century it served as the lingua franca of the Soviet Union, which is why it remains widely understood (and frequently code-switched with) in Ukraine, Belarus, the Caucasus and Central Asia. For language identification this matters: Russian words and whole sentences appear inside Ukrainian, Belarusian, Kazakh and Kyrgyz text, and vice versa.

## Language Family

Russian is an East Slavic language, closely related to Ukrainian and Belarusian and more distantly to Rusyn. All four descend from Old East Slavic, the language of Kyivan Rus. Russian also absorbed a large layer of Church Slavonic (South Slavic) vocabulary through the Orthodox liturgy, which explains doublets such as *город* (native, "town") vs. *-град* (Slavonic, as in *Ленинград*), and gives Russian a learned vocabulary that partly overlaps with Bulgarian.

## Historical Development

Distinctively Russian features emerged in the north-eastern principalities (Vladimir-Suzdal, Novgorod, later Muscovy) from the 13th-15th centuries onward. The Moscow chancery language became the basis of the standard. In the 18th century Lomonosov codified a "three styles" theory balancing Church Slavonic and vernacular elements, and Peter the Great introduced the simplified civil script (*гражданский шрифт*). The modern literary language is conventionally dated to Pushkin (early 19th century).

The orthographic reform of 1917-1918 removed the letters ѣ, і, ѳ and ѵ and the word-final hard sign ъ, producing the present alphabet. The letter ё was introduced in the late 18th century but is optional in most printed text and is commonly replaced by е (this is visible in the frequency list, where both *всё* and *все*, *её* and *ее* occur; see `vocabulary.json`).

## Writing System

Russian uses the Cyrillic alphabet: а б в г д е ё ж з и й к л м н о п р с т у ф х ц ч ш щ ъ ы ь э ю я. Letters that distinguish it from neighbouring Cyrillic orthographies include **ы**, **э**, **ъ** and **ё** (none of which appear in Ukrainian), while Russian lacks Ukrainian і, ї, є, ґ, Belarusian ў, and Serbian/Macedonian ј, љ, њ. See `characteristics.md` and `differences.md` for details.

In informal digital contexts Russian is frequently written in Latin letters ("translit"), most often with English-like digraphs (zh, kh, ch, sh, shch, ya, yu). Czech/scholarly-style transliteration (ž, ch, č, š, šč, ja, ju) also exists. Both are modelled in `variants/romanized_en/` and `variants/romanized_cs/` and discussed in `chalis.md`.

## Typological Summary

Russian is a fusional, synthetic language with six cases, three genders, grammatical aspect, no articles, and flexible SVO-default word order. See `grammar.md`.
