# Kyrgyz (Кыргыз тили)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Kyrgyz (Кыргыз тили / Кыргызча)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: kir
- **ISO 639-1 code**: ky
- **Language family**: Turkic > Common Turkic > Kipchak > Kipchak-Kyrgyz
- **Script**: Cyrillic (with additional letters Ң, Ө, Ү)
- **Number of speakers**: approximately 4.5 million
- **Primary regions**: Kyrgyzstan, China (Xinjiang), Uzbekistan, Tajikistan, Afghanistan, Kazakhstan, Turkey
- **Official status**: Official language of the Kyrgyz Republic (alongside Russian)

## Classification

Kyrgyz belongs to the Kipchak branch of the Turkic language family, which is itself part of the broader proposed Altaic grouping. Within the Kipchak branch, Kyrgyz is sometimes classified in its own sub-branch (Kipchak-Kyrgyz) or grouped with the Kypchak-Nogai languages. Its closest relatives are Kazakh, Karakalpak, and Nogai, though Kyrgyz and Kazakh share particularly high mutual intelligibility. The relationship between Kyrgyz and other Kipchak Turkic languages reflects centuries of shared history among the nomadic peoples of the Central Asian steppe.

## Geographic Distribution

The vast majority of Kyrgyz speakers reside in Kyrgyzstan, where the language serves as the state language. Significant diaspora communities exist in neighboring countries. In China's Xinjiang Uyghur Autonomous Region, approximately 200,000 ethnic Kyrgyz speak the language, often using an Arabic-based script rather than Cyrillic. Smaller Kyrgyz-speaking communities can be found in Uzbekistan (particularly in the Fergana Valley), Tajikistan (primarily in the northern Sughd province), Afghanistan (in the Wakhan Corridor and Badakhshan), and Kazakhstan. A notable community also exists in Turkey, descended from Kyrgyz who migrated in the mid-20th century.

## Official Status and Language Policy

Kyrgyz was declared the state language of the Kyrgyz Republic in 1989, during the final years of the Soviet Union, and this status was reaffirmed in the 1993 and 2010 constitutions. Russian holds the status of an official language, and it continues to play a significant role in government, business, and higher education, particularly in the capital Bishkek and in the north of the country. Since independence in 1991, the Kyrgyz government has pursued policies to promote the use of Kyrgyz in public life, education, and media, though the pace and intensity of these efforts have varied across different administrations.

## Historical Development

The Kyrgyz people are among the oldest Turkic peoples, with references in Chinese historical sources dating back to 201 BC. The Old Kyrgyz language is attested in Yenisei runic inscriptions from the 7th-10th centuries in what is now southern Siberia. After the migration of Kyrgyz tribes to the Tian Shan region (approximately 10th-15th centuries), the language evolved through contact with other Turkic languages as well as Mongolic and Iranian languages.

Before the 20th century, Kyrgyz was primarily an oral language, with the vast Manas epic and other oral literary traditions serving as the principal vehicles of cultural and linguistic transmission. A modified Arabic script was introduced in the early 20th century but was used only briefly. In 1928, as part of Soviet language reforms, a Latin-based alphabet was adopted. This was replaced in 1940 by the current Cyrillic-based alphabet, which includes 36 letters: the 33 letters of the Russian alphabet plus three additional characters (Ң/ң, Ө/ө, Ү/ү) to represent sounds not found in Russian.

## Sociolinguistic Situation

Modern Kyrgyz has several dialects, traditionally divided into northern and southern groups. The northern dialect forms the basis of the standard literary language. The southern dialects show greater influence from Uzbek and other neighboring languages. In contemporary Kyrgyzstan, code-switching between Kyrgyz and Russian is common, particularly among urban speakers. The language continues to develop its technical and scientific vocabulary, often adapting Russian or international loanwords while also drawing on native Turkic roots for neologisms.
