# Bosnian (bosanski jezik)

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Bosnian (bosanski / bosanski jezik)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** bos
- **ISO 639-1 code:** bs
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > South Slavic > Western South Slavic
- **Script:** Latin (primary), Cyrillic (historically used, now rare)
- **Approximate speakers:** ~3.5 million (native), with additional speakers in diaspora communities
- **Geographic regions:** Bosnia and Herzegovina (primary), Serbia (Sandzak region), Montenegro, Croatia, diaspora (Germany, Austria, Sweden, United States, Australia, Turkey)

## Official Status

Bosnian is one of three official languages of Bosnia and Herzegovina, alongside Croatian and Serbian. Under the Dayton Agreement (1995) and the constitution of Bosnia and Herzegovina, all three languages enjoy equal status. In practice, Bosnian is the primary language used by Bosniaks (Bosnian Muslims), though it is understood by all ethnic groups in the country. Bosnian is also recognized as a minority language in several neighboring countries, including Serbia, Montenegro, Kosovo, and North Macedonia.

## Historical Background

The history of Bosnian as a distinct standard language is inseparable from the broader history of the South Slavic linguistic area. During the medieval period, the language spoken in Bosnia was referred to by various names, including "Bosnian" (bosanski), as attested in historical documents from the Ottoman period and earlier. The famous Bosnian chronicler and traveler Evliya Celebi recorded the term "Bosna dili" (Bosnian language) in the 17th century, and the term "bosanski" appears in Franciscan manuscripts from the region.

During the 19th century, the standardization movement led by Vuk Stefanovic Karadzic and the Vienna Literary Agreement of 1850 sought to create a unified South Slavic literary language based on the Stokavian dialect. This standard, known as Serbo-Croatian (srpskohrvatski / hrvatskosrpski), served as the official language of Yugoslavia throughout the 20th century. Under this framework, regional variation was acknowledged but subsumed under a single language label with two primary variants: Eastern (Serbian-oriented, often ekavian) and Western (Croatian-oriented, always ijekavian).

The breakup of Yugoslavia in the early 1990s brought about significant linguistic and political changes. As Bosnia and Herzegovina declared independence in 1992, the question of linguistic identity became intertwined with national identity. Bosniaks, who had previously used the label "Serbo-Croatian" or sometimes "Croatian-Serbian," began to assert the name "Bosnian" (bosanski jezik) for their language. This was not merely a political relabeling but also an effort to codify certain features that had long characterized the speech of Bosniaks, particularly the retention of Turkish and Arabic loanwords, certain phonological features like h-retention, and the consistent use of the ijekavian reflex of the old Slavic vowel yat.

The first post-independence orthographic manual for Bosnian was published in 1996 by Senahid Halilovic, and subsequent grammars, dictionaries, and language planning efforts have further established Bosnian as a distinct standard language. The language is now taught in schools throughout the Federation of Bosnia and Herzegovina and in Bosniak-majority areas.

## Linguistic Classification

Bosnian belongs to the Western South Slavic branch of the Slavic language family. It is based on the Neo-Stokavian ijekavian dialect, which it shares with standard Croatian and Montenegrin (standard Serbian uses both ijekavian and ekavian, with ekavian being dominant in Serbia proper). The mutual intelligibility among Bosnian, Croatian, Serbian, and Montenegrin is very high, approaching 100% in everyday communication. Linguistically, these standards are often described as a pluricentric language — a single language system with multiple standard varieties — though this characterization is politically sensitive.

## Writing System

Bosnian primarily uses the Latin alphabet (Gajeva latinica), which was standardized by Ljudevit Gaj in the 19th century. The alphabet consists of 30 letters, including several digraphs (dz, lj, nj) and letters with diacritics (c with caron, c with acute, d with stroke, s with caron, z with caron). While the Cyrillic script (Vukova cirilica) is also constitutionally recognized in Bosnia and Herzegovina, its use among Bosniaks is minimal in practice, and Latin script dominates in all domains of Bosnian-language publishing, education, and media.
