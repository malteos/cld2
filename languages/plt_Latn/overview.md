# Plateau Malagasy (plt_Latn)

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Plateau Malagasy (Malagasy Ofiisialy / Malagasy standard)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** plt
- **ISO 639-1 code:** mg (covers Malagasy macrolanguage)
- **Language family:** Austronesian > Malayo-Polynesian > Greater Barito > East Barito
- **Script:** Latin
- **Number of speakers:** Approximately 10 million native speakers; understood by the majority of Madagascar's ~28 million inhabitants as a lingua franca
- **Primary regions:** Central highlands of Madagascar (Imerina, Betsileo regions)
- **Official status:** Basis of official/standard Malagasy, one of Madagascar's official languages alongside French

## Geographic Distribution

Plateau Malagasy is spoken natively across the central highlands of Madagascar, particularly in and around the capital Antananarivo (formerly Tananarive), the historic Imerina heartland, and extending into the Betsileo region to the south. As the prestige dialect and foundation of Standard Malagasy, it is used nationwide in government, education, media, and formal communication. Significant diaspora communities exist in France, Reunion, Comoros, and other parts of the Indian Ocean region.

## Historical Background

The origins of Malagasy are remarkable in the context of African linguistics. Unlike the Bantu and other African language families that surround Madagascar geographically, Malagasy belongs to the Austronesian family, specifically the Greater Barito subgroup. Linguistic and genetic evidence points to a migration from southeastern Borneo (present-day Indonesian Kalimantan) around the 5th to 7th century CE. The closest linguistic relative of Malagasy is Ma'anyan, a language spoken in the interior of southern Borneo.

These early Austronesian settlers arrived in Madagascar via maritime routes across the Indian Ocean, likely with stops along the East African coast and possibly through established trade networks. Over centuries, the language absorbed loanwords from Bantu languages (particularly Swahili), Arabic (through trade contacts), Sanskrit (via earlier Indianized Southeast Asian contact), and later from French and English during the colonial period.

The Merina kingdom, centered in the central highlands around Antananarivo, rose to prominence in the 18th and 19th centuries under rulers such as King Andrianampoinimerina and Queen Ranavalona I. The political dominance of the Merina people led to their dialect becoming the prestige variety. When Christian missionaries, particularly from the London Missionary Society, arrived in the early 19th century, they chose the Merina dialect for Bible translation and literacy campaigns. David Jones and David Griffiths standardized the Latin-based orthography in the 1820s, establishing conventions still largely in use today.

During the French colonial period (1896-1960), French became the dominant administrative language, but Malagasy continued as the language of daily life. After independence in 1960, Plateau Malagasy was re-established as an official language. The malgachisation policies of the 1970s under President Didier Ratsiraka promoted Malagasy in education and government, further cementing the Merina-based standard.

## Sociolinguistic Status

Plateau Malagasy occupies a complex sociolinguistic position. It is simultaneously a regional dialect (of the Merina and Betsileo peoples), the national standard language, and a lingua franca across Madagascar's diverse ethnic groups. This creates tensions with speakers of other Malagasy varieties (Sakalava, Tsimihety, Antandroy, Betsimisaraka, and others) who may view the standard as imposing highland cultural hegemony. Despite these tensions, Plateau Malagasy remains the primary medium for national media, literature, and education, making it the most widely understood variety across the island.

## Writing System

The language uses the standard Latin alphabet without diacritics. The orthography is largely phonemic and was standardized in the early 19th century. Notable features include the use of the apostrophe within words (as in amin'ny, "at the") and the frequent occurrence of long words due to the agglutinative morphology.
