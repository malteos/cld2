# Basque (Euskara)

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Basque (endonym: Euskara)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** eus
- **Language family:** Language isolate (no known genetic relatives)
- **Script:** Latin
- **Native speakers:** Approximately 750,000
- **Total speakers:** Approximately 1.1 million (including L2 speakers)
- **Geographic distribution:** Basque Country (Euskal Herria), spanning northern Spain and southwestern France

## Geographic Regions

Basque is spoken across seven historical provinces collectively known as Euskal Herria (the Basque Country). In Spain, these provinces are Bizkaia (Vizcaya), Gipuzkoa (Guipuzcoa), Araba (Alava), and Nafarroa (Navarra). In France, the three northern Basque provinces are Lapurdi (Labourd), Zuberoa (Soule), and Nafarroa Beherea (Basse-Navarre). The strongest concentrations of speakers are found in Gipuzkoa and parts of Bizkaia and Nafarroa, where Basque is used extensively in daily life, education, media, and public administration.

## Official Status

Basque holds co-official status alongside Spanish in the Basque Autonomous Community (encompassing Bizkaia, Gipuzkoa, and Araba) and in parts of the Chartered Community of Navarre, where language policy varies by linguistic zone. In the French Basque Country, Basque has no official status, though regional authorities have increasingly supported its use in education and cultural life. The Basque Government actively promotes the language through policies in education, media, and public services.

## Historical Background

Basque is one of the most remarkable languages in Europe, being the sole surviving pre-Indo-European language on the continent. Its origins remain a subject of scholarly debate, with no widely accepted genetic relationship to any other known language family, living or extinct. Numerous hypotheses have been proposed linking Basque to Iberian, Caucasian languages, Berber, and others, but none have gained consensus among linguists.

The language survived through successive waves of cultural and political change that transformed the rest of the Iberian Peninsula and western Europe. During the Roman period, Latin replaced most indigenous languages in Iberia and Gaul, yet Basque persisted in its mountainous homeland, though it absorbed a substantial layer of Latin vocabulary. The subsequent Germanic invasions by Visigoths and Franks, followed by the Arab and Berber conquest of much of Iberia, again reshaped the linguistic landscape around the Basque-speaking area without displacing the language itself. Throughout the medieval period, Basque coexisted with Romance languages, gradually ceding ground in urban centers and lowland areas while maintaining strength in rural and mountainous regions.

The modern era brought new challenges and opportunities for Basque. Under the Franco dictatorship in Spain (1939-1975), the language was severely repressed, banned from public life, education, and official use. This period saw a significant decline in the number of speakers and the social prestige of the language. However, clandestine efforts to maintain and teach Basque continued, including the establishment of ikastolak (Basque-medium schools) operating outside the official education system.

## Standardization and Revitalization

Following the end of the Franco regime, the revitalization of Basque became a central cultural and political goal. In 1968, the Royal Academy of the Basque Language (Euskaltzaindia) proposed Euskara Batua (Unified Basque), a standardized literary form of the language designed to bridge the significant dialectal differences across regions. Batua draws primarily from the central dialects but incorporates elements from across the dialect spectrum. Its adoption in education, media, and administration has been instrumental in the language's recovery.

The euskaltegi system of language schools for adults has played a vital role in creating new speakers, particularly among those who did not acquire Basque in childhood. Combined with the expansion of Basque-medium education at all levels, from preschool through university, these efforts have resulted in a steady increase in the number of speakers, especially among younger generations. Today, Basque-language media, including television (ETB), radio, newspapers (Berria), and digital platforms, contribute to a vibrant linguistic ecosystem that supports the continued use and development of the language.
