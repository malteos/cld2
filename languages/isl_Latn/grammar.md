# Icelandic Grammar

## Word Order

Icelandic has a basic SVO (Subject-Verb-Object) word order but follows the V2 (verb-second) constraint common to Germanic languages. The finite verb must appear in the second position of main clauses, regardless of what occupies the first position. When an element other than the subject is fronted (topicalized), the subject and verb invert:

- **Eg las bokina** (I read the book) - standard SVO
- **Bokina las eg** (The book read I) - object fronted, V2 maintained
- **I gaer las eg bokina** (Yesterday read I the book) - adverb fronted

In subordinate clauses, the word order shifts: the negation and sentence adverbs precede the finite verb rather than following it.

## Nouns and Case System

Icelandic retains the Old Norse four-case system, which has been lost in the continental Scandinavian languages:

1. **Nominative (nefnifall):** Subject of the sentence
2. **Accusative (tholfal):** Direct object, certain prepositions
3. **Dative (thagufall):** Indirect object, certain prepositions, certain verb complements
4. **Genitive (eignarfall):** Possession, certain prepositions, partitive

Nouns are classified by three genders: **masculine**, **feminine**, and **neuter**. Each noun belongs to one of these genders inherently, and gender determines the declension pattern. There are two numbers: **singular** and **plural**.

### Strong and Weak Declension

Nouns follow either strong or weak declension patterns:

- **Strong declension:** Nouns that do not end in a vowel in the nominative singular (e.g., *hestur* "horse," *kona* "woman," *barn* "child"). These show more variation across case forms.
- **Weak declension:** Nouns that end in a vowel in the nominative singular (e.g., *tunga* "tongue," *hjarta* "heart"). These tend to have more uniform endings.

There are numerous sub-classes within each declension type, making the Icelandic noun system one of the most complex in modern Germanic languages.

## Definite Article

Icelandic has a suffixed definite article, appended directly to the noun. The article inflects for gender, number, and case:

- **Masculine:** -inn (nom. sg.), e.g., *hesturinn* (the horse)
- **Feminine:** -in (nom. sg.), e.g., *konan* (the woman)
- **Neuter:** -id (nom. sg.), e.g., *barnid* (the child)

The indefinite article does not exist in Icelandic; bare nouns are understood as indefinite.

A free-standing demonstrative article (*hinn/hin/hid*) is used before adjectives in certain constructions.

## Adjectives

Adjectives agree with the noun they modify in gender, number, and case. They have two declension patterns:

- **Strong declension:** Used when the adjective modifies an indefinite noun (e.g., *stor borg* "a big city").
- **Weak declension:** Used when the adjective modifies a definite noun, follows a demonstrative, or follows a possessive (e.g., *stora borgin* "the big city").

Adjectives have comparative and superlative forms, both regular (with suffixes *-ari/-astur*) and irregular.

## Verbs

The Icelandic verb system is highly complex and retains many archaic features from Old Norse.

### Strong and Weak Verbs

- **Strong verbs:** Change their stem vowel to indicate tense (ablaut), similar to English "sing/sang/sung." There are seven classes of strong verbs in Icelandic (e.g., *bita/beit/bitum/bitid* "bite").
- **Weak verbs:** Form the past tense with a dental suffix (-di, -ti, -adi). There are four classes of weak verbs.

### Tense and Mood

Icelandic verbs conjugate for:
- **Person:** 1st, 2nd, 3rd
- **Number:** Singular, plural
- **Tense:** Present, past (compound tenses formed with auxiliaries)
- **Mood:** Indicative, subjunctive, imperative

The **subjunctive mood** is far more vital in Icelandic than in other Scandinavian languages. It is used in indirect speech, wishes, hypothetical clauses, purpose clauses, and after certain conjunctions and verbs.

### Middle Voice

Icelandic preserves the Old Norse middle voice, formed by adding the suffix **-st** (from the reflexive pronoun *sik*) to the verb. The middle voice can express:

- **Reflexive meaning:** *klaeadst* (to dress oneself)
- **Reciprocal meaning:** *hittast* (to meet each other)
- **Passive meaning:** *opnast* (to be opened)
- **Change of meaning:** *finnast* (to think/feel, vs. *finna* "to find")

### Impersonal Constructions

Icelandic makes extensive use of impersonal constructions where the subject is not in the nominative case:

- **Mig vantar peninga** (I need money) - literally "me lacks money" (accusative subject)
- **Mer finnst thetta gott** (I think this is good) - literally "me finds this good" (dative subject)
- **Mig dreymdi** (I dreamed) - accusative subject

These constructions are a hallmark of Icelandic grammar and distinguish it from most other European languages.

## Pronouns

Personal pronouns inflect for case, and Icelandic retains a distinction between second person singular (*thu*) and plural (*thid*). There is no formal/informal distinction (unlike German or French). Icelandic also has a reflexive pronoun (*sig/ser/sin*) used for third-person reference back to the subject.

## Prepositions

Prepositions in Icelandic govern specific cases. Some prepositions can govern different cases with different meanings:

- **i** + accusative = motion into (*i husid* "into the house")
- **i** + dative = location (*i husinu* "in the house")

This case-dependent meaning distinction adds complexity to the prepositional system.
