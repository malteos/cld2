# Icelandic (Islenska) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Icelandic (Islenska)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** isl
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** is
- **Script:** Latin (with special characters)
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Germanic > North Germanic > West Scandinavian > Insular Scandinavian
- **Native Speakers:** ~350,000
- **Primary Region:** Iceland
- **Official Status:** Official language of Iceland

## Classification

Icelandic belongs to the North Germanic (Scandinavian) branch of the Indo-European language family. Within North Germanic, it is classified as an Insular Scandinavian language, along with Faroese. This subgroup is distinguished from the Continental Scandinavian languages (Norwegian, Swedish, Danish) by having diverged geographically and linguistically due to the settlement of Iceland and the Faroe Islands by Norse settlers in the 9th and 10th centuries.

Faroese is the closest living relative of Icelandic, though the two are not mutually intelligible in spoken form. Written Icelandic, however, remains remarkably close to Old Norse, the common ancestor of all North Germanic languages, and educated Icelanders can read Old Norse texts from the medieval period with relative ease.

## Historical Background

Icelandic descends from the Old Norse dialect spoken by the Norwegian settlers who colonized Iceland beginning around 874 CE. Due to Iceland's geographic isolation in the North Atlantic, the language has undergone fewer changes than any other Scandinavian language. While continental Scandinavian languages simplified their grammar considerably during the medieval and early modern periods, Icelandic retained most of the complex inflectional morphology of Old Norse.

The rich literary tradition of medieval Iceland, including the Eddas and the Icelandic sagas (Islendingasogur), was composed in a language that differs only slightly from modern Icelandic. This literary heritage has been a powerful force in linguistic conservatism.

## Language Purism

One of the most distinctive sociolinguistic features of Icelandic is its strong tradition of language purism (malhreinsunarstef). Rather than borrowing words from other languages, particularly English, Icelandic creates neologisms from native roots and existing morphological patterns. This practice is overseen by the Arni Magnusson Institute for Icelandic Studies and is broadly supported by Icelandic society.

Notable examples include:
- **simi** (telephone) - from an Old Norse word meaning "thread"
- **tolva** (computer) - a blend of "tala" (number) and "volva" (prophetess)
- **sjonfarpid** (television) - literally "sight-traveler"
- **thyrla** (helicopter) - from "thyrill" (whirl)
- **geimfari** (astronaut) - literally "space-traveler"

## Script and Orthography

Icelandic uses the Latin alphabet augmented with several special characters: a, d (eth), e, i, o, u, y, th (thorn), ae, and o with diaeresis. The letters eth (d) and thorn (th) are inherited from Old English and Old Norse writing traditions and are unique to Icelandic among modern languages. The Icelandic alphabet consists of 32 letters, and spelling has remained relatively stable since the standardization efforts of the 19th century.

## Current Status

Icelandic is the dominant language of Iceland and is used in all domains of public life, education, media, government, and literature. Despite its small number of speakers, the language is well-supported with extensive digital resources, a thriving publishing industry, and strong institutional backing. The Icelandic Language Council (Islensk malnefd) actively works to maintain and develop the language in the face of increasing English influence, particularly in technology and popular culture.
