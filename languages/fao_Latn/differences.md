# Faroese (fao_Latn) -- Differences from Related Languages

## Overview

Faroese belongs to the Insular Scandinavian (West Nordic) branch alongside Icelandic, both descended from Old West Norse. However, centuries of Danish colonial rule (1380--1948) introduced significant Danish lexical influence. The language must be carefully distinguished from Icelandic, Danish, Norwegian (especially Nynorsk), and Old Norse texts.

## Faroese vs. Icelandic (Highest Confusion Risk)

Icelandic is the most confusable language with Faroese. Both are Insular Scandinavian languages that preserve Old Norse morphology, four-case noun declension, and three grammatical genders. Key differentiators:

### The ð + ø diagnostic

The single most reliable differentiator is the character inventory. Faroese uses **ø** where Icelandic uses **ö**. Both languages use **ð** (eth), but only Faroese combines ð with ø. If a text contains both ð and ø, it is almost certainly Faroese. If it contains ð and ö (but no ø), it is almost certainly Icelandic.

### Skerping

Faroese underwent a phonological process called **skerping** (sharpening), in which certain Old Norse long vowels were diphthongized or shifted. This produced distinctive Faroese spellings:
- Old Norse *a* > Faroese **ea** in certain environments (e.g., ON *vatn* > Faroese *vatn* but ON *lata* > Faroese *lata*; the diphthongization patterns differ from Icelandic)
- Short vowels before *nk, ng* were raised and fronted, producing spellings like **einkis** (nothing/genitive) vs. Icelandic **einkis**
- Faroese has distinctive diphthongs **ei, ey, oy** that map differently from their Icelandic counterparts

Skerping affects words such as: *genta* (girl, cf. Icelandic *stulka*), *reingja* vs. Icelandic forms, *keypti* (bought) vs. Icelandic *keypti*.

### Consonant shifts

Faroese underwent consonant changes that Icelandic did not:
- Pre-aspiration and geminate simplification in certain environments
- Loss of initial /h/ before /l, n, r/ is shared, but other consonant cluster developments differ
- Faroese **hv-** is pronounced differently from Icelandic **hv-**, though the spelling is often retained in both

### Vocabulary differences

Despite shared morphological structure, many common words differ:
- Faroese **ikki** "not" vs. Icelandic **ekki**
- Faroese **hon** "she" vs. Icelandic **hun**
- Faroese **hevur** "has" vs. Icelandic **hefur**
- Faroese **verdur** "becomes" vs. Icelandic **verdur** (similar but vowel context differs)
- Faroese **tad** "it" vs. Icelandic **thad** (different orthographic convention for eth)
- Faroese **oyggj** "island" vs. Icelandic **ey**

### Pronoun system

Faroese distinguishes three genders in the third-person plural: **teir** (m.), **taer** (f.), **tey** (n./mixed). Icelandic uses **their** (m.), **thaer** (f.), **thau** (n./mixed) -- the forms are cognate but orthographically distinct due to the different vowel systems.

## Faroese vs. Danish

Danish was the language of administration, church, and education in the Faroe Islands for centuries. As a result:

### Lexical borrowing

Faroese has many Danish loanwords, especially in administrative, ecclesiastical, and modern vocabulary. Words like **regering** (government), **forseti** (president/chairman), and various technical terms may overlap with Danish.

### Morphological divergence

Despite lexical overlap, Faroese retains full nominal case inflection (four cases, three genders) while Danish has reduced to common/neuter gender with no case marking on nouns. The presence of **-ur, -an, -anum, -ans** case endings immediately distinguishes Faroese from Danish.

### Character inventory

Danish uses **ae, o, a** but lacks **d** (eth). Any text containing ð is definitively not Danish. Danish also uses ø, so ø alone is not diagnostic -- but ð is an absolute differentiator.

### Phonological spelling differences

Danish and Faroese diverge sharply in how words are spelled even when cognate:
- Faroese **hus** vs. Danish **hus** (same spelling, different pronunciation)
- Faroese **bokur** vs. Danish **boger** (books)
- Faroese **dagur** vs. Danish **dag** (day -- Faroese retains the -ur ending)

## Faroese vs. Norwegian (Nynorsk)

Norwegian Nynorsk, being based on western Norwegian dialects, shares some features with Faroese due to common West Norse ancestry.

### Shared features that cause confusion

Both Nynorsk and Faroese use forms closer to Old Norse than Bokmal or Danish. Nynorsk uses **-ar** plurals, **-ane** definite plurals, and vocabulary that sometimes aligns with Faroese rather than Danish.

### Key differentiators

- Nynorsk lacks **ð** entirely -- this is an absolute differentiator
- Nynorsk uses **o** (not ø) in some positions where Faroese uses ø, though both languages do use ø
- Nynorsk has lost case inflection on nouns (except possessive -s), while Faroese retains four cases
- Faroese **-ur** endings vs. Nynorsk **-ar, -er** endings
- Nynorsk uses **ikkje** for "not" vs. Faroese **ikki**
- Nynorsk **ho** "she" vs. Faroese **hon**

## Faroese vs. Old Norse

Faroese descends directly from Old West Norse, and the conservative orthography means some texts can superficially resemble Old Norse.

### Key differentiators

- Old Norse uses **th** (thorn) where Faroese uses other conventions
- Old Norse lacks **ø**, using instead **o** (o-caudata, often rendered ö in normalized editions)
- Old Norse has distinct vowel length marking conventions
- Old Norse vocabulary and syntax differ in many high-frequency words
- The Faroese function word inventory (ikki, hevur, verdur, etc.) immediately marks a text as modern Faroese rather than Old Norse

## Summary of Diagnostic Features

| Feature | Faroese | Icelandic | Danish | Nynorsk |
|---------|---------|-----------|--------|---------|
| Uses ð | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| Uses ø | Yes | No (uses ö) | Yes | Yes |
| ð + ø together | Yes | No | No | No |
| Case inflection | 4 cases | 4 cases | None | Minimal |
| -ur nom. ending | Yes | Yes | No | No |
| "not" | ikki | ekki | ikke | ikkje |
| "she" | hon | hun | hun | ho |
| "has" | hevur | hefur | har | har |
| Three-gender plural | teir/taer/tey | their/thaer/thau | de | dei |

The co-occurrence of **ð + ø** remains the fastest and most reliable single test for identifying Faroese text and distinguishing it from all other languages.
