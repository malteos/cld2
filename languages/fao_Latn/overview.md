## Classification

Faroese belongs to the **North Germanic** (Scandinavian) branch of the Indo-European language family. Within that branch it is classified as **West Scandinavian**, also referred to as **Insular Scandinavian**, alongside Icelandic and the extinct Norn language once spoken in Orkney and Shetland. The West Scandinavian group descended from Old West Norse, diverging from the East Scandinavian branch (Danish, Swedish) during the medieval period. Of the living North Germanic languages, Faroese is most closely related to Icelandic, though the two are not mutually intelligible in spoken form. Written Faroese, however, retains enough archaic features that literate Icelanders can often grasp its meaning.

## Geographic Distribution

Faroese is spoken primarily on the **Faroe Islands** (Foroyar), an archipelago of 18 islands situated in the North Atlantic between Iceland, Norway, and Scotland. The islands are a self-governing territory within the Kingdom of Denmark. Nearly all of the roughly 54,000 inhabitants of the Faroe Islands speak Faroese as their first language. A significant diaspora community exists in **Denmark**, particularly in Copenhagen, where several thousand Faroese speakers reside. Smaller pockets of speakers can be found in other Nordic countries and beyond, but the language remains overwhelmingly concentrated in the Faroe Islands themselves.

## Official Status

Faroese is the **official language of the Faroe Islands**, a status it shares with Danish. The 1948 Home Rule Act recognized Faroese as the principal language ("hovuosmali") of the islands, while Danish was designated as an official second language that must be taught in schools. In practice, Faroese dominates daily life, government, media, and education on the islands. Danish is used primarily for communication with the Danish state and is taught as a compulsory subject in schools from the third grade onward. The Faroese parliament (Logting) conducts all business in Faroese.

## History

### Old Norse Origins

The Faroe Islands were settled by Norse Vikings in the 9th century, though Irish monks may have inhabited the islands earlier. The settlers brought Old Norse with them, and the language evolved in relative isolation due to the islands' remote location. The name "Faroe" itself likely derives from Old Norse *far* (sheep) and *oy* (island), reflecting the pastoral economy of the early settlers.

### Medieval Period and the Sheep Letter

The oldest known document written in the Faroe Islands is the **Seydabraevio** (Sheep Letter) of 1298, a legal text regulating sheep farming and land use. This document, written in Old Norse, provides valuable evidence of the language as it was spoken in the medieval Faroes. During the medieval period, Faroese began to diverge more markedly from other West Norse varieties, developing distinctive phonological features such as svarabhakti vowel insertion and pre-aspiration of stops.

### Danish Colonial Period and Language Suppression

Following the Reformation in 1538, Danish replaced Faroese as the language of the church, law, and administration. For roughly three centuries, Faroese had no written standard and existed almost exclusively as a spoken vernacular. Danish was the sole language of education, religious services, and official documents. This prolonged period of diglossia threatened the survival of Faroese, reducing it to a collection of oral dialects with no literary tradition beyond ballads and folk tales passed down through generations.

### 19th-Century Revival

The revival of Faroese as a written language began in earnest in the mid-19th century. The pivotal figure was **Venceslaus Ulricus Hammershaimb** (V.U. Hammershaimb), a Faroese linguist and clergyman who devised a standardized orthography for Faroese in 1846. Hammershaimb chose an etymological spelling system rooted in Old Norse forms rather than a purely phonetic approach, which gave written Faroese a pan-dialectal character and connected it visibly to its medieval heritage. This orthographic decision remains fundamental to the language's written form today.

### Modern Era

The **Faroese Language Committee** (Foroyska malstovan, later Foroyska malnevndin) was established to oversee language planning, terminology development, and the standardization of Faroese. Throughout the 20th century, Faroese steadily reclaimed domains from Danish: it was introduced into schools (first as a subject, then as the medium of instruction), adopted in the church, and used in expanding print and broadcast media. Today, Faroese enjoys robust institutional support, a thriving literary culture, and active use in digital media, though its small speaker population means it remains a language whose long-term vitality demands conscious cultivation.
