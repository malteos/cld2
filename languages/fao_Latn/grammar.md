# Faroese Grammar

## Typological Overview

Faroese is a **fusional** (inflectional) language with a moderately complex morphological system. It preserves much of the grammatical structure inherited from Old Norse, including a four-case noun system, three grammatical genders, and a distinction between strong and weak declension and conjugation patterns. In terms of basic word order, Faroese is an **SVO** (Subject-Verb-Object) language but strictly observes the **verb-second (V2)** constraint in main clauses, a feature shared with the other mainland Scandinavian languages and German.

## Phonology

### Vowel System

Faroese possesses a **rich vowel system** that is notably more complex than those of Danish, Norwegian, or Swedish. The language distinguishes a large inventory of monophthongs and **diphthongs**. Many historically long vowels have undergone diphthongization, producing a system where diphthongs are pervasive in everyday speech. Common diphthongs include /ai/, /oi/, /ui/, /ei/, /ou/, and /au/. Vowel quality is affected by the consonantal environment, particularly by following retroflex or palatal consonants.

Short and long vowel distinctions are phonemic, and vowel length interacts systematically with syllable structure: stressed syllables in Faroese follow a quantity pattern where either the vowel or the following consonant (but not both) is long.

### Svarabhakti Vowels

A distinctive feature of Faroese phonology is the insertion of **svarabhakti vowels** (epenthetic vowels) between certain consonant clusters, particularly between a sonorant and a following stop or fricative. For example, the word *barn* (child) is realized approximately as [badin] in many dialects, with an epenthetic vowel breaking up the /rn/ cluster. This phenomenon is one of the most immediately recognizable features of spoken Faroese.

### Pre-aspiration

Faroese exhibits **pre-aspiration** of voiceless stops /p, t, k/ when they occur after a vowel, a feature it shares with Icelandic and some dialects of Norwegian and Scottish Gaelic. In pre-aspirated stops, a brief period of breathiness or [h]-like friction precedes the stop closure. This means that a word like *hoppa* (to jump) is pronounced with audible aspiration before the /pp/ closure rather than after it.

### Glottalization

In certain phonological environments, Faroese consonants may undergo **glottalization**, where a glottal constriction accompanies or replaces the oral articulation. This is particularly evident in some dialects and in specific consonant clusters. Glottalization interacts with the pre-aspiration system, contributing to the overall complexity of Faroese consonant phonetics.

## Noun System

### Gender

Faroese nouns belong to one of **three grammatical genders**: masculine, feminine, and neuter. Gender assignment is partly semantically motivated (biological sex often correlates with grammatical gender for animate nouns) but is largely arbitrary for inanimate nouns and must be learned lexically. Gender determines the form of accompanying articles, adjectives, and pronouns.

### Case

The language retains the **four-case system** inherited from Old Norse:

| Case         | Function                                                       | Example (masc. *batur* = boat) |
|--------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|-------------------------------|
| Nominative   | Subject, predicate complement                                  | batur                         |
| Accusative   | Direct object, certain prepositions                            | bat                           |
| Dative       | Indirect object, many prepositions, certain verb complements   | bati                          |
| Genitive     | Possession, partitive, certain fixed expressions               | bats                          |

The genitive is the least productive case in modern Faroese; possession is more commonly expressed using prepositional constructions with *hja* or *av*, though the genitive remains in fixed expressions, with certain verbs, and in formal or literary registers.

### Strong and Weak Declension

Nouns follow either **strong** or **weak** declension patterns. Strong nouns exhibit a wider range of stem changes and endings across the four cases and two numbers (singular, plural). Weak nouns have more uniform endings, typically showing *-a* or *-i* in oblique cases. The distinction between strong and weak declension interacts with gender, producing a matrix of paradigms that learners must internalize.

Examples of strong masculine singular: *hestur* (horse) -- nom. *hestur*, acc. *hest*, dat. *hesti*, gen. *hests*.

Examples of weak masculine singular: *bogi* (bow) -- nom. *bogi*, acc. *boga*, dat. *boga*, gen. *boga*.

### Definite Article

The definite article in Faroese is **suffixed** to the noun, as in the other Scandinavian languages. The form of the suffix varies by gender, case, and number. For example, *hestur* (horse) becomes *hesturin* (the horse) in the nominative singular. An independent prenominal definite article (*tann, ta, tao*) is used when the noun is modified by an adjective, yielding constructions such as *tann stori hesturin* (the big horse), which features double definiteness marking.

## Verb System

### Strong and Weak Conjugation

Faroese verbs are divided into **strong** and **weak** conjugation classes. Strong verbs form their past tense through ablaut (vowel gradation in the stem), following patterns inherited from Old Norse and ultimately from Proto-Germanic. For instance, *bita* (to bite): present *eg biti*, past *eg beit*. Weak verbs form the past tense by adding a dental suffix (*-di*, *-ti*, or *-adhi*), analogous to the English *-ed* ending. For example, *kalla* (to call): present *eg kalli*, past *eg kalladhi*.

### Tense

The primary synthetic tenses are **present** and **past** (preterite). Faroese also constructs compound tenses using auxiliaries:

- **Perfect**: *hava* (have) + past participle -- *eg havi etio* (I have eaten)
- **Pluperfect**: *havdhi* (had) + past participle -- *eg havdhi etio* (I had eaten)
- **Future**: typically expressed with the present tense plus a temporal adverb, or with modal verbs such as *skulu* (shall) or *munnu* (will)

### Subjunctive

Faroese retains a **subjunctive mood**, though its use has diminished compared to Old Norse. The subjunctive appears in certain subordinate clauses expressing wishes, hypothetical conditions, indirect speech, and purpose. It is more common in formal and literary registers. Both present and past subjunctive forms exist, distinguished by their endings: weak verbs typically use *-i* for the present subjunctive and modify the dental suffix for the past subjunctive.

### Imperative

The **imperative** mood is used for commands and requests. It is formed from the verb stem, typically without any ending in the singular: *far!* (go!), *kom!* (come!). The plural imperative adds *-io*: *fario!* (go!), *komio!* (come!).

### Middle Voice

A notable feature of Faroese (shared with Icelandic and, historically, Old Norse) is the **middle voice**, formed by adding the suffix **-st** to the verb. The middle voice conveys a range of meanings including reflexive action, reciprocal action, and passive meaning. For example:

- *vaska* (to wash) > *vaskast* (to wash oneself) -- reflexive
- *hittast* (to meet each other, from *hitta*, to meet) -- reciprocal
- *byggjast* (to be built, from *byggja*, to build) -- passive/mediopassive

The *-st* suffix derives historically from the Old Norse reflexive pronoun *sik* (itself), which became cliticized and eventually fused with the verb stem.

## Syntax

### Verb-Second (V2) Word Order

In **main clauses**, Faroese strictly observes **V2 word order**: the finite verb must occupy the second position in the clause, regardless of what fills the first position. If a constituent other than the subject is fronted (topicalized), the subject and verb invert:

- *Eg lesi bokina.* (I read the book.) -- subject first, SVO
- *Bokina lesi eg.* (The book, I read.) -- object fronted, V2 maintained through subject-verb inversion
- *I gjari lesi eg bokina.* (Yesterday I read the book.) -- adverbial fronted, inversion

### Subordinate Clause Word Order

In **subordinate clauses**, the V2 constraint is relaxed, and sentence adverbs such as *ikki* (not) are placed **before** the finite verb rather than after it (as they would be in main clauses). This distinction between main and subordinate clause word order parallels the pattern found in other V2 Germanic languages:

- Main clause: *Eg lesi ikki bokina.* (I do not read the book.) -- negation after verb
- Subordinate clause: *...at eg ikki lesi bokina.* (...that I do not read the book.) -- negation before verb

### Expletive Subjects

Faroese makes use of **expletive (dummy) subjects**, most commonly *tao* (corresponding to English "it" or "there"), in constructions where no semantic subject is present or where the logical subject is postposed:

- *Tao regnar.* (It rains.) -- weather expression
- *Tao eru nokurir menninir a gotiini.* (There are some men in the street.) -- existential construction

The expletive subject is required because Faroese, like other Scandinavian languages, does not permit the verb to appear in absolute initial position in declarative sentences (a consequence of the V2 constraint). The expletive fills the first position so that the finite verb can occupy the second.

### Agreement

Verbs agree with their subjects in **person and number**. Adjectives agree with the nouns they modify in **gender, number, and case**, and further distinguish between strong (indefinite) and weak (definite) declension forms. This system of agreement, while complex, is regular enough to be largely predictable once the underlying paradigms are learned.
