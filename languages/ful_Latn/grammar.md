# Fula / Fulfulde (ful_Latn) - Grammar

## Word Order

Fula follows a basic **Subject-Verb-Object (SVO)** word order. The subject precedes the verb, and the direct object follows it. Indirect objects typically follow the direct object, often introduced by a preposition. Adjectives and demonstratives follow the noun they modify, while possessives also follow the noun. Question words generally appear in situ or at the beginning of a clause.

Example structure: *Gorko oo yahi luumo* ("The man went to the market") -- Subject (gorko oo) + Verb (yahi) + Object/Destination (luumo).

## Morphological Type

Fula is a highly **agglutinative** language with a rich and complex morphological system. Its most distinctive typological feature is an elaborate **noun class system** that pervades virtually every aspect of the grammar. Nouns, adjectives, pronouns, demonstratives, and numerals all agree in class, and the class system operates through a mechanism of **initial consonant alternation** (also called consonant mutation or grade alternation), which is cross-linguistically rare and a hallmark of the Atlantic language family.

## Phonology

### Consonants

Fula has a rich consonant inventory that includes several typologically notable features:

**Prenasalized stops**: Fula has a series of prenasalized consonants including mb, nd, nj, ng, which contrast with their plain stop counterparts. These prenasalized stops are particularly frequent and are a characteristic feature of the language's sound system.

**Implosive consonants**: Fula possesses implosive stops at the bilabial (ɓ), alveolar (ɗ), and palatal (ƴ) places of articulation. These are produced with an ingressive airstream and are phonemically distinct from their plosive counterparts. They are written with hooked letters in the standard orthography and are among the most distinctive sounds of the language.

**Gemination**: Consonant gemination (lengthening) is phonemically contrastive in Fula. Geminate consonants are written as double letters (bb, dd, ff, ll, mm, nn, etc.) and contrast with their singleton counterparts. For example, *fella* vs. *fela* are distinguished by consonant length. Gemination interacts with the noun class system and consonant alternation.

**Other consonants**: The inventory includes voiceless stops (p, t, c, k), voiced stops (b, d, j, g), fricatives (f, s, h), nasals (m, n, ɲ, ŋ), liquids (l, r), the glides (w, y), and the glottal stop ('). The contrast between r and l is maintained, and both can be geminated.

### Vowels

Fula has a five-vowel system (a, e, i, o, u) with a **phonemic vowel length distinction**. Long vowels are written as double vowels (aa, ee, ii, oo, uu) and contrast meaningfully with short vowels. Vowel length is lexically and grammatically significant, playing a role in noun class marking and verbal morphology. For example, *reedu* (stomach) vs. *redu* would differ in meaning based on vowel length.

### Syllable Structure

The predominant syllable structure is CV (consonant-vowel) or CVC. Consonant clusters are rare within syllables but arise at morpheme boundaries. Word-final consonants are common, particularly in verb stems and noun suffixes.

## Noun Class System

The noun class system is the **most distinctive and complex feature** of Fula grammar. There are approximately **25 noun classes** (the exact number varies by dialect and analysis), organized into singular-plural pairings. Each class is associated with:

1. **A class suffix** on the noun (e.g., -o, -el, -ol, -al, -nde, -ngal, -nge, -ngol, -ndu, -ka, -ngel, -kal, -ɗam, -ɗe, -ɗi, -ko, -kon, -koy)
2. **An initial consonant alternation pattern** that affects the root
3. **Agreement patterns** on adjectives, demonstratives, pronouns, and verbs

### Initial Consonant Alternation

This is the defining morphological process of Fula. Nouns and other words undergo systematic changes in their initial consonant depending on grammatical context (typically singular vs. plural, but also in other derivational processes). There are three main **grades**:

- **Grade 1 (continuant/fricative)**: w-, y-, r-, s-, f-, h- (typically associated with singular of certain classes)
- **Grade 2 (stop)**: g-, j-, d-, c-, p-, k- (typically associated with plural or other class forms)
- **Grade 3 (nasal/prenasalized)**: ng-, nj-, nd-, nc-, mb-, nk- (typically diminutive or other derived classes)

For example, the word for "Fulani person" alternates: *Pullo* (singular) / *Fulɓe* (plural) -- note the p/f alternation. Similarly: *wuro* (town, singular) / *gure* (towns, plural) -- w/g alternation; *rawaandu* (dog, singular) / *dawaadi* (dogs, plural) -- r/d alternation.

## Verb System

### Verbal Extensions

Fula has an extensive system of **verbal extensions** (also called verbal suffixes or derivational suffixes) that modify the meaning of the verb root. These are highly productive and can be stacked:

- **-in-** (causative): *yah-* (go) > *yahin-* (cause to go, send)
- **-an-** (applicative/benefactive): *wind-* (write) > *windan-* (write for someone)
- **-ir-/-or-** (instrumental/manner): *yah-* (go) > *yahir-* (go by means of)
- **-it-** (reversive/repetitive): *udd-* (close) > *uddit-* (open)
- **-id-/-od-** (reciprocal): *yi'-* (see) > *yi'id-* (see each other)
- **-aa-/-ee-** (middle voice): *loot-* (wash something) > *lootaa-* (wash oneself)

Multiple extensions can combine: *yahindir-* (cause to go together by means of).

### Tense, Aspect, and Mood

The Fula verb system encodes a rich array of tense-aspect-mood distinctions. The main categories include:

- **General past / completive**: marks completed actions
- **Relative past**: used in relative clauses and subordinate constructions
- **Stative / resultative**: describes states resulting from previous actions
- **Habitual / general present**: for ongoing or habitual actions
- **Progressive**: for actions in progress at a given moment
- **Future**: for anticipated actions
- **Subjunctive / imperative**: for commands, wishes, and hypothetical situations
- **Negative**: each tense has a corresponding negative form, often with distinct morphology

Verbs conjugate by means of **subject pronouns** (often cliticized or fused with the verb), **tense suffixes**, and **voice markers**. The active voice is the default, while the middle and passive voices are marked by specific extensions.

### Subject and Object Marking

Fula uses both independent pronouns and bound pronominal forms. Subject pronouns may appear as independent words or as clitics attached to the verb complex. Object pronouns are typically suffixed or appear as clitics following the verb. The pronominal system distinguishes person (1st, 2nd, 3rd), number (singular, plural), and in some dialects, inclusive vs. exclusive first person plural.

## Syntax Features

### Relative Clauses

Relative clauses in Fula are formed using class-specific relative pronouns that agree with the noun class of the head noun. This means there is no single word for "which" or "that" -- the relative marker changes depending on the class of the antecedent: *mo* (for human singular), *ɓe* (human plural), *nde* (class nde), *ngal* (class ngal), etc.

### Focus and Topic

Fula employs several constructions for focus and topicalization. The copula *ko* is frequently used in cleft constructions for focus: *Ko gorko oo yahi luumo* ("It is the man who went to the market"). Topic constructions often place the topicalized element sentence-initially, followed by a pause or topic marker.

### Possession

Possession is expressed through juxtaposition (possessor following possessed noun), possessive pronouns, or a connective particle. The possessed noun and possessive marker agree in noun class: *suudu am* (my house), *galle am* (my compound).

### Negation

Negation in Fula is expressed through specialized negative verb conjugations rather than a simple negating particle. Each tense-aspect form has a corresponding negative paradigm with distinct suffixes and sometimes altered pronominal forms. In some dialects, the negative marker *-aa* or *-a* appears as a suffix on the verb.

### Adjectives and Numerals

Adjectives in Fula agree with the noun they modify in class, undergoing the same initial consonant alternation. For instance, an adjective meaning "big" might appear as *mawndo* (class -o), *mawngal* (class -ngal), *mawnde* (class -nde), etc. Numerals similarly show class agreement through suffixation and consonant alternation.
