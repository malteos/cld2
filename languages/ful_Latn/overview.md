# Fula / Fulfulde (ful_Latn) - Language Overview

## Names and Classification

Fula is known by many names depending on the region and dialect: **Fulfulde** (in Nigeria, Cameroon, and Central Africa), **Pulaar** (in Senegal, Mauritania, and The Gambia), **Pular** (in Guinea), and **Peul** (the French exonym used across Francophone West Africa). The ISO 639-3 code is **ful**, which serves as a macrolanguage code encompassing several recognized varieties including Adamawa Fulfulde (fub), Borgu Fulfulde (fue), Central-Eastern Niger Fulfulde (fuq), Nigerian Fulfulde (fuv), Western Niger Fulfulde (fuh), Maasina Fulfulde (ffm), Pulaar (fuc), and Pular (fuf).

Fula belongs to the **Atlantic-Congo** family, specifically within the **Atlantic** branch (sometimes called West Atlantic) and the **Senegambian** sub-branch. It is the most widely spoken Atlantic language and one of the most geographically dispersed languages in Africa.

## Script

The primary script used for Fula in official and educational contexts is the **Latin script**, adapted with special characters for sounds specific to the language. These include implosive consonants represented by hooked letters (ɓ, ɗ, ƴ) and the velar nasal (ŋ) and palatal nasal (ɲ). In some orthographic traditions, digraphs are used instead (e.g., bh, dh, yh or 'y, 'b, 'd).

Fula is also written in **Adlam**, a script invented in the late 1980s by Abdoulaye and Ibrahima Barry in Guinea specifically for the Fula language. Adlam has gained significant popularity, particularly in Guinea and among diaspora communities, and was added to Unicode in 2014. Additionally, an **Arabic-based script** (Ajami) has been used historically for writing Fula, especially in religious contexts and older manuscripts.

## Speakers and Distribution

Fula is spoken by approximately **40 to 50 million people** across a vast swath of West and Central Africa, making it one of the most widely spoken languages on the continent. Speakers are found in over 20 countries, with significant populations in:

- **Senegal** and **The Gambia** (Pulaar dialect, also called Toucouleur)
- **Guinea** (Pular, where Fula speakers form the largest ethnic group)
- **Mali** (Maasina Fulfulde, centered around the Inner Niger Delta)
- **Nigeria** (Fulfulde, especially in the northern states; the largest single-country population)
- **Cameroon** (Adamawa Fulfulde)
- **Niger** (Western and Central-Eastern Niger Fulfulde)
- **Burkina Faso**, **Guinea-Bissau**, **Mauritania**, **Chad**, **Central African Republic**, **Ghana**, **Benin**, **Togo**, and **Sierra Leone**

## Official and National Status

Fula holds **national language** status in Senegal, Guinea, Mali, Niger, Nigeria, Cameroon, The Gambia, Mauritania, Burkina Faso, and Guinea-Bissau. In Guinea, it is one of the principal national languages used in education and media. In Senegal, Pulaar is one of the six codified national languages alongside Wolof, Serer, Mandinka, Soninke, and Diola. In Nigeria, Fulfulde is recognized as a major language used in education and broadcasting in the northern regions.

## The Fulani People

The speakers of Fula are the **Fulani** (also called Fula, Fulbe, Peul, or Toucouleur), one of the largest and most widely distributed ethnic groups in Africa. Historically, the Fulani are associated with **pastoral nomadism** and cattle herding, and cattle remain central to Fulani cultural identity and vocabulary. The Fulani played a significant role in the spread of Islam across West Africa, notably through the **jihad movements** of the 18th and 19th centuries, including the Sokoto Caliphate founded by Usman dan Fodio. Today, Fulani people range from fully nomadic pastoralists (Mbororo) to settled urban dwellers, with many intermediate lifestyles.

## Dialectal Variation

Due to its vast geographic spread, Fula exhibits considerable dialectal diversity. The major dialect groups are partially mutually intelligible but can differ substantially in vocabulary and phonology. The most significant dialectal divisions are between the western varieties (Pulaar/Pular of Senegambia and Guinea) and the eastern varieties (Fulfulde of Nigeria, Cameroon, and the Central African region). Despite this variation, a shared grammatical core -- particularly the noun class system and verb morphology -- unites all varieties as clearly one language.
