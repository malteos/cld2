# Distinguishing French from Confusable Languages

French can be confused with several related Romance languages and French-derived creoles. The following guide details the key differences and diagnostic features for accurate language identification.

## French vs. Italian

Italian and French share significant Romance vocabulary, but they are readily distinguishable:

- **Double consonants:** Italian uses frequent double consonants (tutto, bello, anno, fatto), while French rarely does (though "elle," "bonne," "cette" exist, they are far less pervasive).
- **Final vowels:** Italian words overwhelmingly end in vowels (-a, -o, -e, -i), while French words very frequently end in consonants or silent -e.
- **Articles:** Italian uses il, lo, la, i, gli, le vs. French le, la, l', les. The Italian articles "il," "gli," "lo" do not appear in French.
- **Preposition + article contractions:** Italian uses nel, del, al, sul vs. French dans le, du, au, sur le.
- **Nasal vowels:** French nasal spellings (an, en, on, in, un) are absent from Italian orthography.
- **Key Italian identifiers absent in French:** che, di, non, sono, questo, quello, anche, perche, degli, nella.
- **Key French identifiers absent in Italian:** dans, avec, pour, mais, donc, qu', l', très, beaucoup, aussi, où, ne...pas.

## French vs. Spanish

Despite shared Latin roots, numerous differences separate French from Spanish:

- **Orthographic markers:** Spanish uses the inverted question and exclamation marks (? !), the letter n, and heavily uses the letter j (/x/ sound). French uses none of these.
- **Articles:** Spanish uses el, los, las vs. French le, les, la. The Spanish masculine article "el" and plural "los" do not appear in French.
- **Function words:** Spanish uses y (and), pero (but), tambien, porque, donde, cuando, donde, muy, con. French equivalents are et, mais, aussi, parce que, ou, quand, tres, avec.
- **Verb endings:** Spanish infinitives end in -ar, -er, -ir (with -ar being the most common). French infinitives end in -er, -ir, -re (with -er most common, but pronounced differently and spelled differently in conjugations).
- **Key Spanish identifiers absent in French:** el, los, las, es (as article), por, como (with accent: como), muy, pero, hay, este/esta, del (as contraction of de + el).

## French vs. Portuguese

Portuguese and French share several features, including nasal vowels, making them somewhat closer to confuse:

- **Nasal vowels:** Both languages have nasal vowels, but Portuguese marks them differently: with a tilde (a, o) or before m/n. French uses an/am, en/em, on/om, in/im, un/um.
- **Articles:** Portuguese uses o, a, os, as vs. French le, la, les. The Portuguese articles are a strong distinguishing feature.
- **Function words:** Portuguese uses nao, com, para, mas, ou, e, que, em, um/uma. French uses ne...pas, avec, pour, mais, ou, et, que, dans/en, un/une. The Portuguese words "nao," "com," and "em" are clear indicators.
- **Cedilla usage:** Both languages use c, but Portuguese also uses a and o, which do not exist in French.
- **Key Portuguese identifiers absent in French:** o/a/os/as (articles), nao, com, em, e (as "is"), esta, voce, tambem, ja.

## French vs. Romanian

Romanian is a Romance language but has diverged considerably from western Romance:

- **Diacritics:** Romanian uses a, a, i, s, t, which are entirely absent from French.
- **Postposed articles:** Romanian appends definite articles to nouns (omul = "the man"), while French preposes them (l'homme).
- **Slavic vocabulary:** Romanian contains substantial Slavic-origin vocabulary absent from French.
- **Key Romanian identifiers absent in French:** si (and), nu (no), la/lui (different usage), este, sunt, care, acest, mai, foarte, doar, insa.

## French vs. Catalan

Catalan shares features with both French and Spanish:

- **Key differences:** Catalan uses the middle dot in l-l (ella), the raised period, and distinctive function words: i (and), pero, amb (with), mes (more/but), tambe, quan, on.
- **Articles:** Catalan uses el, la, els, les -- "els" is diagnostic of Catalan vs. French.
- **French distinguishers:** avec, dans, pour, mais, donc, très, beaucoup are absent from Catalan.

## French vs. Haitian Creole

Haitian Creole derives from French but has a simplified grammar:

- **Lack of articles:** Haitian Creole uses postposed "la/a" as a definite marker rather than French preposed articles.
- **Verb system:** No conjugation in Haitian Creole; tense is marked by particles (te, ap, pral).
- **Spelling:** Haitian Creole uses a phonetic orthography (e.g., "ou" for French "vous," "mwen" for "moi," "li" for "il/elle").
- **Key Haitian identifiers:** mwen, ou, li, yo, pa, te, ap, se, nan, pou, ki, sa.

## French vs. Occitan

Occitan is the closest genetic relative of French still in use:

- **Articles:** Occitan uses lo/la/los/las vs. French le/la/les.
- **Function words:** Occitan uses e (and), mas, amb (with), per, que, mai (more), ont. French uses et, mais, avec, pour, que, plus, ou.
- **Verb endings:** Occitan infinitives often end in -ar (first conjugation), vs. French -er.
- **Key French identifiers absent in Occitan:** dans, avec, pour, beaucoup, très, aussi, donc, ne...pas, qu', l' (with same frequency).

## Summary of Strongest French Identifiers

The following features, when found in combination, strongly indicate French text:

1. Elision with apostrophe: l', d', n', j', c', s', qu'
2. Function words: dans, avec, pour, mais, donc, aussi, très, beaucoup, encore, même
3. Negation: ne...pas, ne...plus, ne...jamais
4. Nasal vowel spellings: an, en, on, in, un (in combination with French function words)
5. The suffix -ment (extremely frequent in adverbs)
6. The suffix -tion/-sion
7. Partitive articles: du, de la, des
8. Contracted forms: au (a + le), aux (a + les), du (de + le), des (de + les)
