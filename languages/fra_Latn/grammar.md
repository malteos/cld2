# French Grammar

## Word Order

French is a Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) language in declarative sentences. The basic word order is relatively fixed compared to languages with extensive case marking. However, certain constructions allow for variations:

- **Declarative:** Le chat mange la souris. (The cat eats the mouse.)
- **Interrogative:** Formal inversion moves the subject after the verb: Mange-t-il la souris? Informal speech often uses "est-ce que" or rising intonation.
- **Clitic pronouns** precede the verb rather than following the object position: Je le vois. (I see him.)

Adjective placement generally follows the noun (une voiture rouge), though a set of common short adjectives precede the noun (un grand homme, une belle maison). Some adjectives change meaning depending on position (un ancien professeur "a former professor" vs. un meuble ancien "an antique piece of furniture").

## Morphological Type

French is a fusional language. Grammatical information such as tense, mood, person, number, and gender is expressed through affixes that often encode multiple categories simultaneously. For example, the verb ending "-ons" in "nous parlons" simultaneously marks first person, plural number, and present indicative.

## Phonology Highlights

### Nasal Vowels

French possesses a distinctive set of nasal vowels, which are relatively rare among European languages. The main nasal vowels are:

- /ɑ̃/ as in "an, en, em, am" (dans, enfant, temps)
- /ɛ̃/ as in "in, im, ain, ein, un" (vin, important, pain, plein)
- /ɔ̃/ as in "on, om" (bon,ombre)

These nasal vowels are a strong diagnostic feature of French in both spoken and written forms.

### Liaison

Liaison is the pronunciation of a normally silent final consonant before a word beginning with a vowel or silent h. For example, "les amis" is pronounced /le.z‿a.mi/, where the final -s of "les" is realized as /z/. Liaison is obligatory in certain syntactic contexts (determiner + noun, pronoun + verb) and optional or forbidden in others.

### Elision

Elision is the dropping of a final vowel (usually /ə/) before a word beginning with a vowel sound, marked in writing by an apostrophe. Examples include l'homme (le + homme), j'ai (je + ai), c'est (ce + est), qu'il (que + il), and n'est (ne + est). Elision is a highly visible orthographic feature that helps distinguish French text from other Romance languages.

## Noun System

### Gender

French has two grammatical genders: masculine and feminine. Gender assignment is largely arbitrary for inanimate nouns, though certain endings provide clues (nouns ending in -tion, -sion, -ure, -ence, -ance tend to be feminine; nouns ending in -ment, -age, -isme tend to be masculine). Gender affects articles, adjectives, pronouns, and past participle agreement.

### Number

Plural is typically marked by adding -s to the noun, though this is usually silent in spoken French. Certain nouns have irregular plurals (oeil/yeux, travail/travaux). Articles change form for plural: le/la become les, un/une become des.

### Cases

French has no case system for nouns. Grammatical relationships are expressed through word order and prepositions (de for possession, a for indirect objects).

## Article System

French has a rich article system that is obligatory in most noun phrases:

- **Definite articles:** le (masc. sg.), la (fem. sg.), l' (before vowel), les (plural)
- **Indefinite articles:** un (masc. sg.), une (fem. sg.), des (plural)
- **Partitive articles:** du (masc. sg.), de la (fem. sg.), de l' (before vowel), des (plural) -- used to indicate an unspecified quantity of a mass noun (Je bois du cafe. Je mange de la soupe.)

The partitive article is a distinctive feature of French grammar that is absent from many other Romance languages.

## Verb System

The French verb system is one of the most complex aspects of the language, featuring extensive conjugation paradigms.

### Verb Groups

French verbs are traditionally divided into three conjugation groups based on their infinitive endings:

- **First group (-er):** parler, manger, aimer -- the largest and most productive group
- **Second group (-ir with -iss- in plural):** finir, choisir, rougir
- **Third group (irregular):** all other verbs, including -re verbs (vendre, prendre), -ir verbs without -iss- (partir, dormir), and highly irregular verbs (etre, avoir, aller, faire)

### Tenses and Moods

French has an extensive tense and mood system:

- **Indicative mood:** present (je parle), imperfect (je parlais), simple past/passe simple (je parlai), future (je parlerai), compound past/passe compose (j'ai parle), pluperfect (j'avais parle), past anterior (j'eus parle), future anterior (j'aurai parle)
- **Subjunctive mood:** present (que je parle), past (que j'aie parle), imperfect (que je parlasse), pluperfect (que j'eusse parle). The subjunctive is used after certain conjunctions (bien que, pour que, avant que), after expressions of doubt, desire, emotion, and necessity, and in certain relative clauses.
- **Conditional mood:** present (je parlerais), past (j'aurais parle)
- **Imperative mood:** parle, parlons, parlez
- **Infinitive and participles:** parler, parlant, parle

### Auxiliary Verbs

French uses two auxiliary verbs for compound tenses: avoir (most verbs) and etre (reflexive verbs and a set of intransitive verbs of motion or state change, often memorized as the "Dr. & Mrs. Vandertramp" verbs: devenir, revenir, monter, rester, sortir, venir, aller, naitre, descendre, entrer, retourner, tomber, rentrer, arriver, mourir, partir).

## Syntax

### Clitic Pronouns

Object pronouns in French are clitics that attach to the verb rather than standing independently. They follow a fixed order when multiple clitics co-occur: me/te/se/nous/vous > le/la/les > lui/leur > y > en. For example: Il me le donne. (He gives it to me.) Je lui en ai parle. (I spoke to him about it.)

### Negation

Standard French negation uses a two-part structure: ne ... pas surrounds the verb (Je ne sais pas. "I don't know."). Other negative expressions follow the same pattern: ne ... jamais (never), ne ... rien (nothing), ne ... plus (no longer), ne ... personne (nobody), ne ... que (only). In informal spoken French, the ne particle is frequently dropped, leaving only pas or the other negative word.

### Relative Clauses

French uses several relative pronouns: qui (subject), que (direct object), dont (of which/whose), ou (where/when), and compound forms with lequel (lequel, laquelle, lesquels, lesquelles) used after prepositions.
