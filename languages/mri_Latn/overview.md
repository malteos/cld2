## Speakers and Distribution

Maori is the indigenous language of the Maori people of Aotearoa (New Zealand). As of recent estimates, there are approximately 150,000 to 185,000 speakers of varying fluency levels, though some estimates of highly fluent speakers place the number closer to 50,000. The language is spoken primarily in New Zealand, with small diaspora communities in Australia, the United Kingdom, and other countries where New Zealand emigrants have settled.

Within New Zealand, Maori is spoken across both the North Island (Te Ika-a-Maui) and the South Island (Te Waipounamu), though speaker density is higher in certain regions. The Eastern Bay of Plenty, Northland (Te Tai Tokerau), and the East Cape are historically strong Maori-speaking regions. Urban centres such as Auckland, Wellington, and Rotorua also have significant Maori-speaking populations, reflecting broader patterns of Maori urbanisation during the twentieth century.

## Official Status

Maori has been an official language of New Zealand since the Maori Language Act 1987, alongside English and New Zealand Sign Language. The language holds a special constitutional status as a taonga (treasure) under the Treaty of Waitangi (Te Tiriti o Waitangi), signed in 1840 between the British Crown and Maori chiefs. The Te Taura Whiri i te Reo Maori (Maori Language Commission) was established to promote and regulate the use of the Maori language. In 2016, Te Ture mo Te Reo Maori (the Maori Language Act 2016) further strengthened the legal framework supporting Maori language revitalisation.

## Brief History

Maori belongs to the Eastern Polynesian branch of the Austronesian language family. The ancestors of the Maori people are believed to have arrived in New Zealand from eastern Polynesia, likely from the region around the Society Islands and the Cook Islands, between approximately 1250 and 1300 CE. Over several centuries of isolation, the language diverged from its Polynesian relatives, developing distinct vocabulary, phonological features, and grammatical constructions, though it remains closely related to Cook Islands Maori, Tahitian, and other Tahitic languages.

Prior to European contact, Maori was an exclusively oral language with a rich tradition of oratory (whaikorero), song (waiata), genealogical recitation (whakapapa), and narrative (korero). European missionaries, beginning with Thomas Kendall in the early nineteenth century, developed the first written orthography for Maori using the Latin script. Professor Samuel Lee of Cambridge University refined this orthography in 1820, and subsequent work by missionaries such as William Williams further standardised the writing system. The Bible was translated into Maori in the 1830s and 1840s, and Maori literacy rates became remarkably high during this period.

Throughout the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, colonial language policies, urbanisation, and the dominance of English led to a dramatic decline in Maori language use. By the 1970s, fewer than 20 percent of Maori could speak the language fluently. This crisis sparked a powerful revitalisation movement. The establishment of kohanga reo (Maori language preschools) in 1982, kura kaupapa Maori (Maori-medium primary schools) in 1985, and wharekura (Maori-medium secondary schools) created immersion education pathways. Maori Television began broadcasting in 2004, and today a growing number of digital resources, apps, and media support the ongoing revitalisation of te reo Maori.

## Classification Within Polynesian

Maori is classified within the Tahitic subgroup of Eastern Polynesian, making it most closely related to Cook Islands Maori (Rarotongan), Tahitian, and Tuamotuan. More distantly, it is related to Hawaiian, Marquesan, and Rapanui (Easter Island) within the broader Eastern Polynesian group, and to Samoan, Tongan, Niuean, and other languages within the wider Polynesian family. The Polynesian languages themselves form a well-defined subgroup of the Oceanic branch of Austronesian, one of the world's largest language families.
