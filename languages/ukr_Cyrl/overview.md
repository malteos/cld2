# Ukrainian (ukr_Cyrl)

## General Information

- **Language name:** Ukrainian (ukrainska mova)
- **Native name:** Ukrainska mova (Ukrajinsjka mova)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** ukr
- **ISO 639-1 code:** uk
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > East Slavic
- **Script:** Cyrillic (Ukrainian variant)
- **Approximate number of speakers:** 45 million (native), with additional second-language speakers
- **Primary region:** Ukraine
- **Other regions:** Diaspora communities in Canada, the United States, Brazil, Argentina, Poland, Germany, Portugal, the Czech Republic, Italy, the United Kingdom, Australia, and many other countries

## Historical Development

Ukrainian belongs to the East Slavic branch of the Slavic language family, alongside Russian and Belarusian. The East Slavic languages descended from a common ancestor known as Old East Slavic (also called Old Ruthenian), which was the literary and administrative language of Kyivan Rus from the 9th to the 13th centuries.

The differentiation of Ukrainian as a distinct language began during the 12th to 14th centuries, as the political fragmentation of Kyivan Rus led to divergent linguistic developments across its former territories. The earliest texts showing distinctly Ukrainian phonological and morphological features date to the late 12th and 13th centuries.

During the 14th to 18th centuries, much of Ukrainian-speaking territory was under the control of the Grand Duchy of Lithuania, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, and later the Russian Empire and the Austro-Hungarian Empire. This political history had a profound influence on the development of the language. The Ruthenian language (also called Old Ukrainian or Old Belarusian) served as a chancellery language in the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and retained many features that would later become characteristic of modern Ukrainian.

The modern Ukrainian literary language began to take shape in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. Ivan Kotliarevsky's "Eneida" (1798), a burlesque travesty of Virgil's Aeneid written in vernacular Ukrainian, is often cited as the foundational work of modern Ukrainian literature. Taras Shevchenko (1814-1861), widely regarded as the greatest Ukrainian poet, played a decisive role in establishing the literary language and raising the prestige of Ukrainian as a vehicle for high literature and national identity.

Throughout the 19th century, the Russian Empire imposed a series of restrictions on the use of Ukrainian, most notably the Ems Decree of 1876, which banned Ukrainian-language publications and public performances. Despite these prohibitions, Ukrainian continued to develop as a literary and scholarly language, particularly in the western Ukrainian lands under Austro-Hungarian rule, where it enjoyed greater freedom.

The early Soviet period saw a brief flourishing of Ukrainian culture and language during the policy of Ukrainization (korenizatsiia) in the 1920s. However, this was reversed under Stalin in the 1930s, and subsequent Soviet language policy promoted Russian as the dominant language of public life, education, and administration.

Following Ukrainian independence in 1991, Ukrainian was declared the sole state language. Since then, the country has pursued policies aimed at expanding the use of Ukrainian in education, media, government, and public life. The language situation in Ukraine has been complex, with significant Russian-speaking populations in eastern and southern regions, but the trend since 2014 and especially since 2022 has been toward increased use of Ukrainian across all domains of society.

## Typological Characteristics

Ukrainian is a synthetic, highly inflectional language with relatively free word order (subject-verb-object as the default). It features a rich case system for nouns and adjectives (seven cases), a complex verbal system with aspectual distinctions, and a phonological system that includes palatalized consonants. Ukrainian Cyrillic differs from Russian Cyrillic in several letters and is phonemically more transparent than the Russian writing system.
