# Distinguishing Welsh from Confusable Languages

## Welsh vs. Breton (bre)

Welsh and Breton are both Brythonic Celtic languages and share a common ancestor in the Brittonic language spoken across Britain before the Anglo-Saxon period. Breton speakers migrated from Britain to Brittany (Armorica) during the 5th-6th centuries. Despite their shared origins, the two languages have diverged significantly in orthography and vocabulary.

### Key Orthographic Differences

- **Welsh "ll"** (voiceless lateral fricative) has no equivalent in Breton. Breton lacks this sound entirely.
- **Welsh "dd"** (/ð/) vs. Breton "z" or "zh" for similar sounds.
- **Welsh "ff"** (/f/) vs. Breton "f" for the same sound. Welsh uses "f" for /v/ while Breton uses "v" for /v/.
- **Breton "c'h"** (velar fricative /x/) vs. Welsh "ch" for the same sound.
- **Breton "zh"** has no Welsh equivalent.
- **Breton uses "k"** extensively where Welsh uses "c" (Breton "kêr" vs. Welsh "caer," city/fortress).
- **Breton uses "w"** rarely as a vowel; Welsh uses "w" as a vowel very frequently.

### Key Function Word Differences

| Concept | Welsh | Breton |
|---------|-------|--------|
| the | y/yr/'r | an/al/ar |
| is | mae/yw | eo/zo |
| and | a/ac | ha/hag |
| in | yn | e/en |
| to | i | da |
| not | dim/ddim | ne...ket |

### Diagnostic Rule
If the text contains "ll" and "dd" digraphs, the article "y/yr/'r," and lacks "c'h," "zh," and "k" in native words, it is almost certainly Welsh rather than Breton.

## Welsh vs. Cornish (cor)

Welsh and Cornish are closely related Brythonic languages. Cornish was spoken in Cornwall until it became extinct in the late 18th century and has since been revived. The revived forms of Cornish (Kernewek) have drawn on both historical texts and comparison with Welsh and Breton.

### Key Differences

- **Cornish lacks "ll"** (/ɬ/). The Welsh voiceless lateral fricative is absent from modern Cornish.
- **Cornish orthography** varies by revival standard (Kernewek Standard, Kernewek Unyes, etc.) but generally looks distinct from Welsh.
- **Cornish uses "dh"** where Welsh uses "dd" for /ð/.
- **Cornish uses "gh"** in some positions where Welsh uses "ch" or other forms.
- **The Cornish article** is "an" (similar to Breton), not "y/yr/'r" as in Welsh.
- **Cornish function words** differ: "ha/hag" (and) vs. Welsh "a/ac," "yn" (in) vs. Cornish "yn" (similar but overall context differs), "yw/yns" (is) vs. Welsh "mae/yw."

### Diagnostic Rule
Welsh is distinguishable from Cornish primarily by the presence of "ll" and "dd" digraphs, the "y/yr" article, and the overall higher frequency of Welsh text online (Cornish texts are comparatively very rare).

## Welsh vs. Irish (gle)

Welsh and Irish are both Celtic languages but belong to different branches: Welsh is Brythonic (P-Celtic) while Irish is Goidelic (Q-Celtic). The two languages are not mutually intelligible and differ profoundly in phonology, orthography, and grammar.

### Key Orthographic Differences

- **Irish uses "bh, mh, dh, gh, fh, sh, th, ph"** for lenited consonants. Welsh mutation is similar in concept but uses completely different letter combinations.
- **Irish uses accent marks (fada):** "á, é, í, ó, ú." Welsh uses circumflex (to bach): "â, ê, î, ô, û, ŵ, ŷ."
- **Irish has no "ll," "dd," or "ff"** digraphs. These are exclusively Welsh.
- **Irish uses "ao"** frequently (e.g., "aon," "saol"), which is extremely rare in Welsh.
- **Irish uses "igh," "eadh," "aidh"** and other complex vowel sequences absent in Welsh.
- **Welsh uses "w" and "y" as vowels;** Irish does not use "w" as a vowel and "y" is rare in Irish.

### Key Function Word Differences

| Concept | Welsh | Irish |
|---------|-------|-------|
| the | y/yr/'r | an/na |
| is | mae | tá/is |
| and | a/ac | agus/is |
| in | yn | i/in |
| of | o | de/as |
| on | ar | ar |
| not | dim/ddim | ní/cha |

### Diagnostic Rule
The presence of "ll," "dd," "ff," "y/yr" article, and the absence of Irish fada accents and lenition patterns (bh, mh, fh, gh) clearly distinguish Welsh from Irish. The overall visual appearance of the two languages is quite different.

## Welsh vs. English (eng)

Despite centuries of contact and extensive English borrowing into Welsh, the two languages are structurally very different.

### Key Structural Differences

- **Welsh has VSO word order;** English has SVO.
- **Welsh has initial consonant mutations;** English has no such system.
- **Welsh digraphs "ll," "dd," "ff"** do not occur as meaningful units in English (though "ll" appears in English words like "bell," it is simply a doubled letter, not a distinct phoneme).
- **Welsh "f" = /v/**, while English "f" = /f/. Welsh "ff" = /f/.
- **Welsh lacks "k, q, v, x, z"** in native words; all of these are common in English.
- **Welsh uses "w" and "y" as vowels** in contexts that would be impossible in English (e.g., "cwm," "crwn," "byd," "dyn").

### Key Function Word Differences

The most common words in the two languages are entirely different: Welsh "y/yn/i/a/o/mae" vs. English "the/of/and/to/in/is." There is virtually no overlap in high-frequency function words.

### Diagnostic Rule
Even short fragments of Welsh text are easily distinguishable from English by the presence of "ll," "dd," "ff," the article "y/yr," the particle "yn," forms of "bod" (mae, oedd, bydd), and the overall absence of English function words. Welsh borrowings from English are often adapted orthographically (e.g., English "office" > Welsh "swyddfa," though some modern borrowings retain English spelling).

## Summary of Diagnostic Features

The most reliable features for identifying Welsh text are:

1. **"ll" digraph** - extremely frequent, absent from all confusable languages
2. **"dd" digraph** - very frequent, unique to Welsh among Celtic languages
3. **"ff" digraph** - frequent, unique to Welsh
4. **"y/yr/'r" definite article** - ubiquitous, different from all other Celtic articles
5. **"mae"** - the most common verb form, unique to Welsh
6. **"yn" particle** - extremely high frequency, used as preposition and linking particle
7. **Forms of "bod":** "mae, oedd, bydd, roedd, sydd" - uniquely Welsh
8. **"w" and "y" as vowels** in consonant-heavy words (e.g., "cwm," "tywyll," "byd")
9. **Circumflex vowels** "â, ê, î, ô, û, ŵ, ŷ" (to bach) vs. Irish fada or Breton trema
10. **Absence of "k, q, v, x, z"** in native vocabulary
