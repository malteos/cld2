# Welsh (Cymraeg)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Welsh (Cymraeg)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** cym
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** cy
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Celtic > Insular Celtic > Brythonic (Brittonic)
- **Script:** Latin
- **Approximate Speakers:** ~750,000+ (with ~560,000 in Wales and additional speakers in England, Patagonia, and worldwide diaspora)
- **Writing Direction:** Left-to-right

## Geographic Distribution

Welsh is primarily spoken in Wales (Cymru), a constituent country of the United Kingdom. The language has its strongest presence in the northern and western regions of Wales, particularly in Gwynedd, Anglesey (Ynys Mon), Ceredigion, and Carmarthenshire (Sir Gaerfyrddin). Cardiff (Caerdydd), the capital, has also seen significant growth in Welsh-speaking populations due to Welsh-medium education and migration.

A notable Welsh-speaking community exists in Patagonia, Argentina, known as Y Wladfa. This community was established in 1865 when around 150 Welsh settlers arrived in the Chubut Valley. Today, an estimated 5,000-50,000 people in Patagonia retain some knowledge of Welsh, and the language continues to be taught and spoken in towns such as Trelew, Gaiman, and Trevelin.

Smaller Welsh-speaking communities can be found in England (particularly in border regions and cities like London, Liverpool, and Bristol), as well as in Australia, the United States, and Canada.

## Official Status

Welsh holds co-official status alongside English in Wales. The Welsh Language Act of 1993 established the principle that Welsh and English should be treated on the basis of equality in the conduct of public business and the administration of justice in Wales. This was further strengthened by the Welsh Language (Wales) Measure 2011, which gave Welsh official status in Wales for the first time and created the role of the Welsh Language Commissioner (Comisiynydd y Gymraeg) to promote and facilitate the use of Welsh.

The Welsh Government's strategy "Cymraeg 2050" aims to achieve one million Welsh speakers by the year 2050, reflecting a strong policy commitment to language revitalization.

## Historical Background

Welsh descends from Common Brittonic (Brythonic), the Celtic language spoken across most of Britain before and during the Roman occupation. As the Anglo-Saxons expanded across England from the 5th century onward, the Brittonic-speaking communities became isolated into distinct groups, with Welsh emerging as a separate language in what is now Wales.

Welsh possesses one of the oldest literary traditions in Europe. The earliest Welsh poetry dates to the 6th century, with works attributed to Taliesin and Aneirin. The poem "Y Gododdin" by Aneirin, describing a battle between Brittonic warriors and Angles around 600 CE, is among the oldest surviving works of literature from Britain. The Mabinogion, a collection of medieval Welsh prose tales, represents one of the great achievements of medieval European literature.

The Laws of Hywel Dda (Cyfraith Hywel), codified in the 10th century, demonstrate that Welsh was used as a sophisticated legal language centuries before many European vernaculars achieved similar status.

The translation of the Bible into Welsh by William Morgan in 1588 was a landmark event that helped standardize the language and ensure its survival during a period when many other minority languages were declining. The Welsh Bible became a cornerstone of Welsh-language culture and religious life.

## Language Revitalization

Welsh experienced significant decline during the 19th and early 20th centuries due to industrialization, English-language education policies (including the infamous "Welsh Not" punishment in schools), and urbanization. The percentage of Welsh speakers fell dramatically during this period.

The modern revival movement gained momentum in the 20th century. Cymdeithas yr Iaith Gymraeg (the Welsh Language Society), founded in 1962 following a famous radio lecture by Saunders Lewis ("Tynged yr Iaith" / "The Fate of the Language"), campaigned vigorously for Welsh language rights through direct action and civil disobedience. Their campaigns led to bilingual road signs, Welsh-language broadcasting (including the establishment of S4C, the Welsh-language television channel, in 1982), and legal recognition of the language.

Welsh-medium education has expanded significantly, with both designated Welsh-medium schools (ysgolion Cymraeg) and bilingual schools across Wales. The National Eisteddfod (Eisteddfod Genedlaethol Cymru), an annual cultural festival conducted entirely in Welsh, remains a vital institution for the language and culture. Welsh is now widely used in government, education, media, and daily life across Wales, making it one of the more successful examples of language revitalization in Europe.
