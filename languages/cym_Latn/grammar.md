# Welsh Grammar

## Word Order

Welsh is a VSO (Verb-Subject-Object) language, one of the relatively few European languages with this basic word order. In main clauses, the finite verb typically comes first, followed by the subject and then the object:

- **Gwelodd y dyn y ci.** (Saw the man the dog = "The man saw the dog.")
- **Mae'r plant yn chwarae.** (Is the children in playing = "The children are playing.")

However, fronting of constituents for emphasis or focus is common, which can produce apparent SVO or OVS orders:

- **Y dyn a welodd y ci.** (The man who saw the dog = "It was the man who saw the dog.")

In colloquial Welsh, periphrastic constructions with the verb "bod" (to be) are extremely common and can create patterns that superficially resemble SVO order.

## Morphological Type

Welsh is a fusional language with significant morphological complexity. It combines inflectional morphology (particularly in the verb system) with a highly productive system of initial consonant mutations that pervade the grammar.

## Phonology

### Consonants

Welsh has approximately 29 consonant phonemes, including several sounds rare or absent in most other European languages:

- **Voiceless lateral fricative /ɬ/:** Written as "ll," this is one of the most distinctive sounds of Welsh. It is produced by placing the tongue in the position for /l/ but blowing air past the sides of the tongue without voicing.
- **Voiceless nasals /m̥, n̥, ŋ̊/:** Written "mh," "nh," and "ngh" respectively. These occur primarily as products of nasal mutation.
- **Voiceless alveolar trill /r̥/:** Written "rh," a voiceless version of the trilled r.
- **Velar fricative /x/:** Written "ch," similar to Scottish "loch" or German "Bach."
- **Voiced interdental fricative /ð/:** Written "dd," as in English "the."
- **Voiceless interdental fricative /θ/:** Written "th," as in English "think."

Other consonants include stops /p, b, t, d, k, g/, fricatives /f, v, s, ʃ, h/, nasals /m, n, ŋ/, the lateral /l/, the trill /r/, and the approximants /j, w/.

### Vowels

Welsh has both short and long vowels. The vowel inventory includes /a, e, i, o, u, ə, ɨ/ (in Northern dialects) or /a, e, i, o, u, ə/ (in Southern dialects). Notably, the letters "w" and "y" function as vowels in Welsh:

- **w** = /uː/ or /ʊ/ (e.g., "cwm" = valley)
- **y** = /ə/ in non-final syllables, /ɨ/ or /i/ in final syllables (e.g., "yn" = in, "dyn" = man)

Welsh has a rich system of diphthongs, including /ai, ei, oi, au, eu, ou, əi, ɨu, iu, aɨ/.

### Stress

Stress in Welsh falls predictably on the penultimate (second-to-last) syllable in most words:

- **ca.FE.li** (cafeli, cafes)
- **Cym.RAE.g** (Welsh)
- **ys.GOL** (school) - monosyllabic in practice

A small number of words bear final stress, often borrowings or words where a final syllable was historically a separate element.

## Noun System

### Gender

Welsh has two grammatical genders: masculine (gwrywaidd) and feminine (benywaidd). Gender affects:

- The form of the article and adjectives after feminine singular nouns
- Mutation patterns (feminine singular nouns undergo soft mutation after the article "y/yr/'r")
- Pronoun reference

Examples:
- **y bachgen** (the boy, masculine - no mutation)
- **y ferch** (the girl, feminine - "merch" undergoes soft mutation to "ferch" after the article)

### Articles

Welsh has a definite article "y" (before consonants), "yr" (before vowels and h), and "'r" (after vowels). There is no indefinite article; bare nouns are interpreted as indefinite:

- **y ty** (the house)
- **yr afon** (the river)
- **mae'r ci** (the dog is)
- **ci** (a dog)

### Plurals

Welsh plurals are notably irregular and diverse. Common plural formation strategies include:

- **Suffixation:** -au, -iau, -oedd, -i, -ydd, -od, -ed (e.g., "cath" > "cathod," cats; "ty" > "tai," houses)
- **Vowel change (ablaut):** "car" > "ceir" (cars), "bran" > "brain" (crows)
- **Singulative formation:** Some nouns are inherently plural, with a singulative suffix -en/-yn added for the singular (e.g., "coed" = trees, "coeden" = a tree; "plant" = children, "plentyn" = a child)

### Case

Welsh has no case system on nouns. Grammatical relations are indicated by word order, prepositions, and mutations.

## Verb System

### Inflected (Synthetic) Forms

Welsh verbs can be fully inflected for person, number, and tense. Traditional literary Welsh uses inflected forms extensively:

- **Gwelaf** (I see), **gweli** (you see), **gwel** (he/she sees)
- **Gwelais** (I saw), **gwelaist** (you saw), **gwelodd** (he/she saw)
- **Gwelwn** (we see/saw), **gwelwch** (you see/saw), **gwelant** (they see)

### Periphrastic Forms

In spoken Welsh, periphrastic constructions using the verb "bod" (to be) are dominant. The verb "bod" has the most complex conjugation in Welsh:

- **Present:** Rydw i (I am), rwyt ti (you are), mae e/hi (he/she is), rydyn ni (we are), rydych chi (you are), maen nhw (they are)
- **Past (imperfect):** Roeddwn i (I was), roeddet ti (you were), roedd e/hi (he/she was)
- **Future:** Bydda i (I will be), byddi di (you will be), bydd e/hi (he/she will be)

Periphrastic tenses are formed with "bod" + subject + yn + verbnoun:
- **Mae Siân yn canu.** (Siân is singing.)
- **Roedd y plant yn chwarae.** (The children were playing.)

### Affirmative, Negative, and Interrogative Particles

Welsh uses particles to mark sentence type, each triggering specific mutations:

- **Fe/Mi** (affirmative particle, triggers soft mutation): **Fe welodd e'r ci.** (He saw the dog.)
- **Ni/Nid** (negative particle, triggers soft mutation in literary Welsh or aspirate mutation): **Ni welodd e'r ci.** / **Nid yw e'n hapus.** (He didn't see the dog. / He is not happy.) In colloquial Welsh, **ddim** is used: **Dyw e ddim yn hapus.**
- **A** (interrogative particle, triggers soft mutation): **A welodd e'r ci?** (Did he see the dog?)

## Initial Consonant Mutations

The system of initial consonant mutations is the single most distinctive and pervasive feature of Welsh grammar. There are three types of mutation:

### Soft Mutation (Treiglad Meddal)

The most common mutation, triggered in numerous grammatical contexts:

| Radical | Mutated |
|---------|---------|
| p > b   | t > d   |
| c > g   | b > f   |
| d > dd  | g > (disappears) |
| m > f   | ll > l  |
| rh > r  |         |

Triggers include: feminine singular nouns after the article, nouns after adjectives "hen" (old) and "prif" (main), objects of inflected verbs, after prepositions "i," "o," "am," "ar," "at," "dan," "dros," "drwy," "heb," "hyd," "gan," "wrth," adjectives after feminine singular nouns, after the particles "fe/mi," "a" (interrogative), "ni" (negative), and many more contexts.

### Nasal Mutation (Treiglad Trwynol)

| Radical | Mutated   |
|---------|-----------|
| p > mh  | t > nh    |
| c > ngh | b > m     |
| d > n   | g > ng    |

Primary trigger: after the preposition "yn" (in): **yng Nghaerdydd** (in Cardiff), **ym Mangor** (in Bangor).

### Aspirate Mutation (Treiglad Llaes)

| Radical | Mutated |
|---------|---------|
| p > ph  | t > th  |
| c > ch  |         |

Triggers include: after "a" (and), "a" (with, literary), "ei" (her), "tri" (three), "chwe" (six), and the negative particle "ni" in literary Welsh.

These mutations are not optional stylistic features but are grammatically obligatory and constitute a core part of Welsh morphosyntax. Failure to apply mutations correctly is one of the most common errors made by learners and is a strong indicator in computational analysis.

## Other Syntactic Features

- **Relative clauses** use the particle "a" (direct) or "y" (indirect), each triggering different mutations.
- **Possessive pronouns** precede the noun and trigger mutations: "ei" (his) triggers soft mutation, "ei" (her) triggers aspirate mutation and adds "h" before vowels.
- **Numerals** have complex agreement patterns with following nouns, including mutations and sometimes singular noun forms after numerals.
- **Prepositions** are conjugated (inflected for person): "ar" (on) becomes "arna i" (on me), "arnat ti" (on you), "arno fe" (on him), etc. This is used to express many concepts that English handles with verbs (e.g., "Mae eisiau arna i" = "I want," literally "There is want on me").
