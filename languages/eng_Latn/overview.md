# English (eng_Latn)

## General Information

- **Language Name:** English
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** eng
- **Script:** Latin (Latn)
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Germanic > West Germanic > Anglo-Frisian > Anglic
- **Native Speakers:** Approximately 380 million
- **Total Speakers:** Approximately 1.5 billion (including L2 speakers)
- **Type:** Analytic/Isolating with fusional remnants

## Geographic Distribution

English is a truly global language, serving as a primary or official language on every inhabited continent. Its core native-speaking regions include the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and Ireland. English holds official or co-official status in numerous countries across Africa (Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, South Africa, Uganda, Tanzania, Cameroon, Zimbabwe), Asia (India, Pakistan, the Philippines, Singapore, Hong Kong, Malaysia), the Caribbean (Jamaica, Trinidad and Tobago, Barbados, The Bahamas), and the Pacific (Papua New Guinea, Fiji, Samoa). In total, English has some form of official status in over 60 sovereign states and numerous territories.

Beyond its official domains, English functions as the dominant international lingua franca in diplomacy, science, aviation, maritime communication, computing, academia, and global commerce. It is the most widely studied second language in the world, with learners numbering in the hundreds of millions across virtually every country.

## Official Status

English is the de jure official language of countries such as the United Kingdom (by convention rather than statute until recent legislation), the United States (at the federal level it is de facto, though many states have declared it official), Australia, New Zealand, Canada (alongside French), India (alongside Hindi and other scheduled languages), and many others. It serves as one of the six official languages of the United Nations and is an official language of the European Union, the African Union, the Commonwealth of Nations, NATO, and numerous other international organizations.

## Historical Development

### Old English (c. 450-1100)
The history of English begins with the migration of Germanic tribes -- the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes -- from continental Europe to the British Isles in the 5th century. These settlers brought with them closely related West Germanic dialects that gradually displaced the native Celtic languages in much of what is now England. Old English was a highly inflected language with grammatical gender, complex case systems for nouns and adjectives, and verb conjugations that would be largely unrecognizable to modern English speakers. Key literary works from this period include the epic poem Beowulf and the writings of King Alfred the Great. Scandinavian influence from Viking invasions beginning in the 8th century introduced Norse vocabulary and may have contributed to the simplification of English inflectional morphology.

### Middle English (c. 1100-1500)
The Norman Conquest of 1066 profoundly transformed English. Norman French became the language of the court, law, and aristocracy, while English continued as the language of the common people. Over several centuries, massive amounts of French and Latin vocabulary entered the language, creating the characteristic English pattern of having both Germanic and Romance words for many concepts (e.g., "begin" and "commence," "ask" and "inquire"). During this period, English lost most of its inflectional endings, grammatical gender largely disappeared, and word order became increasingly fixed. Geoffrey Chaucer's Canterbury Tales (late 14th century) is the most celebrated literary work of this era.

### Early Modern English (c. 1500-1700)
The Renaissance brought a flood of Latin and Greek borrowings into English. The Great Vowel Shift dramatically altered the pronunciation of long vowels, creating much of the disconnect between English spelling and pronunciation that persists today. The introduction of the printing press by William Caxton in 1476 helped standardize spelling and grammar. William Shakespeare's works and the King James Bible (1611) are landmark texts from this period that significantly shaped the development of modern English vocabulary and idiom.

### Modern English (c. 1700-present)
The expansion of the British Empire from the 17th through 20th centuries spread English across the globe, establishing it as a native language in North America, Australia, New Zealand, and parts of Africa and Asia. The rise of the United States as a global superpower in the 20th century further cemented English as the world's dominant international language. The advent of the internet, global media, popular culture, and international commerce in the late 20th and early 21st centuries accelerated English's role as a global lingua franca. English continues to evolve rapidly, absorbing loanwords from languages worldwide and developing distinct regional varieties (American, British, Australian, Indian, South African, Nigerian English, among many others).
