The Industrial Revolution, which began in Britain during the latter half of the eighteenth century, fundamentally transformed the economic and social fabric of the Western world. What started as a series of innovations in textile manufacturing and iron production gradually expanded into a comprehensive reshaping of how goods were made, transported, and consumed. The development of the steam engine, initially used to pump water from coal mines, proved to be the pivotal invention that powered factories, railways, and steamships, connecting regions and markets in ways that had previously been impossible.

The consequences of industrialization were far-reaching and complex. On one hand, the new factories and machines dramatically increased the production of goods, lowered prices for consumers, and created entirely new industries and forms of employment. Cities grew at an unprecedented rate as workers migrated from rural areas in search of factory jobs, and new technologies in agriculture meant that fewer hands were needed to feed the growing population. On the other hand, the conditions in early industrial workplaces were often appalling, with long hours, dangerous machinery, child labour, and wages that barely covered the cost of subsistence. The overcrowded cities that sprang up around factories suffered from inadequate sanitation, polluted air and water, and widespread disease.

Over time, the most egregious abuses of the industrial system prompted social reform movements that fought for better working conditions, limits on child labour, public health measures, and the extension of voting rights to working-class men and eventually to women. Trade unions emerged as a powerful force for collective bargaining, and governments gradually accepted a greater role in regulating economic activity and providing social services. These struggles and their outcomes laid the groundwork for the modern welfare state and the labour protections that workers in industrialized nations enjoy today, though the tension between economic growth and social justice remains a defining theme of political debate well into the present century.
