The rapid evolution of artificial intelligence over the past decade has brought about changes that few outside the research community could have predicted even a short time ago. Machine learning systems, which improve their performance on tasks by analysing vast quantities of data rather than following explicitly programmed rules, have achieved remarkable results in areas ranging from image recognition and natural language processing to medical diagnosis and autonomous vehicle navigation. These systems are now embedded in countless everyday products and services, from the recommendation algorithms that suggest what to watch or buy online to the voice assistants that respond to spoken commands on smartphones and smart speakers.

One of the most significant recent developments has been the emergence of large language models capable of generating coherent and contextually appropriate text across a wide range of topics and styles. These models, trained on enormous corpora of text drawn from books, websites, and other sources, can summarize documents, answer questions, translate between languages, write code, and engage in extended conversations that often feel remarkably natural. While these capabilities have generated considerable excitement about potential applications in education, customer service, creative writing, and software development, they have also raised important questions about accuracy, bias, intellectual property, and the potential displacement of human workers in fields that were previously thought to require distinctly human cognitive abilities.

The infrastructure supporting modern technology continues to expand and evolve. Cloud computing services allow businesses and individuals to access powerful processing and storage resources without investing in expensive hardware, while advances in semiconductor design and manufacturing continue to increase the speed and efficiency of the chips that underpin digital devices. The rollout of faster wireless networks has enabled new applications in areas such as remote surgery, augmented reality, and the Internet of Things, in which everyday objects from refrigerators to streetlights are connected to the internet and can communicate with one another to optimise their operations and provide useful data to their owners and operators.
