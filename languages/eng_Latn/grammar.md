# English Grammar

## Typological Overview

English is primarily an analytic (isolating) language with significant fusional remnants from its Germanic heritage. It relies heavily on word order and function words (prepositions, articles, auxiliary verbs) rather than inflectional morphology to express grammatical relationships. However, it retains some inflection in its verb system (third-person singular -s, past tense -ed, participles), noun plurals (-s/-es), pronoun case distinctions, and comparative/superlative adjective forms (-er/-est).

## Word Order

English has a relatively fixed Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) word order, which is crucial for determining grammatical roles since the language has largely lost its case-marking system. Departures from SVO order are possible but are typically marked and carry special pragmatic or emphatic force.

- **Declarative:** The dog (S) chased (V) the cat (O).
- **Interrogative (yes/no):** Did the dog chase the cat? (auxiliary fronting)
- **Interrogative (wh-):** What did the dog chase? (wh-fronting + auxiliary inversion)
- **Topicalization:** The cat, the dog chased. (marked, emphatic)
- **Existential:** There is a book on the table. (dummy subject)
- **Passive:** The cat was chased by the dog. (object promotion)

Adjectives precede nouns (a "big red ball"), and adverbs have relatively flexible placement but most commonly follow the verb or appear at sentence boundaries.

## Phonology

### Vowels
English has one of the largest vowel inventories among European languages, with approximately 12-15 monophthongs and 3-8 diphthongs, depending on dialect. The exact inventory varies significantly between dialects (e.g., British Received Pronunciation vs. General American vs. Australian English).

Common monophthongs include: /i, I, e, E, ae, A, Q, O, U, u, V, @/ (using SAMPA notation). Major diphthongs include /eI, aI, OI, @U, aU, I@, e@, U@/.

A defining feature of English vowels is vowel reduction: unstressed vowels in connected speech tend to reduce to schwa /@/ or /I/, giving English its characteristic rhythmic pattern. This is a powerful diagnostic feature since many languages do not exhibit such extensive vowel reduction.

### Consonants
English has approximately 24 consonant phonemes. Notable consonants include:

- **Dental fricatives /T/ and /D/:** The sounds in "think" and "this" are relatively rare cross-linguistically and are highly diagnostic of English. They are spelled with the digraph "th."
- **Post-alveolar affricates /tS/ and /dZ/:** As in "church" and "judge."
- **Approximant /r/:** Realized as a retroflex or bunched approximant in most dialects (unlike the trills and taps found in many other European languages).
- **Dark /l/:** In many dialects, /l/ has an allophonic "dark" variant [5] in syllable codas.

English permits complex consonant clusters in both onset and coda positions:
- Onset: up to three consonants (e.g., "strengths" /str-/, "splice" /spl-/)
- Coda: up to four consonants (e.g., "sixths" /-ksTs/, "texts" /-ksts/)

### Prosody
English is a stress-timed language, meaning that stressed syllables tend to occur at roughly regular intervals, with unstressed syllables compressed between them. This contrasts with syllable-timed languages like French or Spanish. Lexical stress is phonemic in English and can distinguish words (e.g., "REcord" noun vs. "reCORD" verb). Sentence-level intonation patterns convey meaning: rising intonation for yes/no questions, falling intonation for statements and wh-questions.

## Noun System

### Gender
English has no grammatical gender. Nouns do not belong to gender classes and do not require agreement with adjectives or determiners. Natural gender is expressed only through pronouns (he/she/it/they) and a small set of gendered nouns (actor/actress, waiter/waitress), though many of these are falling out of use.

### Case
English has almost entirely lost its case system. Only personal pronouns retain case distinctions:
- **Nominative (subject):** I, you, he, she, it, we, they
- **Accusative (object):** me, you, him, her, it, us, them
- **Genitive (possessive):** my/mine, your/yours, his, her/hers, its, our/ours, their/theirs
- **Nouns:** The possessive/genitive is formed with the clitic 's (the dog's bone) or the preposition "of" (the leg of the table).

### Number
English has a two-way number distinction: singular and plural. Regular plurals are formed by adding -s/-es (cat/cats, box/boxes), with numerous irregular forms (child/children, mouse/mice, sheep/sheep, criterion/criteria).

### Articles and Determiners
English has a definite article "the" and an indefinite article "a" (before consonant sounds) / "an" (before vowel sounds). The use of articles is obligatory in many syntactic contexts where other languages might use bare nouns, making article patterns a strong diagnostic feature of English text.

## Verb System

### Tense and Aspect
English has a rich system of tense and aspect combinations built with auxiliary verbs:

| | Simple | Progressive | Perfect | Perfect Progressive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Present** | I write | I am writing | I have written | I have been writing |
| **Past** | I wrote | I was writing | I had written | I had been writing |
| **Future** | I will write | I will be writing | I will have written | I will have been writing |

### Auxiliary Verbs and Modals
English makes extensive use of auxiliary verbs. The primary auxiliaries are "be," "have," and "do." Modal verbs include "can," "could," "may," "might," "shall," "should," "will," "would," "must," and semi-modals like "ought to," "need to," "have to," and "be going to."

### Do-Support
A distinctive feature of English is "do-support": the auxiliary verb "do" is inserted in negative sentences and questions when no other auxiliary is present:
- Statement: She likes coffee.
- Negative: She does not (doesn't) like coffee.
- Question: Does she like coffee?

This feature is highly unusual among world languages and is a strong diagnostic marker for English.

### Phrasal Verbs
English makes prolific use of phrasal verbs -- verb + particle combinations whose meaning is often non-compositional:
- "give up" (surrender), "look after" (care for), "put off" (postpone), "break down" (malfunction), "figure out" (solve), "come across" (encounter)

Phrasal verbs are extremely common in everyday English and rare in formal registers, where Latinate single-word verbs are preferred. Their frequency is a strong indicator of English text.

### Subject-Verb Agreement
English retains minimal subject-verb agreement. In the present tense, third-person singular subjects take the suffix -s on the verb (she walks, he runs), while all other persons use the bare form. The verb "be" is the most irregular, with distinct forms: am, is, are (present); was, were (past).

## Syntax

### Question Formation
English forms questions through subject-auxiliary inversion. If no auxiliary is present, "do" is inserted:
- "You are going." -> "Are you going?"
- "She left." -> "Did she leave?"

Wh-questions front the interrogative word: "What did you see?" "Where are they going?"

### Relative Clauses
English uses relative pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, that) to introduce relative clauses. The relative pronoun can often be omitted in object relatives: "The book (that) I read was excellent."

### Negation
Negation is typically formed with "not" placed after the first auxiliary verb: "She has not arrived." In colloquial speech, contracted negative forms are standard: "hasn't," "didn't," "won't," "can't." Standard English does not permit negative concord (double negatives), unlike many other languages and some non-standard English dialects.

### Complementation and Subordination
English uses a wide range of subordinating conjunctions (because, although, if, when, while, since, unless, until, whereas) and complementizers (that, whether, if) to build complex sentences. Infinitival complements with "to" (I want to go) and gerund-participial complements with "-ing" (I enjoy swimming) are both productive patterns.

### Passive Voice
The passive is formed with "be" + past participle: "The letter was written by Mary." The agent can be omitted: "The letter was written." Passives are common in formal and academic English.

## Morphology

English derivational morphology is highly productive, drawing on both Germanic and Romance/Latin resources:
- **Prefixes:** un-, re-, dis-, mis-, pre-, post-, over-, under-, anti-, non-
- **Suffixes:** -ness, -ment, -tion/-sion, -ity, -ous, -ful, -less, -able/-ible, -ly, -ize/-ise, -er/-or, -ist, -ism

Compounding is freely productive: "blackboard," "earthquake," "sunshine," "football," "newspaper." Both closed compounds, hyphenated compounds, and open compounds (noun + noun phrases like "bus stop") are common.
