# English vs confusable languages

English shares the Latin script with every other Germanic language in this set, but its lack of diacritics and heavy use of auxiliaries make it easy to separate. The main confusion vector is short snippets, or stretches of English loanwords embedded in Dutch, Afrikaans, or Frisian text.

## eng ↔ nld (Dutch)

- English has no `ij`, no `-je`/`-tje` diminutive, no long double vowels `aa/ee/oo/uu`.
- Dutch function words *het, de, een, en, van, niet, ook, zijn, hebben* are absent in English.
- Dutch uses `z-` initial where English has `s-` (*zee* vs *sea*, *zon* vs *sun*).

## eng ↔ afr (Afrikaans)

- Afrikaans contains many English loanwords, which can fool short-snippet detection. Diagnostic is the Afrikaans double negation *nie … nie* and the invariant copula *is* used for all persons.
- Afrikaans lacks the `-ing` progressive and the `-ed` past; verbs have no person or tense inflection.
- Afrikaans uses `y` heavily for /əi/ (*my, jy, hy, sy*), contrasting with English *my/I/he/she*.

## eng ↔ fry (Frisian)

- Frisian uses diacritic vowels `â, ê, û, ô` — English has none.
- Frisian function words *de, it, in, en, ek, net, hy, sy, jimme*; surface sometimes resembles English (*it* = 'the/it', *en* = 'and') but co-occurs with diacritics and unusual digraphs *ea, ie, oa*.

## eng ↔ deu (German)

- German capitalises all common nouns; English capitalises only proper nouns and sentence-initial words.
- German `ß` and `ä/ö/ü` are strong negative cues for English; their absence plus presence of *the* strongly indicates English.
