# Distinguishing English from Similar Languages

## English vs. Scots (sco) -- Most Confusable

Scots is the language most frequently confused with English in language identification, as both descend from Old English and share extensive vocabulary and structural similarities. Key distinguishing features:

- **Scots-specific vocabulary:** Scots uses words like "ken" (know), "braw" (fine), "wee" (small), "aye" (yes/always), "dinnae/didnae" (don't/didn't), "cannae" (can't), "hae" (have), "nae" (no/not), "auld" (old), "hoose" (house), "mair" (more), "oot" (out), "aboot" (about), and "guid" (good). The presence of these words strongly suggests Scots rather than English.
- **Spelling differences:** Scots often uses "ou" or "oo" where English uses other vowel spellings: "hoose" (house), "moose" (mouse), "oot" (out), "doon" (down), "toon" (town). Scots uses "ae" where English uses "one" or "o": "ane" (one), "bane" (bone), "stane" (stone).
- **Negation patterns:** Scots uses "nae" and "-nae" suffix (cannae, dinnae, willnae) rather than English "not" and "-n't" (can't, don't, won't).
- **Verb forms:** Scots uses "is" for all persons in present tense contexts where English differentiates ("we is" vs. "we are"). The past participle marker "en" is common in Scots.
- **Function words:** English "from" vs. Scots "frae"; English "to" vs. Scots "tae"; English "do" vs. Scots "dae"; English "who" vs. Scots "wha."

When identifying text, the presence of standard English function words (the, of, and, to, is, with, not, don't, can't) and standard English spelling patterns strongly indicates English rather than Scots.

## English vs. Dutch (nld)

Dutch is a close West Germanic relative of English, and some short texts can appear superficially similar:

- **Diagnostic English features absent in Dutch:** The digraph "th" (the, this, that, with, through) does not appear in native Dutch words. Dutch uses "de" and "het" as articles rather than "the." English "and" vs. Dutch "en." English function words "the, is, of, for, with, that" are distinctive.
- **Dutch-specific features:** Dutch uses "ij" and "oe" digraphs extensively (e.g., "zijn," "goed," "boek"). Dutch has "van," "een," "het," "voor," "niet" as common function words. Dutch uses many double vowels (aa, ee, oo, uu) more systematically than English.
- **Word endings:** Dutch uses "-lijk" where English uses "-ly"; Dutch "-heid" corresponds to English "-hood" or "-ness"; Dutch "-en" as infinitive ending vs. English "to + verb."

## English vs. German (deu)

Despite being closely related Germanic languages, English and German are generally easy to distinguish:

- **Capitalization:** German capitalizes all nouns, which is the single most reliable visual distinguishing feature. English capitalizes only proper nouns and sentence-initial words.
- **Word length:** German freely forms long compound words ("Donaudampfschifffahrt"), while English tends to use spaces between compound elements.
- **Function words:** German "der/die/das" vs. English "the"; German "und" vs. English "and"; German "ist" vs. English "is"; German "nicht" vs. English "not."
- **Characters:** German uses umlauts (a, o, u) and eszett (ss), which do not appear in English text.
- **Word order:** German has V2 word order in main clauses and verb-final order in subordinate clauses, creating very different sentence structures from English SVO.

## English vs. French (fra)

French is the largest source of loanwords in English, which can cause overlap in vocabulary, but the languages are structurally distinct:

- **Diacritics:** French extensively uses accents (e, e, e, a, u, i, o, c), which are absent in native English words. The presence of accented characters is a strong indicator of French.
- **Function words:** French "le/la/les" vs. English "the"; French "de/du/des" vs. English "of/from"; French "et" vs. English "and"; French "est" vs. English "is"; French "ne...pas" negation vs. English "not."
- **Spelling patterns:** French words often end in silent consonants (-ent, -ait, -eux). French uses "eau" for /o/, "ou" for /u/, "oi" for /wa/. The trigram "the" is extremely common in English but rare in French.
- **Shared vocabulary with different forms:** Many English-French cognates differ in suffix: English "-tion" vs. French "-tion" (identical but with different pronunciation), English "-ty" vs. French "-te," English "-ous" vs. French "-eux/-euse."

## English vs. Tok Pisin (tpi)

Tok Pisin is an English-based creole spoken in Papua New Guinea. While much of its vocabulary derives from English, it has a distinct grammar and spelling system:

- **Tok Pisin markers:** "bilong" (of/belonging to), "long" (at/to/in), "i" (predicate marker), "em" (him/her/it/the), "-im" (transitive suffix), "ol" (plural marker), "bai" (future marker), "-pela" (adjective suffix).
- **Simplified vocabulary:** Tok Pisin uses a smaller vocabulary with broader semantic ranges. "Pikinini" (child), "meri" (woman), "man" (man/person), "haus" (house), "kai" (food/eat).
- **Spelling:** Tok Pisin uses a phonemic spelling system that differs from English: "yu" (you), "mi" (me/I), "yumi" (we inclusive), "tok" (talk), "save" (know/can), "kamap" (come up/become).

## English vs. Other English-Based Creoles

English-based creoles (Jamaican Patois, Nigerian Pidgin, Krio, Bislama, etc.) share much vocabulary with English but have distinctive features:

- **Reduced inflection:** Creoles typically lack English inflectional morphology (-s, -ed, -ing used differently or absent).
- **Different function words:** Creoles develop their own function word systems from English lexical items (e.g., Jamaican "fi" for "to/for," "dem" as plural marker, "a" as progressive marker).
- **Spelling variation:** Creoles often use phonemic spellings reflecting their own phonology.

## Summary of Key English Diagnostic Features

The most reliable features for identifying English text are:

1. **The word "the"** -- the most frequent word, accounting for 6-7% of all tokens
2. **"th-" words** -- the, this, that, they, them, their, there, then, than, these, those, through, think, thing
3. **Function word set** -- of, and, to, in, a, is, for, was, on, are, with, as, not, but, have, had
4. **Absence of diacritics** -- no accented characters in native vocabulary
5. **"-tion" ending** -- extremely common nominal suffix
6. **"-ing" ending** -- progressive/gerund marker, very frequent
7. **"ough" spelling** -- unique to English (though, through, thought, enough)
8. **Do-support patterns** -- "do not," "does not," "did not" constructions
9. **Standard noun capitalization** -- only proper nouns capitalized (distinguishes from German)
10. **Article usage** -- "the," "a," "an" used pervasively and consistently
