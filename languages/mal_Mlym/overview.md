# Malayalam (mal_Mlym) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name**: Malayalam (മലയാളം)
- **ISO 639-3 Code**: mal
- **ISO 639-1 Code**: ml
- **Script**: Malayalam (Mlym)
- **Language Family**: Dravidian > South Dravidian > Tamil-Kannada > Tamil-Kodagu > Tamil-Malayalam > Malayalam
- **Native Speakers**: ~38 million (2011 census)
- **Total Speakers**: ~40 million including second-language speakers

## Classification

Malayalam belongs to the Dravidian language family, one of the major language families of South Asia. Within the Dravidian family, it is classified under the South Dravidian branch, specifically in the Tamil-Malayalam subgroup. Malayalam is most closely related to Tamil, with the two languages having diverged around the 9th century CE. Before this divergence, the region spoke a form of Tamil-Malayalam or proto-Tamil-Malayalam, and early inscriptions in Kerala show a mixture of Tamil and Malayalam features. The separation became more definitive as Malayalam developed its own literary tradition and absorbed significant influence from Sanskrit, which distinguishes it from Tamil in both vocabulary and phonology.

## Geographic Distribution

Malayalam is primarily spoken in the Indian state of Kerala, located on the southwestern coast of the Indian subcontinent. It is also spoken in the union territory of Lakshadweep, a group of islands in the Arabian Sea. Beyond these primary regions, Malayalam is spoken in the border areas of neighboring states, particularly in the Nilgiris and Coimbatore districts of Tamil Nadu, and in the Kodagu and Mangalore regions of Karnataka.

Kerala has a population of approximately 35 million people, and Malayalam serves as the principal language of education, government, and daily communication throughout the state. The language has a strong literary tradition dating back to at least the 12th century, with roots in earlier Tamil-Malayalam literary works.

## Diaspora Communities

Malayalam has a significant diaspora presence globally. The most substantial expatriate communities are found in the Gulf states, particularly in the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, and Oman. The Malayalam-speaking population in the Gulf is estimated at several million, making it one of the largest Indian diaspora language communities in the region. These communities maintain strong cultural and linguistic ties to Kerala, supporting Malayalam-language media, cultural organizations, and educational institutions.

Significant Malayalam-speaking communities also exist in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, Malaysia, and Singapore. In the United States, concentrations of Malayalam speakers can be found in metropolitan areas such as New York, Chicago, Houston, Dallas, and cities in New Jersey and California. In the UK, London and other major cities host sizable Malayali communities.

## Official Status

Malayalam holds official language status in the state of Kerala and the union territory of Lakshadweep. It is one of the 22 scheduled languages recognized by the Eighth Schedule of the Constitution of India. In 2013, Malayalam was granted Classical Language status by the Government of India, recognizing its antiquity and rich literary heritage. This designation acknowledges that Malayalam has a documented history spanning over a thousand years, a body of ancient literature, and an original literary tradition that is not borrowed from another language.

## Historical Development

The history of Malayalam can be broadly divided into several periods. Old Malayalam (approximately 9th to 13th century) represents the stage when the language began distinguishing itself from Tamil, with increasing Sanskrit influence. Middle Malayalam (13th to 16th century) saw the consolidation of Malayalam as a distinct language with its own grammar and literary conventions. Modern Malayalam (17th century onward) is characterized by standardization, the development of prose literature, and the influence of European languages through colonial contact, particularly Portuguese, Dutch, and English.

The development of Malayalam literature has been profoundly shaped by the Manipravalam tradition, which freely mixed Malayalam and Sanskrit. This bilingual literary style influenced the absorption of a vast Sanskrit vocabulary into Malayalam, giving the language a dual Dravidian-Sanskrit character that persists to this day.

## Writing System

Malayalam uses its own script, the Malayalam script, which is derived from the Grantha script, itself a descendant of the Brahmi script. The modern Malayalam script evolved through several stages, with significant reforms in the 1970s and 1980s that simplified many of the complex conjunct characters. The script is written from left to right and is an abugida, where consonant characters carry an inherent vowel that can be modified or suppressed using diacritical marks.

## Sociolinguistic Features

Malayalam is notable for its relatively high literacy rate in India. Kerala consistently ranks among the top Indian states in literacy, with rates exceeding 90%. The language has a vibrant media landscape including newspapers, television channels, film industry, and digital content. The Malayalam film industry, often referred to as Mollywood, is one of the most critically acclaimed regional film industries in India, known for its realistic and socially conscious cinema.

The language exhibits dialectal variation based on geography, caste, and religion, though mutual intelligibility is generally maintained across dialects. Major dialectal regions include the northern (Malabar), central (Kochi), and southern (Travancore) areas of Kerala.
