# Georgian (ქართული)

## Basic Information

- **Language name**: Georgian (ქართული ენა, kartuli ena)
- **ISO 639-3 code**: kat
- **ISO 639-1 code**: ka
- **Language family**: Kartvelian (South Caucasian)
- **Script**: Georgian / Mkhedruli (მხედრული)
- **BCP 47 tag**: kat_Geor
- **Number of speakers**: Approximately 3.7 million native speakers
- **Regions**: Georgia (primary), diaspora communities in Turkey, Russia, Iran, Europe, and North America
- **Official status**: Official language of Georgia (საქართველო)

## Language Family

Georgian belongs to the Kartvelian (South Caucasian) language family, which is not demonstrably related to any other language family in the world. The Kartvelian family comprises four languages: Georgian, Mingrelian (მეგრული), Laz (ლაზური), and Svan (სვანური). Georgian is the most widely spoken and the only Kartvelian language with a long-standing literary tradition. Proto-Kartvelian is estimated to have split into its daughter languages around the 2nd millennium BCE, with Svan diverging first, followed by the separation of Georgian from the Zan branch (Mingrelian and Laz).

## Script

Georgian uses the Mkhedruli (მხედრული) script, one of the world's unique writing systems. The script consists of 33 letters, each representing a single phoneme, making it a nearly perfect alphabetic system. Georgian is one of only 14 scripts currently in use worldwide that are used for a single language or a small group of closely related languages.

The Georgian writing tradition encompasses three historical scripts:

1. **Asomtavruli** (ასომთავრული) — the oldest, dating from the 5th century CE, also known as Mrgvlovani (rounded script)
2. **Nuskhuri** (ნუსხური) — developed around the 9th century, used primarily for ecclesiastical texts
3. **Mkhedruli** (მხედრული) — the modern script, which emerged in the 11th century and became dominant for secular use

In 2016, the three Georgian scripts were collectively inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, recognizing their cultural significance. The modern Mkhedruli script has no uppercase or lowercase distinction, which is a notable feature among the world's writing systems.

## Historical Overview

Georgian has one of the oldest literary traditions in the world. The earliest known Georgian inscription dates to approximately 430 CE, found at a church in Bolnisi (ბოლნისი) in southern Georgia. Georgian literature flourished from the 5th century onward, with early works consisting primarily of hagiographic and religious texts translated from Greek, Syriac, and Armenian.

The golden age of Georgian literature is associated with the medieval period, particularly the 12th century, when Shota Rustaveli (შოთა რუსთაველი) composed "The Knight in the Panther's Skin" (ვეფხისტყაოსანი), considered the masterpiece of Georgian literature and one of the great works of world literature. This era coincided with the political and cultural zenith of the Georgian kingdom under Queen Tamar (თამარ მეფე).

Throughout its history, Georgian has maintained its distinct identity despite the influence of Persian, Arabic, Turkish, and Russian during various periods of foreign domination. The language absorbed loanwords from these contact languages while preserving its fundamental Kartvelian character.

Modern literary Georgian is based on the eastern Kartlian dialect, which was standardized in the 19th century. The language underwent a period of intensive modernization during the Soviet era, when technical and scientific terminology was developed, often through calques and borrowings from Russian. Since Georgian independence in 1991, there has been a conscious effort to develop native Georgian terminology and reduce dependence on Russian loanwords.

## Typological Features

Georgian is typologically notable for several features: its complex consonant cluster system (allowing words like გვფრცქვნი, gvprtskvni, "you peel us"), its split-ergative case alignment system, polypersonal verb agreement, and a rich system of verbal morphology. These features make Georgian one of the most morphologically complex languages in the Caucasus region, which is itself known for linguistic diversity and complexity.
