# Polish (pol_Latn)

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Polish (polski)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** pol
- **ISO 639-1 code:** pl
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > West Slavic > Lechitic
- **Script:** Latin (with Polish diacritical marks)
- **Native speakers:** approximately 45 million
- **Total speakers:** approximately 50 million (including L2 speakers)
- **Official status:** Official language of Poland; official language of the European Union
- **Primary region:** Poland
- **Diaspora communities:** United States, United Kingdom, Germany, Canada, France, Brazil, Australia, Ireland

## History

Polish belongs to the Lechitic subgroup of West Slavic languages and is the most widely spoken language in this branch. Its development can be traced through several distinct historical periods, reflecting the turbulent history of the Polish nation.

The earliest written records in Polish date from the 12th and 13th centuries. The "Bull of Gniezno" (Bulla gnieznienska) from 1136 contains 410 Polish proper names, providing valuable evidence of early Polish phonology. The first complete sentence recorded in Polish appears in the "Book of Henrykow" (Ksiega henrykowska) from 1270: "Day, ut ia pobrusa, a ti poziwai" (Let me grind, and you take a rest), attributed to a Czech settler speaking to his Polish wife.

The development of literary Polish accelerated during the Renaissance period in the 16th century, often considered the Golden Age of Polish literature. Writers such as Jan Kochanowski, Mikolaj Rej, and Andrzej Frycz Modrzewski established Polish as a sophisticated literary language. Mikolaj Rej famously declared that Poles have their own language and need not rely on Latin, marking a turning point in the acceptance of Polish as a language of scholarship and high culture.

During the period of the Partitions of Poland (1795-1918), when Poland ceased to exist as an independent state, the Polish language became a crucial marker of national identity. The occupying powers -- Russia, Prussia, and Austria -- attempted various policies of linguistic suppression, particularly in the Russian and Prussian partitions. Despite these pressures, Polish not only survived but continued to develop, partly through clandestine education and cultural institutions.

The interwar period (1918-1939) saw significant efforts to standardize the language and expand its use in administration, science, and education. After World War II and the significant territorial and demographic changes that followed, Polish became considerably more homogeneous as regional dialects diminished under the influence of mass media, urbanization, and universal education.

## Geographic Distribution

Poland remains the primary homeland of the Polish language, where it is spoken by nearly the entire population of approximately 38 million. Significant Polish-speaking communities exist throughout the world, largely as a result of successive waves of emigration driven by political upheaval and economic factors. The largest diaspora communities are found in the United States (estimated 9-10 million people of Polish descent), the United Kingdom (approximately 800,000 Polish-born residents), Germany, Canada, Brazil, and Australia. Polish is also spoken by minority populations in neighboring countries, including Lithuania, Belarus, Ukraine, and the Czech Republic.

## Dialects

Polish dialects are traditionally divided into five main groups: Greater Polish (Wielkopolska), Lesser Polish (Malopolska), Masovian (Mazowsze), Silesian (Slask), and Kashubian (Kaszuby). Kashubian is sometimes classified as a separate language and has its own literary tradition and official recognition as a regional language in Poland. Standard Polish is based primarily on the dialects of Greater Poland and Lesser Poland, with significant influence from the Warsaw variety of Masovian.

## Writing System

Polish uses the Latin alphabet augmented with nine diacritical letters: a with ogonek (a), c with acute (c), e with ogonek (e), l with stroke (l), n with acute (n), o with acute (o), s with acute (s), z with acute (z), and z with overdot (z). The alphabet does not include the letters q, v, or x, which appear only in foreign borrowings. Polish orthography also makes extensive use of digraphs such as sz, cz, rz, dz, and the trigraph dzz.
