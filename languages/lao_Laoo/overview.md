# Lao (ພາສາລາວ)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Lao (ພາສາລາວ, phasa lao)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** lao
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** lo
- **Script:** Lao script (Laoo)
- **Language Family:** Kra-Dai > Tai > Southwestern Tai > Lao-Phutai
- **Total Speakers:** Approximately 30 million (including Isan dialect speakers)
- **Native Speakers:** ~7 million in Laos; ~20 million Isan speakers in northeastern Thailand
- **Official Status:** Official language of the Lao People's Democratic Republic

## Geographic Distribution

Lao is the official and dominant language of Laos (Lao PDR), where it serves as the national language and the medium of government, education, and media. The language is spoken natively throughout the Mekong River valley and the lowland plains of the country. Vientiane, the capital, serves as the center of the standard dialect.

Beyond the borders of Laos, Lao is closely related to and largely mutually intelligible with the Isan language spoken in the northeastern provinces of Thailand (Isan region). The Isan region, encompassing approximately 20 provinces of Thailand, is home to the largest population of ethnic Lao speakers in the world. While Isan is often classified as a dialect of Lao, it has been increasingly influenced by Central Thai due to national education policy and media exposure. Lao-speaking diaspora communities also exist in France, the United States, Canada, and Australia, largely as a result of emigration following political changes in 1975.

## Language Family

Lao belongs to the Southwestern branch of the Tai language family, which itself is part of the larger Kra-Dai (Tai-Kadai) language family. This family includes Thai, Shan, Zhuang, and numerous other languages spoken across mainland Southeast Asia and southern China. Within the Tai branch, Lao is most closely related to Thai, and the two languages share a high degree of mutual intelligibility, particularly at the colloquial level. Lao is also related to Phutai, Tai Dam (Black Tai), and other Tai languages spoken in the region.

## Script

The Lao script is an abugida (alphasyllabary) historically derived from the Khmer script, which itself descended from the Pallava script of South India via the Brahmi writing tradition. The script was adapted to write the Lao language and has undergone several reforms, most notably during the French colonial period and again after 1975, when the script was simplified and standardized by the Lao government. The modern Lao script contains fewer characters than the Thai script, as redundant consonants representing Sanskrit and Pali distinctions not present in spoken Lao were removed during reforms. Unlike Thai, Lao script does not use the visarga or multiple characters for the same phoneme.

The Lao script is written from left to right. Vowel signs can appear before, after, above, or below their associated consonant. Tone marks appear above the consonant. Notably, Lao script does not use spaces to separate individual words; instead, spaces are used to separate clauses or sentences, similar to traditional Thai orthography.

## Historical Overview

The history of the Lao language is closely tied to the history of the Tai peoples' southward migration from southern China into mainland Southeast Asia. The Tai-speaking peoples arrived in the Mekong region by approximately the 8th to 10th centuries CE, gradually establishing muang (principalities) throughout the area.

The Kingdom of Lan Xang (Lane Xang, "Million Elephants"), founded by Fa Ngum in 1353, was the first major Lao political entity and played a crucial role in the development of Lao as a literary and administrative language. Under Lan Xang, the Lao script was further developed and used for religious texts, royal chronicles, and legal documents. Theravada Buddhism, which became the dominant religion, brought Pali and Sanskrit loanwords into the Lao lexicon.

After the fragmentation of Lan Xang in 1707 into three rival kingdoms (Vientiane, Luang Prabang, and Champasak), the Lao-speaking territories came under increasing Siamese and later French colonial influence. The French colonial period (1893-1953) introduced French loanwords and led to the Romanization of Lao in administrative contexts, though the Lao script remained in common use. Following independence in 1953 and the establishment of the Lao PDR in 1975, the government undertook script reforms and language standardization based on the Vientiane dialect.

Today, Lao continues to evolve with loanwords from Thai, English, and French enriching its vocabulary, particularly in technology, science, and modern governance.
