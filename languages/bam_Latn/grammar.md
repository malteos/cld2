# Bambara (Bamanankan) - Grammar

## Phonology

### Vowels

Bambara has a rich vowel system with seven oral vowels and their nasal counterparts:

- **Oral vowels**: /a/, /e/, /ɛ/, /i/, /o/, /ɔ/, /u/
- **Nasal vowels**: /ã/, /ẽ/, /ɛ̃/, /ĩ/, /õ/, /ɔ̃/, /ũ/

Vowel length is contrastive in Bambara. Long vowels are written by doubling the vowel letter (e.g., "aa", "ee"). The distinction between /e/ and /ɛ/ and between /o/ and /ɔ/ is phonemic and is critical for meaning differentiation. For example, "se" (to arrive) versus "sɛ" (foot/base).

### Consonants

The consonant inventory includes:

- **Stops**: /b/, /d/, /g/, /p/, /t/, /k/
- **Nasals**: /m/, /n/, /ɲ/, /ŋ/
- **Fricatives**: /f/, /s/, /h/
- **Affricates**: /j/ (written "j"), /c/ (written "c")
- **Liquids**: /l/, /r/
- **Glides**: /w/, /y/

Prenasalized stops (/nd/, /ng/, /mb/, /nj/) are common and considered single consonant units phonologically.

### Tone

Bambara is a tonal language with two level tones: high and low. Tone carries both lexical and grammatical meaning. In the standard orthography, tones are generally not marked, which can create ambiguity in written text. Some educational and linguistic materials do mark tone using acute accent for high tone and grave accent for low tone, or by other conventions.

Examples of tonal minimal pairs:
- "ba" (high tone) = mother; "ba" (low tone) = goat
- "sa" (high tone) = snake; "sa" (low tone) = death

Tone also plays grammatical roles, such as distinguishing between declarative and interrogative sentences and marking certain verbal constructions.

## Morphological Type

Bambara is primarily an isolating (analytic) language, meaning that grammatical relationships are expressed mostly through separate words (particles, auxiliaries) rather than through inflectional morphology on nouns or verbs. However, it does exhibit some agglutinative features, particularly in derivational morphology, where suffixes can be added to roots to create new words.

## Noun System

### No Grammatical Gender

Bambara has no grammatical gender system. There is no distinction between masculine and feminine in articles, pronouns, or adjective agreement. When necessary, sex can be specified lexically (e.g., "cɛ" for male, "muso" for female).

### Definite and Indefinite Marking

The definite article is a suffix. The basic form is a low-tone vowel that harmonizes with the final vowel of the noun:
- After /a/, /ɛ/, /ɔ/: suffix is generally realized as a harmonizing vowel
- Common representation in the standard orthography is a final vowel lengthening or no overt marking in some analyses

The indefinite is often unmarked or uses "dɔ" (some/a certain).

### Plural Marking

The plural is formed by adding the suffix **-w** (sometimes written "-u" in older orthographies):
- "mɔgɔ" (person) -> "mɔgɔw" (people)
- "so" (house) -> "sow" (houses)
- "den" (child) -> "denw" (children)

### Postpositions

Bambara uses postpositions rather than prepositions. The noun or noun phrase comes before the relational marker:

- "so kɔnɔ" = house inside (in the house)
- "jiri kɔrɔ" = tree under (under the tree)
- "dugu la" = village at (at/in the village)
- "mɔgɔ fɛ" = person near/with (near/with the person)
- "faso kan" = country on (on the country)

Common postpositions include: "la" (at/in), "kɔnɔ" (inside), "kan" (on), "kɔrɔ" (under), "fɛ" (near/with/at), "na" (in/to), "bolo" (in the hand of / by means of).

## Pronoun System

Personal pronouns in Bambara:

| Person | Singular | Plural |
|--------|----------|--------|
| 1st | ne (I) | an (we) |
| 2nd | i (you) | aw (you pl.) |
| 3rd | a (he/she/it) | u (they) |

There is no gender distinction in the third person singular. The pronoun "a" covers he, she, and it.

## Verb System

### Aspect-Based System

Bambara's verbal system is fundamentally aspect-based rather than tense-based. The language uses auxiliary markers (often called "predicative markers" or "TAM markers") placed between the subject and the verb to indicate aspect, mood, and polarity:

- **bɛ** - progressive/habitual (imperfective affirmative): "A bɛ dumuni ke" (He/she is eating / eats)
- **tɛ** - negative imperfective: "A tɛ dumuni ke" (He/she is not eating / does not eat)
- **ye** - perfective affirmative (completed action): "A ye dumuni ke" (He/she ate / has eaten)
- **ma** - perfective negative: "A ma dumuni ke" (He/she did not eat)
- **ka** - subjunctive/infinitive: "A ka dumuni ke" (that he/she eat)
- **kana** - negative subjunctive/prohibitive: "I kana taa" (Don't go)
- **bɛna** - future/prospective: "A bɛna dumuni ke" (He/she will eat)
- **tɛna** - negative future: "A tɛna dumuni ke" (He/she will not eat)

### Compound Verbs

Many actions in Bambara are expressed through compound verb constructions using a light verb (such as "ke" = to do/make, "bɔ" = to go out, "don" = to enter) combined with a noun:

- "dumuni ke" = eating do = to eat
- "baara ke" = work do = to work
- "kuma fɔ" = word say = to speak

### Verbal Derivation

Verbs can take derivational suffixes to change meaning:
- **-la/-na** (agentive): "baara" (work) -> "baarakɛla" (worker)
- **-li/-ni** (nominalization): "kalan" (to study) -> "kalanni" (studying/education)

## Word Order

### Basic Word Order

The basic word order in Bambara is **SOV** (Subject-Object-Verb) when no auxiliary is present. However, the most common sentence structure includes an auxiliary marker, resulting in the pattern **S-Aux-O-V**:

- "Muso bɛ mɔgɔw dɛmɛ" = Woman AUX people help = "The woman helps the people"
- "Den ye ji min" = Child PERF water drink = "The child drank water"

### Modifier Order

In noun phrases, modifiers generally follow the noun:
- Adjectives follow the noun: "mɔgɔ jan" (person tall = tall person)
- Demonstratives follow the noun: "so nin" (house this = this house)
- Numerals follow the noun: "den fila" (child two = two children)
- Possessors precede the noun: "Awa ka so" (Awa POSS house = Awa's house)

### Relative Clauses

Relative clauses use the relativizer "min" and generally follow the head noun:
- "Cɛ min nana, o ye ne balimacɛ ye" = Man REL came, that is my brother (The man who came is my brother)

## Sentence Types

### Questions

Yes/no questions are formed by adding "wa" at the end of a declarative sentence:
- "I bɛ taa wa?" = You AUX go Q? = Are you going?

Content questions use interrogative words such as:
- "jɔn" (who), "mun" (what), "min" (which), "cogo di" (how), "ɲɔgɔn" (when)

### Negation

Negation is expressed through the negative auxiliary markers:
- Imperfective negative: "tɛ" (replacing "bɛ")
- Perfective negative: "ma" (replacing "ye")
- Future negative: "tɛna" (replacing "bɛna")

### Coordination

Coordination uses conjunctions:
- "ni" / "ani" (and)
- "walima" (or)
- "nka" (but)

## Focus and Topic

Bambara uses the particle "de" (also spelled "dɛ") for focus/emphasis:
- "Ne de ye a ke" = I FOCUS PERF it do = It is I who did it.

Topic marking can involve fronting the topicalized element:
- "O la, an bɛna taa" = That on, we will go = As for that, we will go.
