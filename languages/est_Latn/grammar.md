# Estonian Grammar

## Word Order

Estonian has a basic SVO (Subject-Verb-Object) word order, but this order is relatively free compared to English or other Germanic languages. Pragmatic considerations such as topic, focus, and information structure frequently motivate departures from the canonical SVO pattern. The verb typically occupies the second position in declarative main clauses (V2 tendency), a feature shared with Germanic languages but unusual among Uralic languages. In subordinate clauses, the verb tends to appear at the end. Question formation can involve inversion or the use of the particle *kas* without changing word order.

Examples of word order variation:
- SVO: *Poiss loeb raamatut.* (The boy reads a book.)
- OVS: *Raamatut loeb poiss.* (It is the book that the boy reads.)
- VSO: *Loeb poiss raamatut.* (The boy does read a book.)

## Phonology

### Vowels

Estonian has one of the richest vowel systems among European languages, with nine vowel phonemes:

| Front unrounded | Front rounded | Back |
|---|---|---|
| i | y (ü) | u |
| e | ø (o) | o |
| ae (a) | | a |

The vowels written as **a, e, i, o, u** correspond broadly to their continental European values. The additional vowels **a, o, u** (written *a, o, u* with diacritics) are:
- **o** (*o*) - a back unrounded vowel unique to Estonian among major European languages, written as **o** (with tilde)
- **a** (*a*) - a front low vowel, written as **a** (with umlaut)
- **o** (*o*) - a front rounded mid vowel, written as **o** (with umlaut)
- **u** (*u*) - a front rounded close vowel, written as **u** (with umlaut)

The special character **o** (with tilde, i.e. *o*) deserves particular attention as it represents a sound highly diagnostic of Estonian - a close-mid back unrounded vowel [ɤ] that does not exist in Finnish or most other European languages.

### Quantity System

Estonian possesses a typologically rare three-way quantity distinction (short, long, and overlong) that applies to both vowels and consonants. This system distinguishes three degrees of length:

1. **Short (Q1):** *koli* (junk) - single letter
2. **Long (Q2):** *kooli* (of school) - double letter
3. **Overlong (Q3):** *kooli* (to school) - also written with double letter but with different prosodic pattern

The distinction between Q2 and Q3 is not systematically reflected in standard orthography, making it a suprasegmental feature that must be learned. This three-way contrast is phonemic: minimal pairs exist that differ only in quantity degree. The overlong quantity involves not just increased duration but also characteristic pitch patterns (falling pitch on the first syllable).

### Consonants

Estonian has a moderately sized consonant inventory. The consonants *s* and *z* (written *s* and *z* with carons) appear primarily in loanwords and are considered marginal phonemes. Consonant clusters are permitted and can be complex, particularly in word-medial and word-final positions. Voicing is not phonemically distinctive for stops in native vocabulary; *p, t, k* represent voiceless unaspirated stops, and their voiced counterparts *b, d, g* represent voiceless lenis stops (shorter and weaker, but not truly voiced as in English or Russian).

## Noun Morphology

### Case System

Estonian has 14 grammatical cases, making it one of the most case-rich languages in Europe. There is no grammatical gender. Nouns distinguish singular and plural. The case system is divided into grammatical cases and semantic cases:

**Grammatical cases (form the core of the system):**

| Case | Singular ending | Function | Example (*maja* = house) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | - | Subject | *maja* |
| Genitive | varied | Possession, object | *maja* |
| Partitive | varied (-t, -d, -a) | Partial object, partitive | *maja* |

**Local cases (internal):**

| Case | Singular ending | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Illative | -sse, short form | Into | *majasse* |
| Inessive | -s | In | *majas* |
| Elative | -st | Out of | *majast* |

**Local cases (external):**

| Case | Singular ending | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Allative | -le | Onto | *majale* |
| Adessive | -l | On | *majal* |
| Ablative | -lt | Off of | *majalt* |

**Abstract/marginal cases:**

| Case | Singular ending | Function | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Translative | -ks | Becoming | *majaks* |
| Terminative | -ni | Until/up to | *majani* |
| Essive | -na | As | *majana* |
| Abessive | -ta | Without | *majata* |
| Comitative | -ga | With | *majaga* |

The first three cases (nominative, genitive, partitive) are the grammatical cases and show the most stem alternation and irregularity. The remaining eleven cases are formed relatively regularly by adding suffixes to the genitive stem (for singular) or the partitive stem (for plural). The illative case has both a regular long form (-*sse*) and frequently used short forms that involve stem changes.

### Noun Declension

Estonian nouns are traditionally organized into declension types based on their stem alternation patterns. The genitive and partitive forms are unpredictable and must be learned for each noun. Consonant gradation (a systematic alternation between strong and weak consonant grades) affects many nouns and verbs, though the system is less regular than in Finnish.

## Verb Morphology

### Moods

Estonian verbs distinguish four moods:

1. **Indicative** (*kindel koneviis*): The default mood for statements of fact. *Ma loen raamatut.* (I read a book.)
2. **Conditional** (*tingiv koneviis*): Formed with the suffix -*ks-*. *Ma loeksin raamatut.* (I would read a book.)
3. **Imperative** (*kasiv koneviis*): Used for commands. *Loe raamatut!* (Read a book!)
4. **Quotative** (*kaudne koneviis*): A distinctive feature of Estonian, used for reported speech or hearsay, formed with the suffix -*vat*. *Ta lugevat raamatut.* (He reportedly reads a book.) This mood is relatively rare among European languages and serves as a diagnostic feature of Estonian.

### Tenses

Estonian has a relatively simple tense system with two basic tenses:
- **Present** (*olevik*): *Ma loen.* (I read / I am reading.)
- **Past** (*minevik*): Simple past with -*si-* or -*i-*: *Ma lugesin.* (I read.)

Compound tenses are formed with the auxiliary *olema* (to be):
- **Present perfect:** *Ma olen lugenud.* (I have read.)
- **Past perfect:** *Ma olin lugenud.* (I had read.)

### Voice

Estonian distinguishes active and passive (impersonal) voice. The impersonal voice (*umbisikuline tegumood*) removes the agent entirely and is formed with specific suffixes:
- Present impersonal: *Loetakse raamatut.* (A book is being read / One reads a book.)
- Past impersonal: *Loeti raamatut.* (A book was read.)

### Negation

Negation in Estonian is expressed through negative auxiliary words rather than affixes:
- **ei** - negates indicative present and past: *Ma ei loe.* (I do not read.)
- **pole** (= ei ole) - negates the present perfect: *Ma pole lugenud.* (I have not read.)
- **ara** - negates imperative: *Ara loe!* (Don't read!)

The main verb appears in a special connegative form when negated, which differs from the affirmative form. This pattern is shared with Finnish but differs in specific details.

### Infinitives and Participles

Estonian has two infinitive forms:
- **-da infinitive** (supine): *lugeda* (to read)
- **-ma infinitive:** *lugema* (to read - used with motion verbs and certain constructions)

The *-ma* infinitive can take case endings (illative, inessive, elative, translative, abessive), creating a rich system of verbal constructions.

Participles include:
- Present active: *lugev* (reading)
- Past active: *lugenud* (having read)
- Present passive: *loetav* (being read, readable)
- Past passive: *loetud* (read, having been read)

## Syntax Features

### No Articles

Estonian lacks both definite and indefinite articles. Definiteness is conveyed through word order, demonstratives, context, and the case system (nominative vs. partitive can signal definiteness distinctions).

### Postpositions and Prepositions

Estonian uses both postpositions and prepositions, but postpositions are far more common. Postpositions typically govern the genitive case:
- *maja taga* (behind the house) - postposition
- *ilma majata* (without a house) - the comitative case can replace certain postpositional phrases

Some words function as prepositions:
- *ule tee* (across the road) - preposition

### Object Marking

The Estonian object system is complex. The case of the direct object (nominative, genitive, or partitive) signals aspect, completeness of action, and definiteness:
- Partitive object: incomplete action or partial object - *Ma loen raamatut.* (I am reading a book / I read books.)
- Genitive/nominative object: completed action with total object - *Ma lugesin raamatu labi.* (I read the book through.)

### Clause Structure

Subordinate clauses are typically introduced by conjunctions such as *et* (that), *kui* (when/if), *sest* (because), *kuigi* (although). Relative clauses use *kes* (who) for animate referents and *mis* (which/that) for inanimate referents. The verb in subordinate clauses tends to appear in clause-final position.

### Coordination

Coordinating conjunctions include *ja* (and), *ning* (and - more formal), *voi* (or), *aga* (but), *kuid* (but - more formal), *ent* (but/however).
