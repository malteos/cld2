# Estonian vs. Related Languages

## Estonian vs. Finnish

Finnish is the language most commonly confused with Estonian. Both belong to the Finnic branch of the Uralic family, and they share substantial structural similarities. However, numerous differences allow reliable discrimination.

### Vowel Inventory and Orthography
- Estonian has the letter **o** (o with tilde), representing the vowel [ɤ]. Finnish lacks this letter and this phoneme entirely. The presence of **o** in a text is a near-definitive marker of Estonian.
- Finnish maintains productive **vowel harmony** (front vowels a, o, y do not co-occur with back vowels a, o, u within native words). Estonian has largely **lost vowel harmony**, so front and back vowels mix freely. A word like *tootukassa* (unemployment fund) with both *oo* and *u* violating Finnish harmony rules is natural in Estonian.

### Quantity System
- Both languages have long vowels and consonants, but Estonian uniquely has a **three-way quantity contrast** (short/long/overlong). Finnish has only a two-way distinction (short/long).

### Case System
- Both languages are highly inflected, but Estonian has **14 cases** while Finnish has **15**. The specific forms differ considerably. For example:
  - Estonian comitative: *-ga* (*majaga* "with a house") vs. Finnish: no comitative case (uses postposition *kanssa*)
  - Estonian illative short forms are common and involve stem changes, while Finnish illative uses *-Vn* (vowel lengthening + n) or *-seen*.
  - Estonian abessive: *-ta* vs. Finnish abessive: *-tta/-tta*

### Negation
- Both languages use a negative auxiliary, but the forms differ:
  - Estonian: *ei* (invariable for all persons: *ma ei loe, sa ei loe, ta ei loe*)
  - Finnish: *en, et, ei, emme, ette, eivat* (conjugated for person)
- Estonian has **pole** as a fused negative copula (*pole* = ei ole). Finnish always uses *ei ole*.

### Quotative Mood
- Estonian has a **quotative mood** (*kaudne koneviis*) marked by *-vat*, used for hearsay/reported information. Finnish lacks this mood.

### Vocabulary Differences
Despite shared ancestry, many common words differ:
| English | Estonian | Finnish |
|---|---|---|
| thank you | *aitah* | *kiitos* |
| dog | *koer* | *koira* |
| bread | *leib* | *leipä* |
| water | *vesi* | *vesi* |
| house | *maja* | *talo* |
| child | *laps* | *lapsi* |
| book | *raamat* | *kirja* |
| big | *suur* | *suuri* |
| good | *hea* | *hyva* |
| month | *kuu* | *kuukausi* |

### Consonant Gradation
Both languages exhibit consonant gradation, but the patterns differ. Estonian gradation is less regular and affects different consonant clusters than Finnish.

### Diagnostic Quick Tests
1. Does the text contain **o** (o with tilde)? -> Estonian
2. Does the text violate vowel harmony freely? -> Estonian
3. Is the negative verb invariable (*ei* for all persons)? -> Estonian
4. Does *-vat* appear as a verb ending for reported speech? -> Estonian
5. Are the function words *aga* (but), *ka* (also), *pole* (is not) present? -> Estonian rather than Finnish (*mutta*, *myos*, *ei ole* in Finnish)

## Estonian vs. Votic

Votic (*vadja keel*) is the closest relative of Estonian, historically spoken in Ingria (now part of Russia near St. Petersburg). Votic is nearly extinct, with only a handful of elderly speakers remaining. Key differences:

- Votic preserves certain archaic Finnic features lost in Estonian, including more regular vowel harmony.
- Votic has its own distinct consonant gradation patterns.
- Votic vocabulary retains words that Estonian has replaced with Germanic loans.
- Votic uses Cyrillic script in some modern documentation, whereas Estonian exclusively uses Latin script.
- The function word inventory differs: Votic uses different conjunctions and particles.
- In practice, Votic is so rare that confusion with Estonian in real-world text identification scenarios is extremely unlikely.

## Estonian vs. Livonian

Livonian (*liivõ keel*) is a critically endangered Finnic language historically spoken in Latvia. It has some structural similarities to Estonian but also significant differences:

- Livonian has its own set of diacritical marks, including letters with macrons and with broken accent marks (e.g., *a*, *o* with various diacritics) that differ from Estonian's diacritics.
- Livonian preserves features of vowel harmony that Estonian has lost.
- Livonian has been heavily influenced by Latvian, whereas Estonian shows more Germanic influence.
- The word stock, while sharing Finnic roots, has diverged considerably.
- Livonian texts are extremely rare, making confusion with Estonian unlikely in practice.

## Estonian vs. Karelian

Karelian (*karjalan kieli*) is a Finnic language spoken in the Republic of Karelia (Russia) and parts of Finland. Key differences from Estonian:

- Karelian is more closely related to Finnish than to Estonian and generally resembles Finnish more in its grammar and vocabulary.
- Karelian maintains vowel harmony, which Estonian has lost.
- Karelian uses different case endings and has different morphophonological alternation patterns.
- Karelian texts may use Cyrillic or Latin script depending on the variety and era.
- Karelian function words (conjunctions, particles) differ from Estonian ones.
- The vocabulary overlap between Karelian and Estonian is lower than between Finnish and Estonian.

## Estonian vs. Hungarian

Hungarian (*magyar*) is a distantly related Uralic language. Despite shared family membership, the languages are not mutually intelligible and differ profoundly:

- Hungarian has vowel harmony; Estonian does not.
- Hungarian has a definite/indefinite verb conjugation distinction; Estonian has no articles and no such verbal distinction.
- Hungarian uses different diacritics (*a*, *e*, *i*, *o*, *o*, *u*, *u*) than Estonian. The key diagnostic difference: Hungarian uses double acute accent (*o*, *u*) which Estonian never uses; Estonian uses tilde (*o*) which Hungarian never uses.
- The vocabulary overlap is minimal, limited to a small number of reconstructed proto-Uralic roots.
- Hungarian word order, while also relatively free, has different structural constraints.
- Function words are entirely different: Hungarian *es* (and), *nem* (not), *de* (but) vs. Estonian *ja*, *ei*, *aga*.
