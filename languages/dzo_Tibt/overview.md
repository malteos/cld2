# Dzongkha (dzo)

## General Information

- **Language name:** Dzongkha
- **ISO 639-3 code:** dzo
- **ISO 639-1 code:** dz
- **Script:** Tibetan script (Uchen)
- **Language family:** Sino-Tibetan > Tibeto-Burman > Bodic > Southern Tibetic
- **Native speakers:** approximately 171,000
- **Total speakers:** approximately 640,000
- **Official status:** National and official language of Bhutan

## Geographic Distribution

Dzongkha is spoken primarily in the western region of Bhutan, particularly in the districts (dzongkhags) of Thimphu, Punakha, Paro, Wangdue Phodrang, Gasa, and Ha. As the national language, it also functions as a lingua franca across the entire country, where numerous other Tibeto-Burman languages are spoken. Small diaspora communities of Dzongkha speakers can be found in Nepal, India (particularly in Sikkim and West Bengal), and other countries with Bhutanese expatriate populations.

## Classification and Linguistic Features

Dzongkha belongs to the Southern Tibetic branch of the Tibeto-Burman language family, which itself is part of the broader Sino-Tibetan phylum. It is closely related to Sikkimese (Dranjoke) and the Tibetan varieties spoken in the border regions between Bhutan and Tibet. While Dzongkha shares a common ancestor with Standard Tibetan (Central Tibetan/Lhasa Tibetan), the two languages diverged significantly over the centuries and are not mutually intelligible in their spoken forms.

Dzongkha is a tonal language with a relatively complex system of consonant clusters in syllable onsets, many of which have been simplified in modern pronunciation compared to Classical Tibetan orthography. It employs a subject-object-verb (SOV) word order, uses postpositions rather than prepositions, and relies heavily on agglutinative morphology with suffixes for marking tense, aspect, mood, and evidentiality. Honorific registers play a significant role in everyday speech, reflecting the hierarchical social structures of Bhutanese society.

## Script

Dzongkha is written in the Tibetan script, specifically the Uchen (dbu can) form, which is the block-letter style used for printed text and formal writing. The cursive form, Umey (dbu med), is also used for informal handwriting. The Tibetan script is an abugida derived from the Brahmi script family, introduced to Tibet in the seventh century during the reign of Songtsen Gampo. Each consonant letter carries an inherent vowel /a/, which can be modified with diacritical marks to represent other vowels.

The script is written from left to right. A distinctive feature of Tibetan orthography is the use of stacked consonant clusters, where multiple consonant letters are arranged vertically to represent complex onsets. Because the orthography largely preserves Classical Tibetan spellings, there is a significant gap between written and spoken forms of Dzongkha, making literacy acquisition a considerable challenge.

## History and Development

Dzongkha traces its lineage to Classical Tibetan, the literary language codified during the Tibetan Empire period (seventh to ninth centuries). As Tibetic-speaking populations settled in the valleys of western Bhutan, their speech gradually diverged from the varieties spoken on the Tibetan Plateau. Geographic isolation created by the Himalayan terrain accelerated this divergence, and by the medieval period, Dzongkha had developed into a clearly distinct language.

The name "Dzongkha" literally means "the language of the dzong," referring to the fortress-monasteries that served as administrative and religious centers in Bhutan. The language gained political prominence in the seventeenth century when Zhabdrung Ngawang Namgyal unified Bhutan and established Dzongkha as the language of governance and religion. However, for much of Bhutan's history, Classical Tibetan (Chokey) remained the dominant literary language, with Dzongkha functioning primarily as a spoken vernacular.

The formal promotion of Dzongkha as a national language began in the 1960s under the modernization policies of Bhutan's third king, Jigme Dorji Wangchuck. The Dzongkha Development Commission (DDC), established in 1986, has played a central role in standardizing the language, developing modern terminology, and promoting its use in education, media, and government. Despite these efforts, English remains widely used in education and business, and Dzongkha coexists with numerous other indigenous languages within Bhutan's borders.

## Relevance to Language Identification

Dzongkha presents specific challenges for automatic language identification. Its use of the Tibetan script places it within a small group of languages sharing this writing system, with Standard Tibetan (bod) being the primary source of confusion. Distinguishing Dzongkha from Standard Tibetan in written text requires attention to vocabulary differences, certain spelling conventions, and characteristic grammatical particles. The relatively small volume of Dzongkha text available on the internet compared to Standard Tibetan can also affect the reliability of statistical language identification models.
