# Luxembourgish (Letzebuergesch) - Language Overview

## Classification

- **ISO 639-3**: ltz
- **Language family**: Indo-European > Germanic > West Germanic > High German > Central German > West Central German > Moselle Franconian
- **Script**: Latin (Latn)
- **Glottocode**: luxe1241

## Speaker Population

Luxembourgish has approximately 400,000 native speakers, the vast majority residing in the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg. Additional speakers are found in the border regions of Germany (particularly in the Eifel and along the Moselle), in the Belgian province of Luxembourg (Arelerland), and in small communities in France (Lorraine). A significant Luxembourgish-speaking diaspora also exists, notably in the United States, Brazil, and other European countries.

## Official Status

Luxembourgish is the national language and one of three official languages of the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg, alongside French and German. It achieved formal recognition through the Language Law (Loi sur le regime des langues) of 24 February 1984, which designated it as the national language (Nationalsproch) and the language of the legislature when not using French or German. Before this legislation, Luxembourgish was considered a dialect of German by many linguists and had no formal legal standing.

In practice, Luxembourg operates as a trilingual society. Luxembourgish serves as the primary spoken language of everyday life, French dominates in written administration, legal texts, and legislation, while German is commonly used in media, education, and certain administrative contexts. Luxembourgish is the language of parliament debates and is increasingly used in written form, especially in digital communication and social media.

## Historical Development

Luxembourgish belongs to the Moselle Franconian group of Central German dialects. Historically, it developed from the Old High German dialects spoken in the region between the Moselle and Rhine rivers. During the medieval period, the language evolved alongside other Franconian dialects, but Luxembourg's distinct political history as an independent entity (county, then duchy) allowed the language to develop separately.

The language gained significant symbolic importance during and after World War II, when it became a marker of national identity in resistance to German occupation. The Nazi regime attempted to suppress Luxembourgish identity and impose Standard German, which backfired and strengthened the population's attachment to their language. After liberation, Luxembourgish became even more strongly associated with national identity.

## Linguistic Features

Luxembourgish is closely related to the German dialects of the Moselle Franconian area, including the dialects of Trier and the Eifel region. However, centuries of contact with French have resulted in extensive lexical borrowing, particularly in domains such as politics, cuisine, law, and modern daily life. Common French loanwords include Trottoir (sidewalk), Camion (truck), and Merci (thank you).

The language has undergone significant standardization efforts since the mid-20th century. An official orthography was established in 1975 and revised in 1999. The Luxembourgish dictionary (Letzebuerger Online Dictionnaire, LOD) continues to expand as the language adapts to modern usage. Despite standardization, regional variation persists, with noticeable differences between northern, central, and southern dialects.

## Current Vitality and Usage

Luxembourgish is considered a vital language despite its relatively small speaker base. It is used extensively in daily conversation, in politics, in media (including radio and television programs), and increasingly in written digital communication. The language faces pressure from French and Portuguese due to Luxembourg's large immigrant population, but government policies actively promote its use, including mandatory Luxembourgish language classes for new residents seeking citizenship.
