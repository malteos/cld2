# Cebuano Characteristics for Language Identification

## Character Patterns

Cebuano uses the **standard Latin alphabet** without diacritics in typical modern writing. The alphabet consists of the 26 standard Latin letters plus the digraph "ng" which represents a single phoneme /ŋ/. Unlike some Philippine languages that have adopted the tilde (as in Spanish "ñ"), Cebuano writing in practice rarely uses diacritical marks. The glottal stop, though phonemically significant, is generally not represented orthographically.

The absence of diacritics distinguishes Cebuano text from many European languages (French, German, Spanish, Portuguese, Vietnamese) at a glance. However, it shares this characteristic with English, Malay, and other Philippine languages written in Latin script, so diacritic absence alone is not diagnostic.

## Character Frequency Patterns

Cebuano text exhibits distinctive character frequency distributions:

- **Highest frequency:** a, n, g, i, o, s, k, u, l, a
- **The digraph "ng"** is extremely frequent, far more so than in most languages using Latin script. It occurs as a standalone word (the ligature marker), as part of the ubiquitous particle "ang," in the common conjunction "nga," and in numerous native roots. The raw frequency of the letter sequence n-g in Cebuano text is a strong identifying signal.
- **Letter "k"** is much more frequent in Cebuano than in Spanish or English, as it replaces the Spanish "c" and "qu" in native orthography.
- **Letters "c," "f," "j," "q," "v," "x," "z"** are rare, appearing mainly in loanwords and proper nouns. Their low frequency is characteristic of Philippine languages.
- **Letter "w"** appears with moderate frequency in native vocabulary (e.g., "wala," "walay").
- **Letter "y"** is moderately frequent, used in pronouns ("siya," "niya") and native words.

## Morphological Patterns for LID

Cebuano verbal morphology provides highly distinctive patterns for language identification. The following affixes and their combinations are characteristic:

### Prefixes
- **mag-** / **nag-** / **maga-** / **naga-** - actor focus verb forms
- **mo-** / **mu-** / **mi-** / **ni-** - actor focus (mu- class)
- **gi-** / **gina-** - completed/incompleted object focus marker
- **i-** / **ig-** - instrumental/benefactive focus
- **pag-** / **pagka-** - verbal noun/infinitive
- **ka-** - stative, abstract noun former
- **maka-** / **naka-** - potential actor focus
- **ma-** / **na-** - potential object/stative
- **mang-** / **nang-** - distributive actor focus
- **tig-** / **mag-** - occupational/habitual marker
- **tag-** - owner/possessor prefix
- **manag-** / **managhig-** - reciprocal
- **mag-ka-** - reciprocal/stative

### Suffixes
- **-on** - object focus (contemplated)
- **-an** - locative focus; also adjective intensifier
- **-ay** - reciprocal/existential

### Infixes
- **-in-** - completed aspect (object focus)
- **-um-** - actor focus (archaic/formal, limited productivity)

### Common Affix Combinations
- **gi- ... -an** - completed locative focus
- **pag- ... -on** - nominal form of object focus
- **pag- ... -an** - nominal form of locative focus
- **pagka-** - abstract noun; manner adverb
- **pagpang-** - nominal form of distributive
- **ika-** - ordinal number prefix
- **mahi-** / **nahi-** - involuntary/accidental
- **magpa-** / **nagpa-** / **mopa-** / **mipa-** / **gipa-** - causative forms

## Cebuano-Specific Markers

Several features make Cebuano text highly identifiable:

1. **The particle "og/ug"** as conjunction and case marker is distinctive to Cebuano among major Philippine languages (Tagalog uses "at" and "ng").
2. **Discourse particles** such as "gyud/jud," "bitaw," "diay," "man," and "lagi" are highly characteristic.
3. **The negator "dili"** contrasts with Tagalog "hindi," Hiligaynon "indi," and Waray "diri."
4. **Existential "aduna/naa"** contrasts with Tagalog "mayroon/may" and Hiligaynon "may."
5. **The pronoun system** with forms like "nako," "nimo," "niya," "namo," "nato," "ninyo," "nila" is distinctive.
6. **Common everyday words** that differ from Tagalog: "balay" (house, vs. Tagalog "bahay"), "dagat" (sea, shared), "kaon" (eat, vs. Tagalog "kain"), "tubig" (water, shared), "dako" (big, vs. Tagalog "malaki").
7. **The high frequency of "ng" as a digraph** appearing in function words, affixes, and roots gives Cebuano text a visually distinctive character.
8. **Word-final vowels and consonants:** Cebuano words commonly end in vowels or in consonants /n/, /g/, /ng/, /s/, /d/, /t/, /k/, contrasting with the phonotactic patterns of non-Philippine languages.
