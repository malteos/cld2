# Cebuano Grammar

## Word Order

Cebuano has a basic **VSO (Verb-Subject-Object)** or **VOS (Verb-Object-Subject)** word order, although it exhibits considerable flexibility. The predicate-initial structure is the unmarked, most natural order in Cebuano:

- **Mokaon ang bata og mangga.** (Will-eat ANG child OG mango.) "The child will eat a mango."
- **Nindot ang balay.** (Beautiful ANG house.) "The house is beautiful."

However, topicalization and pragmatic considerations frequently produce alternative orders. A constituent can be fronted for emphasis or topic-marking, yielding SVO-like or other surface orders. The language relies heavily on case-marking particles rather than rigid word order to indicate grammatical roles, which is why the ordering remains flexible without ambiguity.

## Morphological Type

Cebuano is an **agglutinative** language with a particularly rich system of verbal morphology. Verbs are constructed by combining roots with a variety of prefixes, infixes, and suffixes that indicate voice/focus, aspect, mood, and other semantic information. Nouns and adjectives also undergo affixation, though less extensively than verbs. The morphological system is a hallmark of Philippine-type languages and is central to understanding how Cebuano encodes meaning.

## Phonology

### Vowels

Cebuano has a relatively simple vowel system. The core vowel inventory contains three to five vowels depending on analysis:

| Vowel | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| /a/ | Open central |
| /i/ | Close front |
| /u/ | Close back |
| /e/ | Mid front (often in free variation with /i/) |
| /o/ | Mid back (often in free variation with /u/) |

In traditional Cebuano phonology, there are three primary vowel phonemes: /a/, /i/, and /u/. The mid vowels /e/ and /o/ are historically allophones of /i/ and /u/ respectively, occurring in certain phonological environments. However, due to extensive borrowing from Spanish and English, /e/ and /o/ have become increasingly phonemic in modern Cebuano, particularly in loanwords.

### Consonants

Cebuano has approximately 16 consonant phonemes:

| Manner | Labial | Dental/Alveolar | Palatal | Velar | Glottal |
|--------|--------|-----------------|---------|-------|---------|
| Stops | p, b | t, d | | k, g | ʔ |
| Nasals | m | n | | ng /ŋ/ | |
| Fricatives | | s | | | h |
| Laterals | | l | | | |
| Rhotics | | r | | | |
| Glides | w | | y /j/ | | |

The glottal stop /ʔ/ is a significant phoneme that can occur in word-initial, medial, and final positions, though it is not typically represented in standard orthography. The digraph "ng" represents the single velar nasal phoneme /ŋ/, which is extremely frequent in Cebuano.

### Suprasegmental Features

Cebuano is a **non-tonal** language. Stress is phonemic and generally falls on the penultimate or ultimate syllable. Stress placement can distinguish otherwise identical words. The most common syllable structure is CV (consonant-vowel), though CVC, V, and consonant clusters also occur, particularly in borrowed words.

## Noun System

### Gender and Number

Cebuano nouns have **no grammatical gender**. There is no inflection for masculine, feminine, or neuter. Natural gender is expressed lexically when needed (e.g., "lalaki" for male, "babaye" for female).

Plurality is not obligatorily marked on nouns. When it is expressed, the pluralizer **"mga"** (pronounced /maŋa/) precedes the noun:
- **ang balay** "the house"
- **ang mga balay** "the houses"

### Case Particles

Cebuano uses a system of **case-marking particles** to indicate the grammatical role of noun phrases. There is no morphological case inflection on the nouns themselves. The three main sets are:

| Role | Common Nouns | Personal Names (Singular) | Personal Names (Plural) |
|------|-------------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| Nominative (topic/ang-phrase) | **ang** | **si** | **sila si** |
| Genitive (possessor/agent) | **sa** / **og** | **ni** | **nila ni** |
| Oblique (location/direction) | **sa** | **kang** | **kanila ni** |

The **ang**-marked phrase is the topic or focus of the sentence, corresponding to the grammatical role highlighted by the verb's voice morphology. The **og/sa**-marked phrases indicate non-topic arguments, possessors, or locations.

### Demonstratives

Cebuano has a three-way demonstrative system based on proximity:
- **kini** (this, near speaker)
- **kana** (that, near listener)
- **kadto** (that, far from both)

### Personal Pronouns

| Person | Nominative | Genitive | Oblique |
|--------|-----------|----------|---------|
| 1sg | ako | nako/ko | kanako/nako |
| 2sg | ikaw/ka | nimo/mo | kanimo/nimo |
| 3sg | siya | niya | kaniya/niya |
| 1pl (excl) | kami/mi | namo | kanamo/namo |
| 1pl (incl) | kita/ta | nato | kanato/nato |
| 2pl | kamo/mo | ninyo | kaninyo/ninyo |
| 3pl | sila | nila | kanila/nila |

Cebuano distinguishes between **inclusive** and **exclusive** first person plural, a feature shared across Philippine and many other Austronesian languages.

## Verb System

The Cebuano verb system is the most morphologically complex part of the grammar. It encodes two primary dimensions: **voice/focus** and **aspect**.

### Voice/Focus System

The voice (or focus) system is the defining grammatical feature of Cebuano and other Philippine-type languages. The voice morphology on the verb indicates which semantic role (agent, patient, location, instrument, beneficiary) is promoted to the topic position (the ang-marked noun phrase). This is not equivalent to active/passive voice in European languages but represents a symmetrical system where multiple arguments can be the syntactic topic.

#### Actor Focus (AF)

The ang-marked noun phrase is the agent/actor:
- **Prefixes:** mag-, mo-/mu-, mi-, mang-
- **Mokaon ang bata og mangga.** "The child will eat a mango." (child = ang, topic, actor)

#### Object/Patient Focus (OF)

The ang-marked noun phrase is the patient/object:
- **Suffix:** -on
- **Kan-on sa bata ang mangga.** "The child will eat the mango." (mango = ang, topic, patient)

#### Locative Focus (LF)

The ang-marked noun phrase is the location or direction:
- **Suffix:** -an
- **Kan-an sa bata ang lamesa.** "The child will eat at the table." (table = ang, topic, location)

#### Instrumental/Benefactive Focus (IF)

The ang-marked noun phrase is the instrument or beneficiary:
- **Prefix:** i-
- **Ikan-on sa bata ang kutsara.** "The child will eat with the spoon." (spoon = ang, topic, instrument)

### Aspect System

Cebuano verbs are marked for three aspects (not tenses):

| Aspect | Description | Formation Example (mag- verbs) |
|--------|-------------|-------------------------------|
| **Contemplated** (future) | Action not yet begun | mag- + root: **magkaon** |
| **Incompleted** (progressive/habitual) | Action ongoing or habitual | nag- + root: **nagkaon** |
| **Completed** (perfective) | Action finished | nag- + root (same form, context-dependent) or ni- + root |

For **-um-** verbs:
- Contemplated: mu-/mo- + root: **mokaon**
- Incompleted: reduplication of first syllable: **nagakaon**
- Completed: infix -in- or prefix mi-/ni-: **mikaon**

For **-on** (object focus) verbs:
- Contemplated: root + -on: **kaonon**
- Incompleted: gi- + root (+ reduplication): **gikaon** / **ginakaon**
- Completed: gi- + root: **gikaon**

**Reduplication** of the first CV syllable of the root is commonly used to form incompleted aspect, yielding forms like **nagakakaon** (is eating, repeated action) from **kaon** (eat).

### Imperative and Potential Moods

Imperatives are formed with specific affixes or bare stems:
- **Kaon!** "Eat!"
- **Pagkaon!** "Eat!" (more polite/formal)
- **Ayaw pagkaon.** "Don't eat."

The potential mood uses the prefix **maka-** (AF) or **ma-** (OF):
- **Makakaon ko.** "I can eat."
- **Makaon nako.** "I can eat it."

## Syntax Features

### Ligature (nga / -ng)

The ligature **nga** (or its enclitic form **-ng** after vowels and /n/) connects modifiers to the words they modify. It links adjectives to nouns, relative clauses to head nouns, and functions in various other connective roles:

- **dako nga balay** "big house"
- **taas nga kahoy** "tall tree"
- **batang gamay** (bata + -ng gamay) "small child"
- **ang tawo nga miabut** "the person who arrived"

### Existential and Locative Constructions

Cebuano uses several existential markers:
- **aduna / naa** "there is/are" (existential positive)
- **wala** "there is/are not" (existential negative, also general negator for completed actions)
- **dili** "not" (negator for contemplated/future actions and adjectives)

Examples:
- **Adunay tubig sa baso.** "There is water in the glass."
- **Wala siyay kwarta.** "He/she has no money."
- **Dili siya mokaon.** "He/she will not eat."

### Relative Clauses

Relative clauses follow the head noun and are introduced by the ligature **nga**:
- **Ang babaye nga nagbaligya og isda** "The woman who sells fish"

### Questions

Yes/no questions are formed by the particle **ba** placed after the first content word or phrase:
- **Mokaon ba ka?** "Will you eat?"

Content questions use interrogative words such as:
- **kinsa** "who"
- **unsa** "what"
- **asa / diin** "where"
- **kanus-a** "when"
- **ngano** "why"
- **pila** "how many/much"
- **giunsa** "how"

### Serial Verb Constructions and Clause Chaining

Cebuano frequently chains clauses using connectors like **ug** (and), **pero/apan** (but), **tungod kay** (because), and **kung** (if). Serial verb constructions are common for expressing sequences of actions or motion events.
