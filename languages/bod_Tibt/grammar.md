# Tibetan Grammar (bod_Tibt)

## Word Order

Tibetan is a consistently head-final language with **SOV (Subject-Object-Verb)** word order. The verb always appears at the end of the clause, modifiers precede their heads, and postpositions are used rather than prepositions. Relative clauses precede the noun they modify, and subordinate clauses precede the main clause. This typological profile is consistent across all Tibetan dialects and is shared with other Tibeto-Burman languages.

Basic word order example:
- ང་ས་དེབ་བཀླགས་པ་ཡིན། (nga-s deb bklags-pa yin) "I read a/the book."
  - ང་ས་ (nga-s) = I + ergative marker
  - དེབ་ (deb) = book
  - བཀླགས་པ་ཡིན་ (bklags-pa yin) = read (past, egophoric)

## Morphological Type

Tibetan is primarily **agglutinative**, with grammatical relationships expressed through particles and suffixes that attach to or follow stems. Unlike highly inflecting languages, Tibetan words do not undergo extensive internal modification. Instead, grammatical categories such as case, tense, aspect, mood, and evidentiality are marked by discrete morphemes, typically postposed particles. Some degree of fusional morphology exists in the verb system, particularly in the alternation of verb stems for tense.

## Phonology

### Consonants

Classical Tibetan had a rich consonant inventory that is still reflected in the orthography. Modern spoken Tibetan (particularly the Lhasa dialect) has undergone significant phonological changes, resulting in a substantial gap between spelling and pronunciation.

Key consonant features:
- **Aspiration contrast:** Tibetan distinguishes aspirated and unaspirated stops and affricates at multiple places of articulation (e.g., ཀ /ka/ vs. ཁ /kha/, ཅ /tɕa/ vs. ཆ /tɕha/).
- **Consonant clusters:** Written Tibetan preserves complex onset clusters (e.g., བསྒྲུབས་ bsgrubs), though many of these clusters have been simplified in modern pronunciation. Superscribed, subscribed, and prefixed consonant letters represent these historical clusters.
- **Prenasalized stops:** Some dialects retain prenasalized consonants.
- **Retroflex consonants:** Present in the inventory, particularly in loanwords from Sanskrit.

### Vowels

The basic vowel system includes /a, i, u, e, o/. The inherent vowel of each consonant letter is /a/; other vowels are indicated by diacritical marks (གི་གུ for /i/, ཞབས་ཀྱུ for /u/, འགྲེང་བུ for /e/, ན་རོ for /o/). In Lhasa Tibetan, the vowel inventory has expanded through umlauting processes to include front rounded vowels /y, ø/.

### Tone

Tonal distinctions are present in some dialects, most notably **Lhasa Tibetan**, which has a two-tone system (high and low). The tones developed historically from earlier consonant distinctions: voiced initial consonants generally gave rise to low tone, while voiceless initials produced high tone. Importantly, not all Tibetan dialects are tonal; **Amdo Tibetan**, for example, is non-tonal and instead preserves voicing distinctions in initial consonants.

### Syllable Structure

The maximal syllable structure in Written Tibetan is (C)(C)(C)V(C)(C), though modern pronunciation has reduced this considerably. Each syllable is delimited in writing by the tsheg (་), a crucial feature for text processing.

## Noun System

### Case Marking

Tibetan uses postposed **case particles** to mark grammatical relations. The form of these particles often varies depending on the final consonant of the preceding syllable (a phenomenon known as "case particle allomorphy"). The major cases include:

- **Absolutive (unmarked):** The citation form of the noun, used for intransitive subjects and direct objects. No overt marker.
- **Ergative/Instrumental:** -གིས་ / -གྱིས་ / -ཀྱིས་ / -ས་ / -ཡིས་. Marks the agent of transitive verbs and instruments. The choice among allomorphs depends on the final sound of the preceding word.
- **Genitive:** -གི་ / -གྱི་ / -ཀྱི་ / -ཡི་ / -འི་. Marks possession and various attributive relationships.
- **Dative/Locative:** -ལ་ / -ར་ / -སུ་ / -ཏུ་ / -དུ་. Marks indirect objects, goals, locations, and various other functions.
- **Ablative:** -ནས་ / -ལས་. Marks the source or origin of an action.
- **Comitative/Associative:** -དང་. Marks accompaniment ("with") and is also used as a conjunction ("and").
- **Terminative:** -བར་དུ་ / -ཡན་ཆད་. Marks extent or limit ("up to," "until").

### Pluralization

Pluralization in Tibetan is optional and context-dependent. When explicitly marked, it is indicated by plural particles such as -ཚོ་ (tsho) or -རྣམས་ (rnams). The particle -རྣམས་ is more formal and literary, while -ཚོ་ is used in colloquial speech, particularly with human referents.

### Honorific System

Tibetan has an elaborate **honorific system** (ཞེ་ས་, zhesa) that pervades the lexicon. Many common nouns, verbs, and even some particles have distinct ordinary, honorific, and super-honorific forms. For example:
- "head": ordinary ཚོགས་པ (mgo), honorific དབུ (dbu)
- "to eat": ordinary ཟ (za), honorific མཆོད (mchod), super-honorific གསོལ (gsol)

## Verb System

### Stem Alternation

Classical Tibetan verbs can have up to four stems: **present, past, future, and imperative**. The alternation among stems involves changes in the initial consonant, prefix, or suffix. For example:
- འབྲི (vbri, "write," present), བྲིས (bris, past), འབྲི (vbri, future), བྲིས (bris, imperative)

In modern spoken Tibetan, the stem alternation system has been significantly simplified, with many verbs using the same form across tenses, and tense being marked instead by auxiliary verbs and particles.

### Tense and Aspect

Tense and aspect are marked through a combination of verb stem alternation, auxiliary verbs, and sentence-final particles. Key distinctions include:
- **Past:** Marked by past stem + auxiliary (e.g., བྱས་པ་རེད། byas-pa red, "did")
- **Present/Habitual:** Marked by present stem + auxiliary (e.g., བྱེད་ཀྱི་འདུག byed-kyi vdug, "is doing")
- **Future/Intentional:** Marked by future stem + auxiliary (e.g., བྱ་རྒྱུ་ཡིན། bya-rgyu yin, "will do")
- **Perfective vs. Imperfective:** Distinguished through different auxiliary constructions.

### Evidentiality

One of the most distinctive features of Tibetan grammar is its system of **evidentiality** — grammaticalized marking of the speaker's source of information. Tibetan sentence-final auxiliaries encode whether the speaker:
- Has **direct personal knowledge** (egophoric): ཡིན (yin), བྱིན (byin)
- Has **direct sensory evidence** (sensory evidential): འདུག (vdug), སོང (song)
- Is reporting **hearsay or inference** (reportative/inferential): རེད (red), བྱུང (byung)

For example:
- ཁོང་བོད་པ་**ཡིན**། (khong bod-pa **yin**) "He is Tibetan" (I know this personally)
- ཁོང་བོད་པ་**རེད**། (khong bod-pa **red**) "He is Tibetan" (I've been told / it's common knowledge)

This evidential system is not optional; speakers must choose an appropriate evidential marker in virtually every declarative sentence.

### Auxiliary Verbs

Tibetan makes extensive use of auxiliary verbs (often called "copulas" or "sentence-final particles") that combine information about tense, evidentiality, and polarity. The main auxiliary verbs include:
- ཡིན (yin) — egophoric existential/copula
- རེད (red) — factual/reportative copula
- འདུག (vdug) — sensory evidential existential
- ཡོད (yod) — egophoric existential ("have/exist")
- སྣང (snang) — sensory evidential for internal states
- མིན (min), མ་རེད (ma red), མེད (med) — negative counterparts

### Negation

Negation is expressed through negative prefixes on the auxiliary verb or through negative auxiliary forms:
- མ (ma) — negation of past tense
- མི (mi) — negation of present/future tense

## Syntax Features

### Topic-Comment Structure

Tibetan frequently employs **topic-comment** constructions, where the topic is marked by the particle -ནི་ (ni). The topic particle sets the frame for the comment that follows:
- དེབ་**ནི**་ང་ས་བཀླགས་པ་ཡིན། (deb **ni** nga-s bklags-pa yin) "As for the book, I read it."

### Nominalization

**Nominalization** is pervasive in Tibetan grammar. Verbs and clauses are frequently nominalized using particles such as -པ་/-བ (pa/ba), -མཁན (mkhan), and -ས (sa). Nominalization serves many functions:
- Creating relative clauses: ང་ས་བཀླགས་པའི་དེབ། (nga-s bklags-pa'i deb) "the book that I read"
- Forming complement clauses
- Creating agent nouns: བྲིས་མཁན (bris-mkhan) "writer" (one who writes)

### Clause-Final Verbs

As a strictly verb-final language, Tibetan places the main verb and its associated auxiliaries at the absolute end of the clause. Embedded clauses are also verb-final and are typically marked by nominalizers or conjunctive particles before the main clause verb.

### Conjunctive Particles

Clauses are linked by conjunctive particles that appear at the end of the subordinate clause:
- -ནས (nas) — "after; from" (sequential)
- -ཏེ/-སྟེ/-དེ (te/ste/de) — "and then; because"
- -ན (na) — "if; when" (conditional)
- -ཡང/-ཀྱང (yang/kyang) — "although" (concessive)
- -དུས (dus) — "when" (temporal)

### Question Formation

Questions are formed by replacing the sentence-final auxiliary with an interrogative particle or by adding an interrogative word:
- Yes/no questions: Replace final auxiliary with interrogative form (e.g., རེད་པས། red-pas? "Is it?")
- Content questions: Use interrogative words like གང (gang "what/which"), སུ (su "who"), ག་པར (ga-par "where"), ག་དུས (ga-dus "when") in situ.

### Clause Chaining

Complex sentences in Tibetan are typically formed through **clause chaining**, where a series of subordinate clauses (each ending in a conjunctive particle) build up to a single main clause with the final verb. This results in long, multi-clause sentences that unfold linearly before reaching the main predication.
