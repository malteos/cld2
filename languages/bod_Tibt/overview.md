# Tibetan (bod_Tibt)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Tibetan (བོད་སྐད་, Bodskad)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** bod
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** bo
- **Script:** Tibetan script (Uchen / དབུ་ཅན་)
- **Language Family:** Sino-Tibetan > Tibeto-Burman > Bodic > Tibetic
- **Approximate Speakers:** ~6 million (including all dialects)
- **Writing Direction:** Left-to-right, top-to-bottom

## Geographic Distribution

Tibetan is spoken across a vast geographic area spanning several countries and regions in Central and South Asia. The primary regions include:

- **Tibet Autonomous Region (TAR):** The historical heartland of the Tibetan language, now an autonomous region within the People's Republic of China. Lhasa, the capital, is the center of the prestige dialect.
- **Chinese provinces:** Significant Tibetan-speaking populations exist in Qinghai, Gansu, Sichuan, and Yunnan provinces, in areas historically known as Amdo and Kham.
- **Nepal:** Northern districts bordering Tibet have Tibetan-speaking communities, and Kathmandu hosts a substantial Tibetan diaspora population.
- **Bhutan:** While Dzongkha (the national language) is a distinct Tibetic language, Classical Tibetan has historically served as a literary and religious language in Bhutan.
- **India:** Tibetan is spoken in Ladakh (Jammu & Kashmir), Sikkim, Himachal Pradesh (particularly Dharamsala, the seat of the Tibetan government-in-exile), Arunachal Pradesh, and in Tibetan refugee settlements across the country.
- **Diaspora communities:** Tibetan-speaking communities exist worldwide, with notable populations in the United States, Canada, Switzerland, and other countries.

## Official Status

Tibetan is an official language of the Tibet Autonomous Region of China, where it holds co-official status alongside Mandarin Chinese. It is used in government, education, media, and public signage, though the extent of its use in official domains has been a matter of ongoing debate. In India, Tibetan is recognized as a scheduled language in Sikkim. The Central Tibetan Administration (the Tibetan government-in-exile based in Dharamsala, India) uses Tibetan as its primary administrative language.

## History of the Language

The Tibetan language has a documented history spanning over 1,300 years. The Tibetic branch diverged from other Sino-Tibetan languages several millennia ago, though the exact timeline remains debated among historical linguists. Proto-Tibetic is reconstructed based on comparative evidence from modern Tibetic languages and Classical Tibetan texts.

The earliest attested form of Tibetan dates to the 7th century CE, during the reign of King Songtsen Gampo (སྲོང་བཙན་སྒམ་པོ) of the Tibetan Empire. According to tradition, the king sent his minister Thonmi Sambhota (ཐོན་མི་སམ་བྷོ་ཊ) to India to study writing systems. Thonmi Sambhota is credited with creating the Tibetan script, adapting it from an Indic script (likely a form of Brahmi or Gupta script) to suit the phonology of the Tibetan language. He is also credited with composing the first Tibetan grammar treatises.

The development of the Tibetan literary tradition is closely intertwined with the introduction and spread of Buddhism in Tibet. From the 8th century onward, massive translation projects rendered the Buddhist canon from Sanskrit into Tibetan, resulting in the Kangyur (བཀའ་འགྱུར་, the translated words of the Buddha) and Tengyur (བསྟན་འགྱུར་, translated treatises). These translation efforts not only preserved many Buddhist texts whose Sanskrit originals were subsequently lost but also profoundly shaped the Tibetan literary language, introducing extensive philosophical and technical vocabulary.

Classical Tibetan, the literary language that emerged from these early translation efforts, remained remarkably stable for centuries and served as a lingua franca across the Tibetan cultural sphere. It functioned as a scholarly language in Mongolia, Bhutan, Ladakh, and other regions influenced by Tibetan Buddhism.

## History of the Script

The Tibetan script (དབུ་ཅན་, Uchen, meaning "with a head") is an abugida (alphasyllabary) derived from Indic scripts. It consists of 30 consonant letters and 4 vowel diacritics. The script has several distinctive features, including the use of subjoined (stacked) consonant letters to represent consonant clusters, and the tsheg (་), a small dot used as a syllable delimiter.

Two main script styles exist: Uchen (དབུ་ཅན་, "with a head"), the formal printed style with a horizontal head stroke, and Umeh (དབུ་མེད་, "headless"), a cursive style used for handwriting and informal contexts. Several regional and calligraphic variants also exist.

The orthography of Written Tibetan was largely standardized during the early imperial period and has remained conservative, preserving many features of Old Tibetan pronunciation that are no longer reflected in modern spoken dialects. As a result, there is a significant gap between the written and spoken forms of the language, somewhat analogous to the relationship between English spelling and pronunciation.

## Major Dialects

Tibetan encompasses a wide range of dialects, often grouped into three major dialect groups:

- **U-Tsang (དབུས་གཙང་):** Central Tibetan, including the Lhasa dialect, which serves as the prestige standard.
- **Kham (ཁམས་):** Eastern Tibetan dialects spoken in Kham.
- **Amdo (ཨ་མདོ་):** Northeastern Tibetan dialects, notable for lacking the tonal distinctions found in Central Tibetan.

These dialect groups show significant variation in phonology, vocabulary, and grammar, and mutual intelligibility between distant dialects can be limited.
