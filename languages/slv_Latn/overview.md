# Slovenian (slv_Latn) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language name:** Slovenian (Slovenski jezik / Slovenscina)
- **ISO 639-3 code:** slv
- **ISO 639-1 code:** sl
- **Language family:** Indo-European > Balto-Slavic > Slavic > South Slavic > Western South Slavic
- **Script:** Latin (with diacritics)
- **Native speakers:** Approximately 2.5 million
- **Total speakers:** Approximately 2.5 million (limited L2 population)

## Geographic Distribution

Slovenian is the official language of the Republic of Slovenia, where it is spoken by the vast majority of the population. It also has official recognition and is spoken by minority communities in several neighboring countries:

- **Italy:** In the Friuli Venezia Giulia region, particularly around Trieste (Trst), Gorizia (Gorica), and in the Resia Valley. The Slovenian-speaking community in Italy numbers approximately 80,000-100,000 people.
- **Austria:** In the southern Austrian state of Carinthia (Koroska), particularly around Klagenfurt (Celovec) and in the Gail Valley and Rosental areas. Approximately 25,000-50,000 speakers remain.
- **Hungary:** In the Raba region (Porabje) near the border, a small community of several thousand speakers.
- **Croatia:** Some speakers in border areas of Istria and along the Croatian-Slovenian border.

Diaspora communities exist in Argentina, the United States, Canada, Germany, Australia, and other countries, established through waves of emigration in the 19th and 20th centuries.

## Historical Development

Slovenian has one of the longest documented histories among Slavic languages. The Freising Manuscripts (Brizinski spomeniki), dating from approximately 972-1039 CE, are the earliest known text in any Slavic language written in the Latin script. These religious texts demonstrate that a distinct Slovenian linguistic identity existed already in the early medieval period.

The development of standard Slovenian is closely associated with the Protestant Reformation. Primoz Trubar published the first printed book in Slovenian, "Catechismus" and "Abecedarium," in 1550. His contemporary Jurij Dalmatin translated the Bible into Slovenian in 1584, and Adam Bohoric published the first Slovenian grammar, "Arcticae horulae," in 1584 as well. These works established the foundations of literary Slovenian.

During the Counter-Reformation and subsequent centuries, Slovenian continued to develop as a literary language despite political pressures from German-speaking authorities. The Enlightenment period brought renewed interest in standardizing the language, with figures such as Jernej Kopitar playing a central role. Kopitar published a comprehensive grammar in 1808 and advocated for a standard based on the central Lower Carniolan dialect.

The 19th century saw the emergence of modern standard Slovenian, shaped by the literary works of France Preseren, whose poetry is considered the pinnacle of Slovenian Romantic literature. The Spring of Nations in 1848 brought political aspirations for a United Slovenia, and the language became a symbol of national identity.

## Standard Language and Dialects

Modern standard Slovenian is based primarily on the central dialects of Upper and Lower Carniola. Despite the small number of speakers, Slovenian is remarkably dialectally diverse, with approximately 46 distinct dialects grouped into 7 major dialect groups: Carinthian, Upper Carniolan, Lower Carniolan, Littoral, Rovte, Styrian, and Pannonian. Some dialects are mutually unintelligible, reflecting the mountainous terrain that historically isolated communities.

## Relationship to Other South Slavic Languages

Slovenian belongs to the Western South Slavic branch, along with the Bosnian-Croatian-Serbian (BCS) continuum. While Slovenian shares many features with these languages, it is clearly distinct and not mutually intelligible with them. Key distinguishing features include the preservation of the dual grammatical number, a different accentual system, and significant lexical and phonological differences. Slovenian has been influenced historically by German, Italian, and Hungarian due to prolonged contact with these languages.
