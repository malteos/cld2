# Assamese (asm_Beng) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Assamese (অসমীয়া, Oxomiya)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** asm
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** as
- **Script:** Bengali/Assamese script (Eastern Nagari variant)
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Indo-Iranian > Indo-Aryan > Eastern Indo-Aryan
- **Native Speakers:** Approximately 15 million
- **Total Speakers:** Approximately 23 million (including L2 speakers)
- **Classification:** Low-resource language for NLP purposes

## Geographic Distribution

Assamese is spoken primarily in the Indian state of **Assam**, where it serves as the official state language. It is also spoken in parts of neighboring states and regions:

- **Assam** (primary region, majority of speakers)
- **Arunachal Pradesh** (used as a lingua franca in parts of the state)
- **Nagaland** (spoken by communities in western districts)
- **Meghalaya** (spoken in border areas)
- **Parts of West Bengal** (particularly in districts bordering Assam)

Small Assamese-speaking diaspora communities exist in other Indian cities (Delhi, Bangalore, Mumbai) and internationally (United Kingdom, United States, Canada, Gulf states).

## Official Status

Assamese holds official language status in the state of Assam under the Indian constitution (Eighth Schedule). It is one of the 22 scheduled languages of India. Assamese is the medium of instruction in schools and universities in Assam, and it is used in government administration, courts, and media throughout the state.

## Historical Development

The history of Assamese language and literature stretches back over a millennium. The earliest known Assamese text is the **Charyapada** (8th-12th century), a collection of Buddhist mystical songs shared with Bengali and Odia literary traditions. These songs represent the earliest stage of Eastern Indo-Aryan languages separating from Magadhi Prakrit and Apabhramsha.

The **Early Assamese period** (14th-16th century) saw the composition of major literary works. Madhav Kandali's translation of the Ramayana (the *Saptakanda Ramayana*, 14th century) is one of the earliest Ramayana translations in any Indian language. The **Vaishnava movement** led by Srimanta Sankardev (15th-16th century) produced a vast body of devotional literature including the *Kirtan Ghosa* and *Dasham*, which standardized literary Assamese.

The **Ahom period** (13th-19th century) produced the **Buranjis**, historical chronicles written in Assamese prose, which are unique in Indian literary history as a tradition of secular historiography. These texts documented the political and social history of the Ahom kingdom and contributed significantly to the development of Assamese prose.

During **British colonial rule**, Assamese faced a significant challenge when Bengali was imposed as the official language and medium of education in Assam (1836-1873). The efforts of American Baptist missionaries, particularly **Nathan Brown** and **Miles Bronson**, who established the first Assamese printing press and published the journal *Orunodoi* (1846), were instrumental in preserving and reviving the language. The language was eventually restored as the official language and medium of instruction after sustained campaigns by Assamese intellectuals.

The **modern period** has seen the standardization of Assamese based on the dialect of the Brahmaputra Valley (particularly the Sibsagar dialect). Assamese literature has flourished with notable authors, poets, and intellectuals contributing to various genres including fiction, poetry, drama, and literary criticism.

## Script

Assamese uses the **Assamese script**, a variant of the Eastern Nagari script closely related to the Bengali script. The Assamese script has a few distinctive characters not found in Bengali, most notably **ৰ** (ra) and **ৱ** (wa/va). The script is an abugida, where consonant letters carry an inherent vowel sound /ɔ/ that can be modified with vowel diacritics (matras). The writing direction is left to right. The script includes 11 vowel letters, 40 consonant letters, and various conjunct consonant forms (juktakkhar).

## Sociolinguistic Context

Assamese serves as a lingua franca in much of northeastern India, bridging communication between speakers of Tibeto-Burman, Austroasiatic, and other language families. The language exists in a complex sociolinguistic environment with several recognized dialects including Kamrupi (western), Standard/Central Assamese, and Goalpariya (southwestern, sometimes classified separately). The language has borrowed vocabulary from Sanskrit, Persian, Arabic, English, and various Tibeto-Burman and Austroasiatic languages spoken in the region.
