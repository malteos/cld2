# Modern Standard Arabic (arb_Arab) - Grammar

## Word Order

Modern Standard Arabic exhibits flexible word order, though distinct patterns prevail depending on context. The canonical word order is **VSO (Verb-Subject-Object)**, which is the traditional and most characteristic order in formal Arabic:

- **VSO:** كتبَ الطالبُ الرسالةَ (kataba al-talibu al-risalata - "The student wrote the letter")

However, **SVO (Subject-Verb-Object)** order is increasingly common in modern journalistic and academic writing, particularly under the influence of contact with European languages:

- **SVO:** الطالبُ كتبَ الرسالةَ (al-talibu kataba al-risalata - "The student wrote the letter")

In nominal (verbless) sentences, the order is typically **Subject-Predicate**:

- الكتابُ جديدٌ (al-kitabu jadidun - "The book is new")

Topicalization and focus constructions allow other word orders. Prepositional phrases, adverbs, and temporal expressions frequently appear sentence-initially.

## Morphological Type

Arabic is a **fusional language** with a highly productive and distinctive **root-and-pattern (Semitic) morphological system**. The core of this system is the **trilateral root** (also called triliteral root), typically consisting of three consonants that carry the basic semantic meaning. Derivational and inflectional morphology is achieved by fitting these root consonants into various vowel patterns (called "forms" or "measures" / awzan).

For example, the root **ك-ت-ب** (k-t-b), carrying the semantic notion of "writing," yields:

- كَتَبَ (kataba) - "he wrote" (Form I verb, perfect)
- كِتَاب (kitab) - "book" (noun)
- كَاتِب (katib) - "writer" (active participle)
- مَكْتَب (maktab) - "office/desk" (noun of place)
- مَكْتُوب (maktub) - "written/letter" (passive participle)
- كُتُب (kutub) - "books" (broken plural)
- مَكْتَبَة (maktaba) - "library" (noun)
- كِتَابَة (kitaba) - "writing" (verbal noun/masdar)
- اِكْتَتَبَ (iktataba) - "he subscribed" (Form VIII verb)

This root-and-pattern system is one of the most defining features of Arabic morphology and is central to the Semitic language family.

## Phonology

Arabic has a rich consonantal inventory that includes several distinctive sounds:

**Pharyngeal consonants:**
- ح (ha) - voiceless pharyngeal fricative /h/
- ع ('ayn) - voiced pharyngeal fricative /Q/

**Emphatic (pharyngealized) consonants:**
- ص (sad) - emphatic /s/ (pharyngealized voiceless alveolar fricative)
- ض (dad) - emphatic /d/ (pharyngealized voiced alveolar stop)
- ط (ta) - emphatic /t/ (pharyngealized voiceless alveolar stop)
- ظ (za) - emphatic /z/ or /dh/ (pharyngealized voiced dental fricative)

**Uvular consonants:**
- ق (qaf) - voiceless uvular stop /q/
- خ (kha) - voiceless uvular fricative /x/
- غ (ghayn) - voiced uvular fricative /G/

**Glottal consonants:**
- ء (hamza) - glottal stop
- ه (ha) - voiceless glottal fricative /h/

The vowel system is relatively simple, consisting of three short vowels (a, i, u) and their long counterparts (aa, ii, uu). Vowel length is phonemic (e.g., كَتَبَ kataba "he wrote" vs. كَاتَب kaataba "he corresponded").

## Noun System

### Case System

Formal MSA preserves a **three-case system** from Classical Arabic:

- **Nominative (marfu'):** marked by -u (ـُ) or -un (ـٌ) for indefinite. Used for subjects and predicates.
- **Accusative (mansub):** marked by -a (ـَ) or -an (ـً) for indefinite. Used for direct objects, adverbs, and after certain particles.
- **Genitive (majrur):** marked by -i (ـِ) or -in (ـٍ) for indefinite. Used after prepositions and in idafa constructions.

In practice, case endings are fully pronounced only in formal speech (news broadcasts, Quran recitation, formal lectures). In ordinary educated speech and informal writing, case endings are often dropped (a phenomenon called "pausal form").

### Gender

Arabic has **two grammatical genders**: masculine and feminine. Feminine nouns are typically marked by the ta marbuta (ة):

- مُدَرِّس (mudarris) - "teacher (m.)"
- مُدَرِّسَة (mudarrisa) - "teacher (f.)"

Some nouns have inherent gender without overt marking, particularly body parts that come in pairs and certain natural phenomena.

### Number

Arabic distinguishes **three numbers**: singular, dual, and plural.

- **Singular:** كِتَاب (kitab) - "book"
- **Dual:** كِتَابَان (kitaban) - "two books" (nominative) / كِتَابَيْن (kitabayn) - (accusative/genitive)
- **Plural:** كُتُب (kutub) - "books"

Plurals are formed in two ways:
- **Sound (regular) plurals:** suffix-based (-un/-in for masculine, -at for feminine)
- **Broken (irregular) plurals:** internal vowel change patterns, extremely common and diverse (e.g., كِتَاب → كُتُب, مَدْرَسَة → مَدَارِس)

### Definiteness

Definiteness is marked by the **definite article ال (al-)**, which is prefixed to the noun:

- كِتَاب (kitab) - "a book" (indefinite, marked by tanwin/nunation)
- الكِتَاب (al-kitab) - "the book" (definite)

The lam of the article assimilates to "sun letters" (الحروف الشمسية): ت ث د ذ ر ز س ش ص ض ط ظ ل ن. It does not assimilate to "moon letters" (الحروف القمرية): ا ب ج ح خ ع غ ف ق ك م ه و ي.

## Verb System

### Verb Forms (Awzan)

Arabic verbs are organized into **ten main forms (I-X)**, plus several rare forms (XI-XV). Each form modifies the base meaning of the trilateral root in predictable ways:

- **Form I** (فَعَلَ fa'ala): basic meaning - كَتَبَ "he wrote"
- **Form II** (فَعَّلَ fa''ala): causative/intensive - دَرَّسَ "he taught"
- **Form III** (فَاعَلَ fa'ala): reciprocal/associative - كَاتَبَ "he corresponded with"
- **Form IV** (أَفْعَلَ af'ala): causative - أَرْسَلَ "he sent"
- **Form V** (تَفَعَّلَ tafa''ala): reflexive of II - تَعَلَّمَ "he learned"
- **Form VI** (تَفَاعَلَ tafa'ala): reciprocal of III - تَبَادَلَ "they exchanged"
- **Form VII** (اِنْفَعَلَ infa'ala): passive/reflexive - اِنْكَسَرَ "it broke"
- **Form VIII** (اِفْتَعَلَ ifta'ala): reflexive/middle - اِجْتَمَعَ "he gathered/met"
- **Form IX** (اِفْعَلَّ if'alla): colors/physical traits - اِحْمَرَّ "it became red"
- **Form X** (اِسْتَفْعَلَ istaf'ala): requestive/estimative - اِسْتَخْدَمَ "he used/employed"

### Tense/Aspect

Arabic verbs have two primary conjugations:

- **Perfect (al-madi):** completed action - كَتَبَ (kataba) "he wrote"
- **Imperfect (al-mudari'):** incomplete/ongoing action - يَكْتُبُ (yaktubu) "he writes/is writing"

Additional tenses and moods are formed with particles:
- **Future:** سَـ (sa-) or سَوْفَ (sawfa) + imperfect: سَيَكْتُبُ "he will write"
- **Subjunctive:** أَنْ + imperfect: أَنْ يَكْتُبَ "that he write"
- **Jussive:** لَمْ + imperfect: لَمْ يَكْتُبْ "he did not write"
- **Imperative:** اُكْتُبْ (uktub) "write!"

### Voice

Arabic has **active and passive voices**. The passive is formed through internal vowel changes:
- Active: كَتَبَ (kataba) - "he wrote"
- Passive: كُتِبَ (kutiba) - "it was written"

### Agreement

Arabic verbs show **rich agreement** with their subjects in person (1st, 2nd, 3rd), number (singular, dual, plural), and gender (masculine, feminine). The imperfect verb يَكْتُبُ has 13 distinct inflected forms to cover all combinations.

In VSO order, the verb typically appears in singular form regardless of the subject's number (partial agreement). In SVO order, the verb fully agrees with the subject.

## Syntax

### Idafa (Construct State)

The **idafa** (إضافة, "annexation") is a fundamental Arabic construction for expressing possession, association, and compound nouns. The first noun (mudaf) loses its tanwin and definite article, and the second noun (mudaf ilayhi) takes the genitive case:

- كِتَابُ الطَّالِبِ (kitabu al-talibi) - "the student's book"
- بَابُ البَيْتِ (babu al-bayti) - "the door of the house"

### Relative Clauses

Relative clauses are introduced by relative pronouns that agree in gender and number: الَّذِي (al-ladhi, m.sg.), الَّتِي (al-lati, f.sg.), الَّذِينَ (al-ladhina, m.pl.), اللَّوَاتِي (al-lawati, f.pl.), اللَّذَانِ (al-ladhani, m.du.), اللَّتَانِ (al-latani, f.du.). When the antecedent is indefinite, no relative pronoun is used:

- الرَّجُلُ الَّذِي جَاءَ (al-rajulu al-ladhi ja'a) - "the man who came"
- رَجُلٌ جَاءَ (rajulun ja'a) - "a man who came" (no relative pronoun)

### Negation

Different negation particles are used depending on the tense and sentence type:
- لا (la): general negation, present tense negation
- لم (lam): past negation (with jussive)
- لن (lan): future negation (with subjunctive)
- ما (ma): past negation (with perfect verb), also negation in nominal sentences
- ليس (laysa): negation of nominal sentences ("is not")

### Coordination and Subordination

Common conjunctions include وَ (wa, "and"), فَـ (fa, "and then/so"), ثُمَّ (thumma, "then"), أَوْ (aw, "or"), لَكِنَّ (lakinna, "but"), and إِنَّ (inna, "indeed/that"). The particle أَنَّ (anna, "that") introduces noun clauses, while إِذَا (idha, "if/when") and لَوْ (law, "if" - counterfactual) introduce conditional clauses.
