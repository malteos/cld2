# Modern Standard Arabic (arb_Arab)

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Modern Standard Arabic (MSA) / al-fusha al-mu'asira (الفصحى المعاصرة)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** arb
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** ar (macrolanguage)
- **Script:** Arabic (Arab), right-to-left abjad
- **Language Family:** Afro-Asiatic > Semitic > Central Semitic > Arabic
- **Speakers:** ~274 million native speakers; ~420 million total (including L2 speakers)
- **Status:** High-resource language

## Geographic Distribution

Modern Standard Arabic is the official or co-official language in over 25 countries spanning the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region and beyond. It serves as the official language of Algeria, Bahrain, Chad, Comoros, Djibouti, Egypt, Eritrea, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Mauritania, Morocco, Oman, Palestine, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, Somalia, Sudan, Syria, Tunisia, United Arab Emirates, and Yemen. Arabic is also widely used in diaspora communities across Europe, the Americas, and Southeast Asia.

As the liturgical language of Islam, Arabic holds religious significance for approximately 1.8 billion Muslims worldwide, many of whom learn to read the Quran in Arabic regardless of their native language. This religious dimension extends the reach and influence of Arabic far beyond its native-speaking population.

## Official Status

Modern Standard Arabic is one of the six official languages of the United Nations, alongside Chinese, English, French, Russian, and Spanish. It was designated a UN official language in 1973. Arabic is also an official language of the Arab League, the African Union, and the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation.

Within Arab countries, MSA serves as the language of government, education, media, and formal written communication. It exists in a diglossic relationship with regional spoken dialects (such as Egyptian Arabic, Levantine Arabic, Gulf Arabic, and Maghrebi Arabic), which are used in everyday conversation.

## Historical Development

The history of the Arabic language spans over 1,500 years of documented use. Its development can be broadly divided into several periods:

**Pre-Islamic Arabic (before 610 CE):** Arabic existed as a collection of tribal dialects on the Arabian Peninsula. Pre-Islamic poetry (al-mu'allaqat) represents the earliest literary corpus and established poetic conventions that would influence the language for centuries.

**Classical Arabic (7th-12th centuries):** The revelation of the Quran in the 7th century CE established Classical Arabic as a literary and religious standard. The rapid expansion of the Islamic empire spread Arabic across North Africa, the Levant, Mesopotamia, and parts of Central Asia. Grammarians such as Sibawayh (d. 796 CE) codified Arabic grammar, and the language became the vehicle for major contributions in philosophy, science, mathematics, and medicine during the Islamic Golden Age.

**Middle Arabic (12th-18th centuries):** During this period, spoken dialects increasingly diverged from the Classical standard. Arabic continued as the literary and scholarly language across the Islamic world, though Persian and Turkish also gained prominence in certain administrative and literary domains.

**Modern Standard Arabic (19th century-present):** The Nahda (Arab Renaissance) of the 19th century revitalized Arabic as a modern literary and intellectual language. Scholars and writers in Egypt, Lebanon, and Syria worked to adapt Classical Arabic for contemporary use, introducing new vocabulary for modern concepts while preserving the grammatical framework. The establishment of language academies in Damascus (1919), Cairo (1932), Baghdad (1947), and other cities helped standardize modern terminology.

Today, MSA represents a standardized, modernized form of Classical Arabic. While it preserves the core grammatical system and much of the vocabulary of Classical Arabic, it incorporates modern terminology and somewhat simplified stylistic conventions. MSA is the language of newspapers, television news broadcasts, academic publications, official documents, and formal speeches throughout the Arab world.

## Writing System

Arabic is written in the Arabic script, a cursive abjad (consonantal alphabet) that is written from right to left. The script consists of 28 letters, most of which have four contextual forms (initial, medial, final, and isolated). Short vowels are typically not written but can be indicated through optional diacritical marks (tashkeel/harakat). The Arabic script has been adapted for use by many other languages, including Persian, Urdu, Pashto, Kurdish (Sorani), and historically Malay (Jawi), Turkish (Ottoman), and others.
