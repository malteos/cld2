# Norwegian Nynorsk (nno_Latn) - Language Overview

## Basic Information

- **Language Name:** Norwegian Nynorsk (Norsk nynorsk)
- **ISO 639-3 Code:** nno
- **ISO 639-1 Code:** nn
- **Script:** Latin (Latn)
- **Language Family:** Indo-European > Germanic > North Germanic > West Scandinavian
- **Typological Classification:** Fusional, V2 word order
- **Number of Primary Users:** Approximately 600,000 (as a primary written standard)
- **Total Population Exposed:** ~5.4 million (all Norwegians encounter Nynorsk)

## Geographic Distribution

Norwegian Nynorsk is one of the two official written standards of Norway (alongside Bokmal). It is the primary written standard in western and rural regions of the country, particularly in:

- **Vestland** (Bergen and surrounding areas, though Bergen itself predominantly uses Bokmal)
- **Rogaland** (parts)
- **More og Romsdal**
- **Sogn og Fjordane** (now part of Vestland)
- **Parts of Telemark, Agder, and Innlandet**

Approximately 25-27% of Norwegian municipalities have declared Nynorsk as their official written standard. It is predominantly used in rural and western Norway, while Bokmal dominates urban centers and eastern/northern Norway.

## Official Status

Nynorsk holds equal legal status with Bokmal as one of Norway's two official written standards under the Norwegian Language Act (Spraklov). Government bodies are required to respond in the language standard used by the citizen, and all official documents must be available in both forms. The Norwegian Broadcasting Corporation (NRK) is required to use at least 25% Nynorsk in its programming. Students in Norwegian schools must learn both written standards, though they choose one as their primary form (hovudmal).

## Historical Background

Norwegian Nynorsk was created in the mid-19th century by the linguist and poet **Ivar Aasen** (1813-1896). After Norway gained independence from Denmark in 1814, the country continued to use Danish as its written language. Aasen undertook extensive fieldwork across rural Norway between 1842 and 1846, documenting Norwegian dialects that had survived centuries of Danish administrative rule. From these dialect studies, he constructed a new written standard that he called "Landsmal" (language of the land), which was intended to represent a genuinely Norwegian written form rooted in the speech of ordinary Norwegians rather than in Danish literary traditions.

Aasen published his grammar "Det norske Folkesprogs Grammatik" in 1848 and his dictionary "Ordbog over det norske Folkesprog" in 1850. These works laid the foundation for the new written standard. In 1885, the Norwegian parliament (Stortinget) granted Landsmal equal status with the Danish-influenced Riksmal (later called Bokmal).

Throughout the 20th century, the Norwegian government pursued a policy of "samnorsk" (Common Norwegian), attempting to merge the two standards. This policy was largely abandoned by the early 2000s, and both standards are now maintained as distinct entities. The name "Nynorsk" (New Norwegian) was officially adopted in 1929 to replace "Landsmal."

## Current Usage and Vitality

Nynorsk remains a living written standard with robust institutional support. It is used in literature, journalism, education, government administration, and broadcasting. Notable Nynorsk authors include Olav Duun, Tarjei Vesaas, Jon Fosse (Nobel Prize in Literature 2023), and Edvard Hoem. The language has a dedicated language council (Sprakradet) and normative dictionary (Nynorskordboka).

Despite its official status, Nynorsk faces pressure from Bokmal, which is used by roughly 85-87% of the population as their primary written standard. Urbanization and media dominance of Bokmal contribute to a gradual decline in the number of Nynorsk users, though the language maintains strong cultural significance and active advocacy communities.
