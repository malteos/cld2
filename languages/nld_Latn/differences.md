# Dutch vs confusable languages

Dutch is typologically between German and English, and its closest daughter language Afrikaans is its principal confuser in the CommonLID set. Frisian is a neighbouring West Germanic sister language often mixed into Dutch text in the Netherlands.

## nld ↔ afr (closest pair)

- Afrikaans has double negation *nie … nie* at clause end; standard Dutch never uses this.
- Afrikaans is strongly analytic: present, past and future share one uninflected verb form, with past marked by *het* + past participle (*het gewerk*). Dutch retains full person/number and weak-strong past inflection.
- Plurals: Dutch *honden, huizen, boeken* vs Afrikaans *honde, huise, boeke* (no final `-n`).
- Dutch *zijn* (3pl/inf 'to be') vs Afrikaans invariable *is/was/wees*; Dutch *niet* vs Afrikaans *nie*; Dutch *gij/jullie* vs Afrikaans *julle*.
- Afrikaans spells many Dutch long vowels the same (*huis, straat*) but drops silent consonants and uses *y* where Dutch uses *ij* (*my* vs *mijn*).

## nld ↔ fry (Western Frisian)

- Frisian uses `â`, `ê`, `û`, `ô` (circumflex) — Dutch does not.
- Dutch `ij` ≈ Frisian `ei` or `â` (*tijd* vs *tiid*; *wij* vs *wy*).
- Frisian 2pl pronoun *jimme*, 3sg.m *hy*, definite article *it* (cf. Dutch *het*). Frisian negation *net*, Dutch *niet*.
- Frisian diphthongs `ea`, `ie`, `oa` (*read, brief, boat*) are not native Dutch spellings.

## nld ↔ deu (German)

- No `ß`, no noun capitalisation, no case inflection on articles in Dutch.
- Dutch *de/het/een* vs German *der/die/das/den/dem/des/ein*.
- `ij` is Dutch-only; `sch-` clusters exist in both but Dutch lacks German's `sp-`/`st-` /ʃ/ realisation.

## nld ↔ eng (English)

- Presence of `ij`, `aa/ee/oo/uu`, or any of *het, de, een, niet, zijn, hebben* is decisive for Dutch.
